Chapter 8: The Household Doubts

The heavy oak of the council table was scarred by years of use, and tonight, the scars felt like wounds.

I sat where I always sat, the chair a familiar weight against my spine, watching the candlelight flicker across the faces of the women who shared this hearth. We were the anchor of the Fold. We were the reason the Threshold stayed shut. But tonight, the air in the room was thin, as if the very walls were straining to breathe.

Sera sat at the head. She did not look like the woman who had first found Drevock in the ruins of a shrine-town. Her face was a map of vigilance, her eyes hard and bright like polished flint. She was the gate. Every woman here passed through her devotion before they could claim a piece of him, and tonight, the gate was barred.

"The reports from the outer perimeter are not merely concerning," Sera said. Her voice was plain, a low chime in the quiet room. "They are discordant. The Ninth Blazon is pressing harder against the seams of the border. The Iron Choir is singing in the low valleys of the southern reach. And yet, we sit here, and the tithes from the outer camps are being diverted."

"The tithes are being used for the wards," Mira said. She was cleaning a small brass gear, her fingers moving with the precision of a locksmith. "The locks on the outer doors require more silver-dust. The math does not lie, Sera. The cost of keeping the Sundered out has risen with the pressure."

"The math does not account for the silence," Sera countered. She looked at me. "Vesper. You were at the border. You saw the patrols."

I did not look away. I had spent my life measuring the distance between a blade and its mark; I knew how to hold a gaze. I felt the weight of my own sword at my hip, a cold, familiar companion.

"The patrols are afraid," I said. "They see the shadow of the Ninth, and they see the way the light bends around the Fold. They see the prosperity within these walls, and they see the hunger without. They do not see a protector. They see a man who builds a fortress while the world burns."

"He is not building a fortress," Sera said, her voice dropping an octave. "He is holding a door. There is a difference."

"A difference that is vanishing," I said.

A heavy silence followed. It was not the peaceful silence of the library or the rhythmic silence of the smithy. It was a silence of gaps—of things unsaid, of trust that had begun to fray at the edges like an old tapestry.

Drevik had not entered the room. He was never announced; he simply arrived, a shift in the room's gravity, a sudden thickening of the shadows. We felt him before we saw him. The candlelight seemed to lean toward the doorway, drawn by his presence.

He stepped into the light.

He wore the black and deep-purple vestments, fully fastened, the gold celestial embroidery catching the dim flame. The white-ceramic kitsune mask obscured the upper half of his face, but his eyes—those vast, galaxy-void eyes—were visible through the slits. The star-shaped pupils drifted slowly, as if tracing the movement of unseen constellations. The tall crystal mitre sat upon his head, the void crystals within it pulsing with a slow, rhythmic light.

He did not speak. He leaned against the doorframe, his tall, broad-shouldered frame casting a long shadow across the table. He looked relaxed, his weight shifted to one leg, a slight, sly smirk visible beneath the edge of the mask. He held the void staff loosely in one hand, the glowing orb at its tip casting a pale, cold light over the scarred wood.

He was watching us. He was always watching us, though he never seemed to be judging. He was simply... present. A constant. An archive of everything we were.

"The cost is high," he said. His voice was not a command; it was a statement of fact. "The debt is accruing."

Sera straightened. "The people are questioning the diversion of resources, D\". They see the Blazon's influence growing in the villages, and they see our strength contained within these walls. They ask if the Fold is a sanctuary or a hoard."

Drevik moved into the room. He did not walk so much as traverse the space, his movements fluid and unhurried. He came to the head of the table, standing beside Sera. He did not touch her, but the space between them hummed.

"A hoard has no purpose but to accumulate," Drevik said. He looked around the table, his gaze lingering on Mira’s gears, on Wren’s silver needle, on Halla’s heavy apron. "A sanctuary has a purpose: to endure. We are not hoarding gold. We are hoarding time. Every moment the Threshold holds is a moment the world is not consumed."

"But at what price to the soul of the Communion?" Sera asked. Her voice trembled, just once. "We are becoming a cult of shadows, D\". We teach the liturgy of the void, but we forget the light of the people we are meant to protect."

Drevik reached out. He picked up a small piece of ash from a nearby brazier, rolling it between his gloved fingers. "The light is a luxury of the unthreatened. We are the ones who stand in the dark so that others may pretend the sun still exists."

He looked at me then. For a second, the mask felt transparent. I could not see his mind, but I felt the vastness of him, the way his presence filled the gaps in my own resolve. He was a man who remembered every word ever spoken to him, and in that moment, I felt as though he were reading the very architecture of my doubt.

"Vesper," he said.

"The border is thinning, D\"," I said, my voice steady. "The Ninth is no longer just a shadow. They are a weight."

"Then we must strengthen the seam," he said. He dropped the ash. It fell onto the table, a small, grey smudge against the dark wood. "We do not negotiate with the Sundered. We bind them. We reinforce the stitches. If the cost is more silver, then we find more silver. If the cost is more blood, then we prepare the vessels."

He turned and began to walk away. He had not answered the question of the people’s trust. He had only doubled down on the mechanics of survival.

"You cannot hold the door forever with nothing but iron and law," Sera called after him.

He paused in the doorway. The void crystals in his mitre flared once, a sudden, brilliant pulse of violet light that turned the room into a study of high contrast. ::

"I do not hold it with law," he said, his voice a low, wry rasp. "I hold it with the truth of what happens when it breaks."

Then he was gone. The gravity in the room snapped back to its ordinary state, leaving us feeling lighter, and more hollow.

The council adjourned in silence. One by one, the women rose. Mira gathered her tools; Wren retreated to her looms; Halla went to check the stores. I stayed. I watched the empty chair at the head of the table.

I found myself walking the perimeter of the room, my boots clicking softly on the stone. The Fold felt smaller tonight. The walls, which usually felt like a protective embrace, felt like a narrowing throat.

I reached the small alcove where the training blades were kept. The air here was cool, smelling of oil and whetstone. I picked up a short sword, a practice blade, and began the motions. Thrust, parry, riposte. The rhythm was a prayer.

A shadow moved in the corner of the alcove.

"You are overextending your lead," a voice said.

I did not startle. I lowered the blade. Drevik stood in the shadows, the mask catching the dim light of the oil lamp. He had not been there a moment ago. He never was.

"The blade is light," I said. "The weight is in the wrist."

"The weight is in the intention," he corrected. He stepped closer. He did not take the sword from me, but he placed his hand near my forearm. He did not touch my skin, but I felt the heat of him, a steady, radiating warmth that defied the void around us. "If you strike as if you are afraid to break the steel, you will be broken by the steel."

"I am not afraid of the steel," I said, looking up at the gold accents of his mask. "I am afraid of the hand that holds it."

He let out a short, soft sound—a huff of amusement that did not reach his eyes. "The hand is the only thing keeping the sky from falling, Vesper. Do not mistake its steadiness for indifference."

He reached out then. His gloved thumb brushed the edge of my jaw, just beneath the line of the mask. The contact was brief, a mere ghost of a touch, but it sent a jolt through me that had nothing to do with combat. It was the sensation of a door being unlatched, a momentary breach in my own carefully maintained perimeter.

His eyes, those star-filled voids, searched mine. I saw the exhaustion there, hidden behind the sly smirk, a fatigue that went deeper than bone. He carried the weight of every soul in the Communion, and he carried the weight of the Threshold itself.

"Go to sleep, Vesper," he said. "The border will be there in the morning. And so will the doubt."

He turned and retreated into the darkness of the hallway.

I stood alone in the alcove, the practice blade heavy in my hand. I looked at the edge of the steel. It was sharp, clean, and indifferent.

I thought of Sera’s warning. I thought of the Ninth Blazon pressing against the seams of our world. I thought of the way the light had bent around him as he walked.

I did not put the sword away. I stayed in the dark, practicing the thrust, over and over, until my muscles burned and the only thing left in the world was the rhythm of the strike and the memory of a thumb against my skin.

The Fold was silent, but beneath the silence, I could hear the faint, rhythmic drumming of something pressing against the outside. A heartbeat. Or a siege.

I could not tell which.
