The blade was not a thing of ornament. It was a thing of weight and edge, and it did not belong in the soft, honeyed light of the Fold.

I found it tucked beneath the hem of my practice tunic, near the edge of the sleeping pallet, where the fabric had bunched against the floorboards. At first, I thought it was merely a stray thread or a scrap of discarded linen, something caught in the weave of my clothes during the morning’s drills. But when my fingers brushed the cold, unforgiving resistance of metal, the blood in my veins seemed to turn to slush.

It was a stiletto, slender and dark, its surface etched with the precise, unyielding, interlocking sigils of the Ninth Blazon. The craftsmanship was terrifyingly beautiful, a geometric nightmare of silvered lines that caught the dim light and refused to let it go. It was a piece of the Iron Choir. It was a weapon of the very thing we were sworn to hold back, a physical manifestation of the encroaching void.

I did not move. I stayed exactly as I was, kneeling on the rug, the damp cold of the floorboards seeping through my thin clothes and into my bones. The air in my quarters was still, heavy with the scent of dried lavender and the stale dust of the afternoon, but the room had changed. The symmetry of my sanctuary—the careful arrangement of my books, the precise fold of my blankets, the orderly peace of my solitude—was broken. The very geometry of my life had been skewed.

My heart hammered against my ribs, a frantic bird in a cage of bone, but I forced my breath to remain shallow. To gasp was to admit the wound. I reached out, my fingers trembling only slightly, and pulled the blade fully from its hiding place.

The metal was heavy, possessing a cold gravity that pulled at my hand, dragging my arm toward the floor. I knew this steel. I had seen it in the hands of the envoys who came to the Threshold, the ones who arrived in armored carriages of black glass, speaking of law and blazon and the necessity of border adjustments, but never of invasion. To hold it here, inside the sanctity of the Fold, was a seam ripped open. It was a breach of the highest order. It was a declaration of war written in silver.

I wiped the blade on a scrap of linen, my movements precise, the way I cleaned my own daggers after a long session in the training hall. I watched the way the light died against the etched sigils. I did not tremble. To tremble was to invite the fear to take root. I placed the stiletto in a small wooden box, the kind used for medicinal salves, and closed the lid with a soft, final click.

I had to tell him.

The halls of the Fold were long and silent in the late afternoon. The light was always amber, a permanent, suspended autumn that never faded into true night, a trick of the Fold’s architecture to keep the residents from losing their sense of time. I walked past the library, where the void-glass windows held the trapped, bruised dusk of the outside world, and toward the central hearth. My footsteps, usually silent on the thick rugs, felt deafening, like hammer blows against the silence.

Drevik was there. He was standing by the heavy oak table, his back to me. He was reviewing a ledger—not a book of words, and certainly not a book of stories, but a map of the recent shifts in the Threshold, a tally of the magical pressures pressing against our boundaries. His vestments were dark, the deep, regal purple absorbing the light around him, and the gold embroidery caught the flicker of the hearth-fire like dying stars. The tall crystal mitre on his head held the void-crystals in a slow, rhythmic, hypnotic dance, casting dancing shadows across the spines of the books behind him.

He did not turn when I entered. He did not have to. The room always shifted when he was in it, the very atmosphere tightening, the tension of the world settling into a focused, sharp point centered on his presence.

"The counts are off, Vesper," he said. His voice was low, a steady, resonant note that vibrated in the quiet air. "The pressure from the Ninth is not matching the tithe of the wards. There is a leak in the architecture. A discrepancy in the flow."

"It is not a leak in the architecture," I said. I stood by the door, my hands clasped tightly before me, my knuckles white. "It is a leak in the house."

He paused. The movement was small, a mere halt in the frantic scratching of his quill against the parchment. He turned then, and the movement was slow, deliberate, like the turning of a heavy gear.

He did an excellent job of hiding his shock. He did not look like a man who had been interrupted by a catastrophe. He looked like a man who had been waiting for the truth to arrive, as if the catastrophe had been an appointment he had noted in his calendar weeks ago. The white-ceramic kitsune mask caught the firelight, the gold accents bright and unblinking, giving him the appearance of a porcelain god. His galaxy-void eyes, with those strange, star-shaped pupils that seemed to contain entire nebulas, settled on mine. He was vast, a presence that occupied the very space between the walls, yet he was as calm as a settled stone.

"Explain," he said.

"I found something in my quarters," I said. I walked toward the table, my legs feeling heavy, as if I were wading through deep water. "A piece of the Blazon. A stinking, silver stiletto. Etched with their law."

The silence that followed was not empty. It was heavy, a physical weight that pressed against my chest, like the air before a summer storm breaks. I watched him through the flickering light. He did me no favors with his expression; the mask hid the mouth, the jaw, the breath, but the tilt of his head was clinical, detached. He was weighing the weight of my words against the weight of the blade.

"The Ninth does not leave gifts, Vesper," he said. His voice was devoid of emotion, a flat line. "They leave wreckage. They leave scorched earth and broken treaties."

"This was not wreckage. This was a placement. It was tucked beneath the hem of my tunic, near the edge of my pallet. It was waiting to be found. It was meant to be seen."

I saw the slight, almost imperceptible shift in the room. It was the way the shadows near the corners of the hearth seemed to sharpen, growing longer and more predatory. Drevik leaned back, the void staff leaning against the edge of the table, its tip glowing with a faint, dying luminescence. He did not reach for it. He did me no comfort. He simply existed in the center of the breach I had brought into the room.

"Who else has access to your quarters?" he asked. The question was a blade in itself.

"Only the others. Only the Hearth."

The word hit the floor like a dropped coin, ringing in the silence. The Hearth. The women who held the anchors. The women who were the very reason the Fold remained anchored to this reality, the very reason we hadn's been swallowed by the void long ago. To even suggest one of them could be complicit felt like a sacrilege.

"Sera was here this morning," I said. The words were plain, stripped of all emotion. I did not dress them in suspicion; I only laid them out like a list of accounts, a ledger of presence. "She brought the incense for the evening liturgy. She moved the linens to clean the dust."

Drevik did not answer immediately. He looked down at the ledger, his fingers tapping a slow, rhythmic, hypnotic beat against the dark wood of the table. Tap. Tap. Tap. He was calculating the cost of the breach. He was running the numbers of a betrayal, tracing the lines of potential treason through the very fabric of our fellowship.

"And Wren?" he asked.

"She was mace-mending the tapestries near the window this afternoon. She passed my door twice on her way to the gardens."

"And Mira?"

"She was checking the locks on the outer boundary. She was in the hall, near my entrance, less than an hour ago."

He looked up again. The star-pupils in his eyes seemed to pulse, a faint, rhythmic light that matched the flickering of the hearth. "You are presenting a ledger of possibilities, Vesper. A list of shadows. I need a receipt. I need a name. I need a thief."

"The name is in the steel," I said, my voice rising despite my effort. I stepped closer, into the warmth of the hearth, feeling the heat of the fire against my face. "The steel belongs to them. The placement belongs to us. If the blade is in my room, the shadow is in our house. You cannot tell me this is a coincidence of geography."

He stood up. He was tall, a silhouette of black and deep purple that seemed to command the very gravity of the hall, pulling the light toward him. He walked around the table. He did not come to me to comfort me, nor did he come to confront me. He came to inspect the damage, as a surveyor might inspect a cracked foundation.

He stopped a foot away. I could smell the scent of him—ash, cold stone, and that sharp, ozone tang that preceded a lightning strike. He was a man who held the world together, yet here, in the center of his own sanctuary, the seams were fraying.

"If I search the quarters," he said, his voice dropping to a low, dangerous register that made the hair on my arms stand up, "and I find nothing but cloth and dust, what then? If I find only the emptiness of your accusations, Vesper, what becomes of your standing in the Hearth?"

"Then the blade was planted to turn us against one and another," I said, meeting his gaze with everything I had. "Then the thief is a ghost, a phantom playing with our trust, waiting for the next piece of the Armory to fall into our laps. Either way, the House is compromised."

He looked at me for a long time. The mask was a wall, a porcelain barrier I could not breach. I could not see his mouth, could not see the tension in his lips, but I could see the way his gaze searched mine, looking for the tremor, the lie, the hidden debt, the small, shameful secret that might explain why such a weapon would be in my bed. I gave him nothing but the hard, cold truth of my eyes.

"The Ninth is watching the Threshold," he said, more to himself than to me, his voice trailing off into the shadows. "They are looking for a crack. A way to turn the anchor into a wedge. They want us to tear ourselves apart from the inside."

"They have found it," I said.

He reached out. For a heartbeat, I thought he might touch my cheek, a small, human gesture of the kindness that anchored him to us, the only thing that made his divinity bearable. Instead, his hand hovered near my shoulder, a ghost of a movement, as if he were measuring the distance between us and the disaster.

"Go to your rooms," he said, his voice brittle. "Lock the door. Dout leave until the bells for the third watch. Do not speak to the others."

"And the others?" I asked, my voice small. "What do I tell Sera? What do I tell Mira? They will notice I have vanished. They will notice the silence."

He turned away, walking back toward the ledger, his shadow stretching long and thin across the floorboards, reaching toward the dark corners of the room.

"Tell them nothing," he said, his back once more a wall of purple and gold. "Tell them the Threshold is steady. Tell them the House is whole."

I watched him work. He picked up the quill. He began to write, the scratch of the nib on the parchment the only sound in the room, a rhythmic, lonely sound. He was retreating into the archive of his mind, locking the door to his own uncertainty, burying the truth beneath layers of ink and protocol.

I left the hall. As I walked through the dim corridors, the amber light felt sickly, like old, bruised fruit. The very air felt heavy, thick with the unspoken. I passed the training hall, where the sound of muffled, rhythmic impacts echoed—the heartbeat of the house—and the kitchen, where the hearth was still warm with the day's bread, a scent of normalcy that felt like an insult.

The Fold was the same as it had been an hour ago. The walls were thick, the magic was heavy, the air was safe. But as I passed the closed door of Sera’s chambers, I felt the weight of the stiletto in my mind, a cold, sharp presence.

The seam was broken. The thief was in the house. And as I reached my room and turned the heavy iron key in the lock, I realized the most terrifying truth of all: the dark behind the door wasn't just encroaching. It was already inside, and it was waiting for me to sleep.
