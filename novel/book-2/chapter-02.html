Chapter 2: Rival Chapter

The shadow hit him before the warning did.

Drevik spun in the training hall, barely catching the flicker of movement in his peripheral vision. His hand shot up, shadows wrapping around his forearm like living gauntlets, and he deflected the strike that would have cracked his ribs.

"Good reflexes." Lexi stepped back, her auburn hair slicked with sweat, green eyes sharp with appraisal. "But you're still telegraphing your blocks. Anyone with half a brain would have feinted left and taken your legs out from under you."

"Maybe I wanted you to think that." Drevik rolled his shoulders, feeling the satisfying pull of muscles worked hard. Three weeks of accelerated training had carved new definition into his frame. "Maybe I was setting you up."

Lexi's mouth curved—not quite a smile, but close enough to make his chest tight. "You're getting cocky. Dangerous trait in an acolyte."

"Learned from the best."

She threw a practice dagger at his head. He caught it.

They both froze. Drevik stared at the blade in his hand, then at Lexi, whose expression had shifted from teasing to something more complicated. The shadows around his arm dissipated like smoke.

"That was new," she said quietly.

"I didn't think about it." He set the dagger down on the nearest bench with careful deliberation. "It just happened."

"Shadow reflexes. Usually takes years to develop." She moved closer, her voice dropping even though they were alone in the hall. "Your abilities are manifesting faster than anyone expected. The Elders are going to have questions."

"Let them ask." Drevik met her gaze. "I have nothing to hide."

The words hung between them, weighted with things neither said. Lexi's eyes flickered to his mouth, then away, and she stepped back with a briskness that seemed forced.

"Get cleaned up. There's a gathering in the inner sanctum in an hour. Something about visitors from another chapter."

"Visitors?"

"Didn't catch the details. Just be there." She paused at the door. "And Drevik—whatever happens, stay sharp. I don't trust anyone who travels all this way without announcing their purpose first."

She left before he could respond. He watched the door swing shut, the echo of her footsteps fading into the stone corridors that honeycombed beneath the university campus. The Order's hidden heart, invisible to the students walking overhead, absorbed in their mundane concerns of grades and social lives.

Drevik flexed his hand, remembering the way the shadows had moved without conscious thought. His whole life, he'd felt like an outsider looking in—at the orphanage, at the scholarship program, at the world that seemed to operate by rules no one had bothered to explain. Now he'd stumbled into a place where the rules were written in shadow and moonlight, and he still wasn't sure he understood them.

But he was learning.

*

The inner sanctum occupied a natural cavern beneath the oldest building on campus. Drevik descended the worn steps, passing torches that burned without fuel and tapestries that depicted events from centuries past. The air grew cooler, touched with the mineral scent of stone and something else—a faint ozone tang that prickled along his skin.

Other acolytes filled the chamber, clustered in small groups along the walls. The stone floor had been carved with intricate patterns that Drevik had learned were more than decoration—they were containment circles, binding symbols, pathways for power. He found a position near a pillar and studied the room.

Elder Thornwood stood at the center, his presence commanding despite his slight frame. Gray streaked his dark hair, and lines mapped his face with the precision of ancient roads. Beside him, two other Elders Drevik recognized from local gatherings watched the entrance with matching expressions of guarded anticipation.

But it was the strangers who drew his attention.

Six figures stood opposite the local Order members, dressed in robes that differed subtly from the local style—darker, with silver threading along the hems. At their head stood a woman perhaps forty years old, her black hair shot through with silver, her face carrying the kind of beauty that came from bone structure and absolute confidence. Her eyes moved across the room with unsettling precision, cataloging faces, assessing threats.

And beside her—

Drevik's breath caught. The young woman stood apart from the other visitors, her posture relaxed but alert. She had hair the color of deep water, dark with hints of blue under the torchlight, and features that seemed carved from porcelain and painted with the subtlest hand. Her eyes found his across the chamber, held for one breath, two, and then moved on.

But not before something flickered in them—recognition, perhaps, or interest.

"Thank you for coming on such short notice," Elder Thornwood was saying. "The Northern Chapter's concerns have been noted."

"Concerns." The silver-haired woman's voice carried the weight of someone accustomed to being heard. "Is that what we're calling them? I would have chosen 'warnings,' myself. Or perhaps 'evidence of territorial violations.'"

Drevik tuned into the conversation with sharpened focus. Territory disputes between chapters weren't uncommon—resources, artifacts, and sites of power all required careful allocation. But something in the woman's tone suggested this wasn't a routine disagreement.

"The boundary between our domains has been stable for decades," Thornwood replied. "If you're suggesting—"

"I'm suggesting nothing. I'm stating facts." The woman stepped forward, and Drevik saw several local acolytes tense. "Three times in the past month, your people have crossed into our territory. Three times, they've interacted with sites that fall under our protection. This is not a matter for debate—it is a matter for correction."

"Name the sites."

"The Shadowwell. The Crimson Crossroads. The Dreamer's Threshold."

Each name landed in the room like a stone dropped into still water. Drevik saw confusion on most faces, but Elder Thornwood's expression tightened almost imperceptibly.

"The Shadowwell is disputed territory," he said carefully. "Has been for a century. As for the other two—"

"Disputed." The woman's smile didn't reach her eyes. "How convenient. Tell me, Thornwood, do your people dispute territory when it suits them, or is this a new interpretation of ancient agreements?"

"Is there a purpose to this confrontation, Magistra Blackwood?" A new voice entered the conversation—Elder Corvin, the oldest of the local leadership, her white hair framing a face that had witnessed more decades than anyone dared guess. "We came to discuss, not to trade accusations."

"Then let us discuss." Magistra Blackwood spread her hands in a gesture that might have seemed conciliatory from anyone else. "The Northern Chapter proposes a formal redefinition of boundaries. We believe the current arrangement has become... ambiguous. Clarity serves everyone's interests."

"Clarity." Corvin's voice was dry as parchment. "Is that what we're calling expansion?"

"Security. Stability. The same things your chapter claims to value." Blackwood's gaze swept the room again, and this time it landed on Drevik with unmistakable intent. "I see you've been acquiring new members. How... unusual. An acolyte of such promising ability, recruited from outside the traditional bloodlines. Whatever must the conservative factions think?"

Every eye in the room turned to Drevik. He felt the weight of attention like a physical thing, pressing against his chest, making his pulse quicken. The shadows in the corners of the chamber seemed to deepen, responding to his tension.

"Ability should be cultivated regardless of origin," Thornwood said, his voice carefully neutral. "You of all people understand that, Magistra. Or have the Northern traditions changed so dramatically?"

"Our traditions remain as they have always been—strong, unified, uncompromised." Something flickered across Blackwood's face, there and gone. "But we hear rumors. Whispers of abilities that manifest outside known parameters. Of acolytes who see things that haven't happened yet." Her eyes fixed on Drevik again. "Such talents would be valuable. Perhaps we should discuss a transfer of personnel. For the good of the Order as a whole."

The suggestion landed in the chamber like a blade on stone. Drevik heard the sharp intake of breath from nearby acolytes. Transfer requests between chapters weren't unknown, but they required consent—from the acolyte, from their current leadership, from the Council that governed all chapters. The way Blackwood framed it, more as a demand than a request, carried implications that made his skin prickle.

"Drevik Markinsoul remains with this chapter," Thornwood said, and his voice had gone cold. "That is not a matter for negotiation."

"Everything is negotiable, Elder. Given sufficient motivation." Blackwood's smile finally reached her eyes, which only made it more unsettling. "We will speak again tomorrow. My delegation requires rest after our journey. I trust you can provide appropriate accommodations?"

Thornwood's nod was barely perceptible. The Northern delegation turned to file out of the chamber, but the dark-haired young woman lingered, her gaze sweeping the room one final time. When it passed over Drevik, she tilted her head slightly—a gesture that might have been acknowledgment, might have been challenge.

Then she was gone, and the chamber erupted into murmured conversation.

Drevik stood frozen, processing what had just happened. The territory dispute, the pointed references to his recruitment, the barely-veiled threat about transfers—it coalesced into a picture he didn't fully understand but recognized as dangerous.

A hand closed around his wrist. He turned to find Lexi beside him, her expression tight with controlled fury.

"That woman," she said under her breath. "She's been pushing for expansion for years. Using territory disputes as an excuse to consolidate power. If she gets control of the sites she mentioned—"

"What sites? What are they?"

Lexi glanced around, then pulled him toward a quieter corner. "The Shadowwell is a natural wellspring of shadow energy. The Crossroads is where the veil between worlds grows thin. And the Dreamer's Threshold..." She hesitated. "It's a place where reality can be reshaped. Where powerful practitioners can alter the fabric of possibility itself."

"That sounds dangerous."

"It's supposed to be impossible to use. The last person who tried spent three centuries trapped in a loop of their own making, reliving the same day over and over until their mind simply... unraveled." Her grip on his wrist tightened. "But Blackwood has never let impossibility stop her before. If she's targeting those sites, she has a plan. And if she's asking about you, you're part of it."

"Me?" Drevik shook his head. "I'm nobody. A scholarship student who stumbled into this world by accident."

"You're an acolyte with abilities that haven't been seen in generations. You're an outsider with no blood ties to any chapter, making you a political unknown. And you're rising faster than anyone expected." Lexi's voice dropped further. "That makes you valuable. That makes you dangerous. And that makes you a target."

The weight of her words settled over him. He'd known, abstractly, that his rapid progression through the ranks drew attention. He'd known that his unusual abilities set him apart. But hearing it laid out so plainly, from someone who understood the politics of this world better than he ever would, made it real in a way it hadn't been before.

"What do I do?"

"What you've been doing. Train. Learn. Prove yourself." Lexi released his wrist, but stayed close. "And stay away from the Northern delegation. Especially Blackwood's daughter."

"Daughter?" Drevik's mind flashed to the dark-haired young woman, the one who had watched him with such unsettling attention.

"Mira Blackwood. Prodigy, ambitious, and absolutely ruthless. She's been competing in inter-chapter trials since she was fourteen, and she's never lost." Lexi's jaw tightened. "She'll try to get close to you. It's what she does—finds weaknesses, exploits them, discards what she's used. Don't let her."

"I wasn't planning on it."

"Good." Lexi stepped back, her composure returning like a mask sliding into place. "Get some rest. Tomorrow's going to be complicated."

She walked away before he could respond. Drevik watched her go, noting the tension in her shoulders, the controlled precision of her movements. He'd learned to read her over the past weeks, to see past the surface she presented to the world. Something about this situation had her on edge beyond political concerns.

He filed that observation away for later examination.

*

The night passed in fragments of uneasy sleep. Drevik's dreams were scattered images—dark corridors, silver threads, eyes watching from shadows. He woke before dawn, his body tuned to rhythms he was only beginning to understand, and found himself wandering the compound's lower levels without conscious destination.

The training hall was empty at this hour. He considered practicing, working off the restless energy that thrummed through him, but the shadows felt different here at night. More aware. More hungry.

Instead, he made his way to the archives—a sprawling collection of chambers where the Order's history was preserved in texts both mundane and magical. He'd been spending more time here lately, learning the context for the world he'd stumbled into.

A light burned in one of the reading rooms.

Drevik paused at the threshold. The chamber beyond was small, intimate, its walls lined with books and scrolls organized in systems he hadn't yet mastered. A single lamp cast warm light across the central table, illuminating the figure bent over an ancient text.

Mira Blackwood looked up as he entered. Her dark hair fell over one shoulder, and her expression shifted from concentration to surprise to something more calculating in the space of a heartbeat.

"Drevik Markinsoul." She spoke his name like she was tasting it, testing its weight. "I wondered when we'd meet properly."

"Your mother's delegation only arrived yesterday." He kept his voice neutral. "I wouldn't have expected to find you in the archives before dawn."

"I don't sleep well in new places. And I've always found old texts... comforting." She gestured to the chair across from her. "Please. Sit. I promise I don't bite. Not without invitation, anyway."

The last words carried a weight that made the air between them feel suddenly thicker. Drevik remained standing.

"What are you doing here?"

"Reading. What does it look like?" She leaned back, studying him with open appreciation. "You're taller than I expected. The reports made you sound... smaller."

"Reports?"

"Did you think your little chapter was the only one paying attention to you?" Mira's laugh was soft, almost musical. "Every chapter in the region knows about Drevik Markinsoul—the outsider acolyte who shadow-walked on his first attempt, who sees futures that haven't happened, who caught the eye of Elder Thornwood's daughter within days of his initiation."

Drevik's jaw tightened. "Lexi Thornwood has been my instructor. Nothing more."

"Has she?" Mira tilted her head, that gesture he'd seen in the chamber repeated. "Interesting. That's not what the reports suggest. But then, reports can be... interpreted. You learn that, when you grow up in a chapter where information is the most valuable currency."

"What do you want, Mira?"

The question hung in the air. Mira rose from her chair, moving around the table with a fluid grace that reminded him of Lexi in the training hall—but where Lexi's movements were sharp, efficient, Mira's were languid, almost lazy. A predator who knew her prey wasn't going anywhere.

"What do any of us want?" She stopped an arm's length away, close enough that he could smell her perfume—something floral with an undertone of smoke. "Power. Position. Purpose. The same things you want, I imagine."

"You don't know what I want."

"Don't I?" Her eyes held his, dark and deep. "You want to belong. You want to matter. You want to understand why you can do things that others can't, why the shadows answer you like old friends instead of reluctant servants." She stepped closer, and her voice dropped. "I can help you with those things. My chapter has resources yours doesn't. Knowledge that's been... restricted in certain circles."

"In exchange for what?"

"Such a practical question. I appreciate that." Her smile was a knife wrapped in silk. "Nothing you'd find objectionable. Perhaps some cooperation, when the situation calls for it. Perhaps some information about local chapter politics—nothing that would compromise your loyalties, just... perspective. Perhaps, eventually, a demonstration of those fascinating abilities of yours."

"You're asking me to spy for you."

"I'm asking you to consider your options." Her expression sobered. "My mother wants you transferred to our chapter. She sees you as a resource to be acquired. I see you as something more interesting—a potential ally. There's a difference, Drevik. One you'd do well to understand before you make choices that can't be unmade."

The shadows in the corners of the room had grown deeper, responding to his tension. Mira noticed—of course she did—and her smile returned.

"Interesting," she murmured. "They like you. The shadows. They respond to your emotions almost like they're alive."

"They're not alive."

"Are you certain?" She reached out, and her fingers brushed the edge of the darkness pooling near his shoulder. It didn't retreat from her touch. "Shadow magic is old magic. Old things have a way of developing... awareness. Perhaps that's part of your gift. Perhaps you hear them in ways others don't."

Drevik stepped back, breaking whatever connection had formed between them. "If you're trying to recruit me, you're going about it strangely."

"I'm not trying to recruit you. I'm trying to understand you." Mira's hand dropped to her side. "There's a difference. Recruitment is transactional. Understanding is... personal."

"Is that what you're offering? Personal understanding?"

"What I'm offering is perspective." She retrieved a small book from the table and tucked it into the folds of her robe. "Your chapter is old, Drevik. Established. Comfortable in its ways. That comfort can breed blindness. My mother isn't the only one who sees value in you—and not everyone who watches has your best interests at heart."

"You're speaking in riddles."

"I'm speaking carefully. There's a difference." She moved toward the door, pausing beside him. "Your Lexi is fierce. Loyal. Protective. Those are admirable qualities. But fierce loyalty can blind people to flaws in those they protect. Loyal protectors can miss threats that come from unexpected directions." Her voice dropped to barely a whisper. "Someone in your inner circle is feeding information to entities from the Shadowlands. Watch your back, Drevik. Not everyone who stands beside you stands with you."

She was gone before he could respond, leaving him alone in the reading room with ancient texts and the weight of her warning pressing down on his chest.

*

The morning gathering was smaller than the previous day's—just the senior acolytes and a handful of Elders, gathered in one of the compound's more intimate chambers. Drevik stood at the back, his mind still churning through Mira's words, when the demonstration was announced.

"We've been asked to provide a showing of our training methods," Elder Corvin explained, her voice carrying the weariness of someone who had weathered many such requests. "The Northern delegation wishes to observe how we prepare our acolytes for field work. This is not unusual—inter-chapter visits often include such exchanges."

It was unusual. Drevik could read it in the tightness around Corvin's eyes, the way Thornwood's jaw had clenched when the topic was raised. This wasn't a friendly exchange of techniques. This was an assessment, possibly a test, possibly something more dangerous.

The chamber had been arranged for combat practice. Mats covered the stone floor, and various training weapons lined the walls. Drevik watched as the Northern delegation filed in, Magistra Blackwood at their head, her silver-threaded robes catching the light like fish scales.

And behind her, Mira, dressed in practice attire that matched the local style—or perhaps mocked it.

"Who will demonstrate?" Blackwood asked, though her eyes were already fixed on Drevik.

"Our senior acolytes will provide a suitable exhibition," Thornwood replied. He gestured, and four figures moved to the center of the room. Drevik recognized them—Marcus, pale and thin but deadly with thrown weapons; Elena, whose strength in physical combat was unmatched; and two others whose names he hadn't yet learned.

"And your newest acquisition?" Blackwood's gaze hadn't shifted. "I've heard such interesting reports. Surely you'll let him participate?"

Lexi stepped forward, but Drevik was already moving. He walked past her, past the other acolytes, until he stood at the center of the room. The shadows seemed to lean toward him, drawn by the tension thrumming through his body.

"I'm happy to demonstrate," he said quietly. "Who would you like me to face?"

Blackwood's smile was everything her daughter's was not—cold, calculating, utterly without warmth. "Mira. I think you two should become better acquainted."

Mira rose from her position among the delegation and moved to stand opposite him. She'd braided her dark hair back from her face, revealing the sharp angles of her features. Her eyes held his, and beneath the competitive assessment, he saw something else—a warning, perhaps, or an invitation.

"Standard combat rules?" she asked, her voice carrying to the room. "First to yield, no permanent damage, shadow abilities permitted?"

"Permitted but not required," Thornwood interjected. "This is a demonstration, not a trial by combat."

"Of course." Mira's smile was a blade. "I wouldn't want to damage such a promising acolyte. The chapter has invested considerable resources in his training, I'm sure."

She moved first.

Drevik had barely a heartbeat to react. Mira's attack was a blur of motion—fast, precise, aimed at the nerve cluster in his shoulder. He deflected, shadows wrapping around his forearm, and felt the impact vibrate through his bones.

She was fast. Faster than anyone he'd faced, faster even than Lexi. And she fought with a precision that spoke of years of formal training, every movement economical and deadly.

He gave ground, letting her press forward, watching the patterns in her attacks. Jab, cross, sweep. Feint left, strike right. Her combinations were textbook perfect, the kind of thing he'd studied in training scrolls—but executed with a speed that made theory irrelevant.

Then he saw the opening.

Drevik ducked under a swing that would have caught his jaw and swept her legs. Mira leaped over the attempt, but he'd expected that—his follow-up was a shadow-enhanced grab at her ankle. The darkness wrapped around her boot, pulling her off balance.

She twisted in midair, landing on her hands and springing back to her feet in one fluid motion. Her eyes had widened fractionally.

"Nice," she breathed. "You've been trained well."

She came at him again, but this time her attacks incorporated shadow work. Darkness flickered at the edges of his vision, and he had to fight the urge to flinch away from shapes that weren't quite there. Hallucinations cast by her abilities, meant to distract and disorient.

He closed his eyes.

"Drevik—" Lexi's voice, sharp with concern.

He tuned it out. The shadows in the room spoke to him—not in words, but in impressions, in movements, in the subtle currents of air and energy. He could feel Mira's position without seeing her, could track her movements by the way she disturbed the darkness around her.

When she struck, he was ready.

His counter caught her wrist, spun her around, and pinned her against his chest with shadows holding her arms. For a moment, they stood frozen, her back pressed to his front, both of them breathing hard.

"Yield," he said quietly.

Mira laughed. It was genuine this time, surprised and delighted. "I yield."

He released her. She stepped away, turning to face him with an expression that had shifted into something more complex than competitive assessment.

"Well done," Magistra Blackwood said, and her voice had an edge that hadn't been there before. "Your training methods are... effective."

"Our acolytes learn quickly," Thornwood replied. "When given proper instruction."

The implication was clear. Mira had never lost a match—until now. And Drevik, the outsider, the unknown quantity, had beaten her in front of both delegations.

He saw Lexi's face as he returned to his position. Her expression held pride and fear in equal measure.

*

The rest of the day passed in a blur of meetings, discussions, and careful political maneuvering. Drevik was required at several of them—apparently, his existence was now a topic of inter-chapter debate—and by the time evening fell, he was exhausted in ways that had nothing to do with physical exertion.

He found himself in the compound's garden, one of the few places where natural moonlight reached the underground complex. The plants here grew in careful patterns, their growth shaped by generations of shadow magic into configurations that optimized their medicinal properties.

"You were impressive today."

He didn't turn at Mira's voice. "Are you following me?"

"Coincidence. I find gardens restful after confrontations." She moved to stand beside him, her eyes on the moonlit plants. "My mother will be... unhappy. She doesn't like being reminded that her plans can go awry."

"Your mother's plans are her concern."

"Aren't they? You're part of them now, whether you like it or not." Mira plucked a leaf from a nearby plant, rolling it between her fingers. "You made a statement today, whether you intended to or not. You showed that you're not just a promising acolyte—you're a weapon. Weapons are valuable. Weapons are coveted. Weapons are targeted by those who fear them."

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying that you should be careful." Her voice had lost its playful edge. "My mother doesn't lose. She doesn't accept setbacks. She'll find another way to get what she wants, and what she wants is you. Or, if she can't have you, she'll find a way to ensure no one else can benefit from your abilities."

"You're warning me against your own mother?"

"I'm telling you the truth. What you do with it is your choice." Mira released the leaf, watching it drift to the ground. "I told you earlier—someone in your inner circle is compromised. That wasn't manipulation. That was fact. My mother has contacts everywhere. She's had years to cultivate influence in chapters that think they're immune to her reach."

"Why tell me this?"

"Because you interest me." The admission was quiet, almost reluctant. "Because you're the first person in years who has surprised me. And because I know what it's like to be a piece on someone else's board, moved without your consent, sacrificed when convenient."

She turned to face him fully. In the moonlight, her features held an intensity he hadn't seen before.

"I'm not offering alliance. I'm not asking for loyalty. I'm offering information, freely given. What you do with it—whether you trust it, whether you act on it—is entirely your choice. But I thought you should know that not everyone who smiles at you is your friend, and not everyone who challenges you is your enemy."

She reached out and brushed a strand of hair from his forehead. The gesture was intimate, unexpected, and Lexi's voice echoed in his memory—She'll try to get close to you. It's what she does.

"I'll see you tomorrow," Mira said. "The negotiations continue. Try to get some rest. You'll need your strength."

She walked away, leaving him alone with the moonlight and the weight of too many secrets.

*

He found Lexi in the training hall, as he'd expected. She was working through forms alone, her movements sharp with controlled frustration.

"You're still awake," he said.

"So are you." She didn't stop her practice. "Mira found you, didn't she?"

"She found me in the garden. We talked."

"Talked." Lexi's voice could have cut glass. "About what?"

"Warnings. Information. Her mother's intentions." He moved to stand in her path, forcing her to stop. "Lexi, what's going on? Why does the Northern chapter care so much about me?"

She was silent for a long moment. Then she turned away, her shoulders tight.

"You're valuable. Not just as an acolyte—as a symbol. An outsider who rose quickly, who has abilities that should be impossible. If my chapter keeps you, it proves that our methods work, that bloodlines aren't everything. If the Northern chapter takes you, it proves that power can be consolidated, that tradition matters more than innovation."

"That's politics. You're worried about something else."

Lexi spun to face him. "I'm worried about you. About what happens when people start treating you like a prize instead of a person. About what happens when you start believing the things people like Mira tell you."

"She said someone in my inner circle is compromised. Is she right?"

The question landed between them. Lexi's expression shifted through several emotions—surprise, concern, and something else he couldn't read.

"I don't know," she finally admitted. "It's possible. My mother has enemies, and this chapter has its share of secrets. But Mira could also be trying to make you paranoid, to isolate you from the people who actually care about you."

"Or she could be telling the truth."

"Or she could be telling the truth." Lexi stepped closer. "That's what makes her dangerous. She gives you just enough fact to make the lies believable."

Drevik reached out and caught her hand. It was an impulsive gesture, one he hadn't consciously decided to make, but he felt her fingers interlace with his.

"I need to know who I can trust," he said quietly. "I need to know if you're one of them."

"You already know the answer to that." Her voice was rough. "I've been training you, protecting you, fighting beside you since you arrived. If you can't trust me—"

"I want to hear you say it."

She looked up at him, and in the dim light of the training hall, he saw something shift in her expression. The fierce protector, the careful mentor, gave way to something more vulnerable.

"You can trust me," she said. "With your life. With anything. I swear it."

He believed her. He wasn't sure why—he'd known her for weeks, had learned to respect her skill and admire her strength, but trust was something else entirely. Yet looking into her eyes, feeling her hand in his, he believed her.

He just hoped he wasn't making a mistake.

*

That night, Drevik dreamed of shadows.

They moved around him in patterns he almost understood, speaking in voices that sounded like whispers at the edge of hearing. He walked through corridors that shifted and changed, doors opening onto rooms that shouldn't exist, stairs leading down into darkness that felt alive.

And in the darkness, eyes watched him. Silver eyes, cold and assessing. Mira's eyes, her mother's eyes, the eyes of everyone who saw him as a weapon to be claimed.

He woke with a start, his heart pounding, and found the shadows in his room pooled around him like a protective blanket. They retreated when he stirred, slinking back into the corners as if embarrassed.

"Someone in your inner circle is feeding information to entities from the Shadowlands."

Mira's words echoed in his mind. He lay in the darkness, listening to his own breathing, and wondered how long it would take to figure out which of the people around him was lying to him.

And what would happen when he did.
