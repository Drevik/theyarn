CHAPTER FOURTEEN — HALLA'S SILENCE

The requisition list came to supper folded in oilcloth, and Drevik read it aloud with the salt cellar at his elbow.

"Two granaries north of the crossroads. The ferry tollhouse at Wether's Landing. The wayhouse at Brant's Ford — standing writ, executed last moon. Keeper retained." He read the page once and passed it down the table, and after that he did not need the page, because he never needed a page twice.

"The world ends in paperwork," he said. "I'd hoped for choirs."

Vesper took the list by its corners and squared its edges, the way she took every claim that came into her hands.

Halla ladled. Nobody at that table knew that Brant's Ford had been hers — eleven years of long table, eleven winters of bins — and she had carried exactly one thing of it into the Fold, folded in oilcloth of her own, in the deep pocket of her apron, where it had lain since spring.

He salted his bowl before he tasted it. Three suppers running. In a year of feeding him she had never once seen him salt blind; he stole the first slice of every batch and ate it walking the length of her kitchen, and the theft was how she knew him glad. A man who salts before tasting trusts nothing, not even the pot. She ladled to the rim and did not spill, and passed the bread with a steady hand, and said nothing, and that was the ninth night of saying nothing.

The kitchen never cooled, so at midnight she baked.

Midnight in the Fold was a lamp matter, not a sky matter. She lit two lamps, set the sponge, and opened the stores ledger to the day's page. Flour, down two measures. Salt, level. Oil, down one. Her hand went down the column the way it had gone down columns for eleven years, honest as a scale, and stopped where the column ended — because the true entry for the day was one wayhouse, lost by silence, and there was no line on the page for that.

A ledger lies by omission first. She had taught it to every girl who ever worked her bins: keep a true count or keep nothing, because a pot can feed a lie only so long before the lie eats first.

She took the paper out of the apron's deep pocket. Onion-skin, three seals, a wage line with a sum in it that would have paid the miller twice over. The Bureau had written her name at the top before she had answered a single question. The form was older than her answer. She had been a long time understanding what that meant, and tonight she understood it with her hands: they do not ask. They file. They write you into the count and then wait for you to agree with it.

The kitchen door opened on the third hour.

Sera came in with her lamp and did not ask for food, because she never asked for anything she could carry herself. She set the lamp on the table, sat, and watched the dough with the attention she gave empty altars.

"You're proofing at the wrong hour," Sera said.

"The pot wanted watching."

"The pot has wanted watching for nine nights." Sera folded her hands. "You haven't tasted it since the stewards came home. I put the spoon in your hand on Tuesday and you gave the pot to Wren and called it done. You never call it done. You call it right, or you call it again."

Halla punched down the dough. It gave under her fists the way dough gives, honest about being flat.

"You've been paying," Sera said. "A month of it. Second helpings for everyone, and you take none. You bake for six and eat like a debtor. Sit somewhere, Halla. Then tell him."

"Tell him what? He trusts me with his stores."

"He trusts you with his table. There's a difference, and you've been hiding in it." Sera's voice did not rise. It never rose; it was built for stone rooms. "Is it about the Bureau?"

Not a guess. Halla had watched this woman pray at an altar that gave back nothing, year upon year, and knew that such women learn the shapes of what others carry.

"There's a paper in my apron that should have been ash in the spring."

"Then tonight it's ash, or it's said. Those are the two mercies. The third is keeping it, and that one eats."

"Which do you pick, when you have one?"

"Said," Sera said. "And I've slept since."

Halla banked the oven. She took the loaf in one hand and the paper in the other, and followed Sera down the corridor, and the lamps behind her burned straight, and she was not afraid.

The Fold had one room with a single door, and the door was his. He kept the seam hours there the way she kept the proof hours: whether or not anything came. Tonight the crystals in the mitre were moving fast on their stand, and the staff leaned against the frame with its orb guttered low, and Drevik stood in his vestments with the collar open at the throat, the mask in his hand. The open collar was his way of being off duty, if a man who holds a door shut is ever off duty.

He turned, and the room turned with him. Halla had fed eleven years of the road — carters, saints, liars, one drowned prince — and she could guess any of them to the bone by their second bowl. She had given up guessing him in the first month. Not because he hid. Because the house was larger than the door.

"A loaf at this hour," he said, "is a gift or a bribe. I'll take my chances."

"She'll pay you both," Sera said, and set her lamp on the shelf, and sat where Halla could see her hands.

Halla set the loaf on the table. Then she set the paper beside it. She had thought the whole walk down the corridor about which to give him first. She gave him the paper.

He looked at them — bread and paper, side by side — and put the mask down on the table, the way another man would take off a hat coming into a church.

"Brant's Ford is mine," she said.

He did not move.

"Eleven years. The long table, the bins, the second pot. Petra runs it now; nine years she stood at my elbow before I left it to her. You never asked where I fed people before I fed you, and I took your not-asking for grace. It wasn't. It was luck, and last moon it ran out." She kept her hands still on the table, because they wanted to work, and there was nothing there to work on. "In the thaw, a Bureau woman came. Ate two bowls, paid exact to the copper. Then she walked my cellar with a form on a board and copied my bin-counts. Eleven years of my counting went into her ink in one afternoon. She asked nothing a host may refuse. She wrote down everything a host can't help showing."

"She asked about the ford," he said.

"She asked who'd bound the thing in the ford the autumn before. I said the ford keeps no guests' book." Halla looked at the lamp. "That was true. It was also the first hole in my ledger. And when she left, she left this." The paper sat between them, thin as an onion's coat. "A standing claim on the house as a provisioning asset. A wage line, filled in — a winter's flour, three times over. And my name at the top, in her ink, written before I'd said a word. The form was older than my answer. That's what they are. They file you first and wait for you to agree."

"Why keep it?"

"I had a miller I owed and girls with boots gone soft." She said it plainly, because there was no other honest way to say it. "I kept it in the tin with the good scissors, like a coin I'd decided not to spend. Then you came through in the green season, and the wage stopped being the point, and the paper stayed in the tin. And I brought it here in my apron, and I never entered it in any count." She straightened. "Say what it is, so I hear it said by a voice that isn't mine. I kept a meeting off my count. In kitchens we call that a false ledger."

"Nine days," he said. "Heavy loaves. I was waiting to be told why."

"You knew."

"I knew the bread." He turned the paper over once, corner to corner, the way he counted anything he meant to keep. "I don't read my household, Halla. A man who holds doors shut all day gets one room he doesn't search, and it's this one. You put the omission in the one room I don't search, and you found the one shelf I don't reach. I don't like it there."

"The writ executed last moon," she said. "Keeper retained. That's Petra. She'll bake their rations or starve beside her own ovens. And the road out of Brant's Ford runs north now — to the dig at Harlaw. My flour. My ovens. Whatever they're lifting out from under that bell, my bread is feeding the hands that lift it."

"Then hear the cost, said plainly, because I give no mercy without its invoice." He laid his hand flat on the paper. "They own your name on that road, and under it they have filed eleven years of your habits — who salts, who owes, who sleeps light, who keeps a second set of keys. They own the house this month, and the house is feeding Harlaw. And when they come for you — they will; you're their tidy asset now — they won't come as soldiers. They'll come as a bill, with your own mark on it. That's what nine nights of silence bought, and it was dear."

The lamp hissed a little. Sera's hands stayed folded.

"Now the rest," he said. "The meeting was hospitality. She ate your bread; you owed her the wall — I'd have done the same with worse manners. The wage line — a woman alone with a debt is owed one coin she doesn't spend. I've done worse for less reason. The sin is nine days old, not nine months. You knew what the Bureau was the night the stewards came home, and you kept it out of the count one more night, and then one more. That's not treason. It's a false ledger, and you knew it, because your loaves told me so before you did."

He broke the night's loaf without asking. Took the first slice. Ate it standing, unhurried, the way he had eaten the first slice in her wayhouse kitchen before she had a name for him. The crumb was right. The theft was back.

"Put it in the count," he said. "All of it. The house, the assessor, the miller — bring me the miller's bill in the morning. You kept Bureau coin to pay a miller. We'll pay him with money that doesn't eat. As for Brant's Ford, I promise nothing tonight about the house. The bread I intend to see about."

"And Petra?"

"Say her name to me again tomorrow, slowly. I keep what's said to me. There is no second copy of my ledger, so there is nothing to steal from it." He looked past Halla to Sera. "You found her before I did."

"She was proofing at the wrong hour," Sera said. "I opened a door she was already standing at."

Somewhere beyond the wall, the Threshold took a knock. The staff's orb came up from ember to coal, and the crystals in the mitre moved fast, faster. Drevik took the mitre from its stand, and then the mask, and fastened the collar to the throat.

She had seen the mask come off at that table a hundred times. She had never once watched him put it on indoors before this month. Now she watched it every night.

"The telling is yours," he said, already half-turned to the seam. "Tomorrow, at table. I won't take it from you by knowing first. Keep the paper till then — a count read aloud is the only count that stays read."

He nodded to her and went to his door, and the coal-light went with him, and the nod was kind, and it was less than a slice of bread.

"Sleep," Sera said in the corridor. "The seam is his tonight and the telling is yours tomorrow. Nothing else wants you."

Halla went back to her kitchen and tasted the pot. It wanted salt. She said so out loud, alone, and it felt like a psalm.

In the morning the Fold had grown a cold-larder off the kitchen. Slate shelves, a stone floor that took the cold and kept it, wide enough for a wayhouse's stores. Nobody had asked it to. She ran her hand over the slate once, and then minded the porridge, because the porridge needed her more than wonder did.

At breakfast the loaves were light. Sera sat at her right, shoulder to shoulder, and Halla sat like a woman with a bench under her, for once. She had queued her telling behind the porridge.

Mira came in last, sleep-mussed, key ring clipped at her hip where it always rode. She took a loaf, halved it, chewed.

"These are edible," Mira said, which from Mira was a toast.

The door rang once. Once was the world.

Drevik opened it — nobody else could — and took in the morning post from the outer chapel: oilcloth, a road-band's take off the second Bureau courier they'd lifted this month. He read it standing at the head of the table, vested and masked, straight from the seam. The crystals in the mitre were still moving fast.

"Requisition registry, north road circuit," he said. "Granaries, tollhouse — those we knew. And: Asset. Wayhouse, Brant's Ford. Standing retainer. Provisioning drawn against Harlaw. Correspondence acknowledged." He set the page down. "Correspondence acknowledged."

Vesper took the page by the corners. "There's a notation under it. Acknowledged, block nine. Block nine is a clerk's block. Replies filed under block nine go out on forms issued to cleared hands. Wayhouse keepers don't get block nine. Clerks do."

Mira leaned in over Vesper's shoulder before she could stop herself. "Vane's office runs block nine. She sat two desks from me for four years. If there's an acknowledged reply, it went through hands like—"

She stopped. Everyone at the table heard where the sentence went.

"Hands like yours are at this table," Vesper said. "Nowhere else in his orbit."

"I burned my warrants when I walked," Mira said. "I kept my seals, yes — a woman who surrenders her keyring has to trust somebody else's locks, and I trusted mine. That is not correspondence. That's carpentry."

"Then who answered them?"

Nobody spoke. The porridge skinned over in its pot. Wren had gone still over a hem, her thread slack between her fingers; she had sat where Mira sat, at another table, once, and the whole table knew it, and it made the table gentle and the gentle didn't help.

"Claims are not testimony," Drevik said. "It's a bill. It says what they want us to owe."

"It's a claim sworn into a registry," Vesper said. "In my law, an unanswered claim gets believed by default. Until someone at this table answers it, the claim has one owner here. Answer it, Mira."

Mira stood. Her bench scraped the slate. She unclipped the key ring and set it down on the boards, and the keys' ringing was the loudest thing in the Fold.

"Take them," she said. "If keys are evidence, I won't sit at a table where hardware stands trial."

Sera's eyes were not on Mira. They were on Halla, and had been since the registry entry, patient as proof.

Drevik was watching her too. He had been watching since the word acknowledged, the star-shaped pupils of him steady, and under the steadiness the cost of every second she waited. He would not take this from her. That was the whole of his mercy, and it was heavy.

The pot can feed a lie only so long before the lie eats first. She had taught that to every girl who ever worked her bins. It was time she obeyed it in front of witnesses.

Halla stood. Her bench complained against the slate. The table came around to her, face after face, and Drevik's came last and easiest, because he had known since midnight, and he had left the telling to her, and the telling had come due.

She took the paper out of the apron.

"Before you try her," she said, "you'll hear my count."
