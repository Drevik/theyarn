Chapter 13: Chaos and Choice

Chapter 13: Chaos and Choice

The screaming started three seconds before the world tore apart.

Drevik was standing at the edge of the ceremonial chamber, watching Elder Thornwood address the gathered initiates, when the first rift opened. It wasn't like the controlled portals the Wardens created—this was a ragged wound in reality, bleeding shadow and cold air into the room. Something crawled through it.

He didn't have time to see what. His vision went white, and then—

—a figure in the crowd, hood drawn low, moving against the flow of fleeing bodies. Hands moving in a pattern Drevik recognized from his training. Shadow-knife forming. Elder Thornwood's back exposed. The blade flying. Three seconds. Two. One—

Drevik moved.

He didn't think. Thinking took too long. His body simply lunged, crossing the distance between the crowd and the dais in three desperate strides. He hit Elder Thornwood from behind, shoving the older man aside just as the shadow-knife sang through the air.

It was meant for the Elder's heart.

It found Drevik's shoulder instead.

The pain was immediate and absolute—not just physical, but wrong, like the blade was made of frozen nothing that wanted to eat him from the inside out. He heard himself scream as he went down, cracking his head against the stone floor. Above him, chaos erupted. More rifts opening. Shadow creatures pouring through. The assembled initiates scattering in panic.

And through it all, a hooded figure turning toward him, frozen in the act of retreat.

Their eyes met.

Drevik saw the shock in the stranger's face—shock that an initiate had stopped the assassination. Then the figure raised one hand, shadows gathering, and a portal swallowed them whole. But not before Drevik saw it: the glint of silver at the figure's collar.

Warden insignia.

A Warden. A Warden had just tried to kill an Elder.

"Drevik!"

Lexi's voice cut through the screaming and the roaring of shadow beasts. He tried to answer, but his lungs weren't working right. The shadow-knife had dissolved, leaving a wound that gaped and wept something that wasn't quite blood. He could feel it trying to close, trying to heal, but the shadow-taint kept the edges from knitting.

Lexi's face appeared above him, pale and tight with fear. Her hands pressed against his shoulder, and he felt the cool rush of her healing magic trying to counter the corruption.

"You idiot," she hissed, but her voice cracked. "You absolute idiot, what were you thinking?"

"Assassin," Drevik managed. "Warden. Saw the insignia."

Her eyes went wide. Around them, the battle raged—Wardens and Seekers fighting shadow creatures, trying to close the rifts, trying to protect the fleeing initiates. But Lexi didn't move from his side.

"Thornwood," Drevik gasped. "Is he—"

"I don't care about Thornwood right now!" Lexi's hands pressed harder, and more healing magic flooded through him. It felt like ice water in his veins, fighting the shadow-infection. "I care about you, you reckless—"

A shadow creature lunged at them from the darkness. Lexi didn't even turn—she raised one hand and blasted it apart with a bolt of pure lunar light, never breaking eye contact with Drevik.

"We need to move him," someone said—Drevik couldn't see who. His vision was swimming, the edges going dark.

"I can move him," Lexi snarled. "Get back to the fight."

"Lexi, you can't—"

"I said get back to the fight."

There was something in her voice that Drevik had never heard before. Not anger. Not fear. Something rawer. Something that made the other person retreat without another word.

Lexi's arms slid under him, careful of his wounded shoulder. She was strong—stronger than she looked, the result of years of training—and she lifted him like he weighed nothing. The world lurched sickeningly as she shadow-walked them both out of the ceremonial chamber.

They emerged in a small room Drevik didn't recognize. Stone walls, soft candlelight, a narrow bed. A healing space, he realized dimly. One of the Order's private recovery rooms.

Lexi lowered him onto the bed with surprising gentleness. Her hands were shaking.

"Lexi," Drevik tried. "The battle—"

"Others can handle it." Her voice was clipped, professional. "You need healing. Real healing. The shadow-taint is deep."

She moved to a cabinet on the far wall, pulling out jars and bundles of herbs, a bowl, clean cloths. Her movements were quick and precise, but Drevik could see the tension in her shoulders. The way her jaw was clenched.

"Lexi."

She didn't turn around.

"Lexi."

She froze. Then slowly, she faced him. In the soft candlelight, her face was a mask of controlled fear—but her eyes betrayed her. They were wet. Red-rimmed.

"Healing requires skin contact," she said, her voice barely steady. "I need to remove your shirt."

Drevik nodded, trying to sit up. His body screamed in protest. Lexi was there instantly, supporting his back, helping him pull the ruined fabric over his head. The movement jostled his wound, and he bit back a groan.

She lowered him back to the bed, then stood looking down at him. Her gaze traced the wound—the ugly gash across his shoulder, the blackened veins spreading from it where the shadow-taint was trying to crawl deeper into his body. Her expression shifted from fear to something harder. Determination.

"This is going to hurt," she warned. "The shadow-taint fights back when you try to purge it."

"I trust you."

Something flickered in her eyes at those words. She swallowed hard, then climbed onto the bed beside him, straddling his hips. The position was practical—she needed to brace herself to channel enough healing energy—but Drevik's body didn't care about practicality. Even through the pain, he was aware of her weight settling over him. The warmth of her thighs against his sides. The way her auburn hair fell forward as she leaned close.

"Look at me," she commanded softly. "Don't close your eyes. If you start to fade, I need to know."

He nodded. She placed both hands over the wound.

The healing began.

It was like fire and ice at once—her magic burning through the shadow-taint, freezing the corruption in its tracks. Drevik's back arched off the bed as pain lanced through him. He grabbed at something—her hip, her thigh—and she didn't pull away. Just kept channeling, her face tight with concentration, sweat beading on her forehead.

"That's it," she murmured, though whether to him or herself, he couldn't tell. "Fight it. Don't let it take hold."

He could feel the shadow-taint retreating, pushed back by the relentless force of her healing. But the pain was blinding. He was dimly aware that he was making sounds—gasps, groans, sounds that might have been words if he could form them. Through it all, Lexi held steady, her weight grounding him, her eyes never leaving his face.

"Almost," she breathed. "Just a little more—"

The last of the shadow-taint gave way. The wound began to close properly, new tissue knitting together with supernatural speed. Drevik collapsed back against the bed, panting, his whole body trembling with exhaustion and relief.

Lexi slumped forward, catching herself on her hands. Her face was inches from his, both of them breathing hard. The healing had taken a lot out of her—Drevik could see the fatigue in her features, the dark circles under her eyes.

But she was still looking at him with that raw, unguarded expression.

"You could have died," she whispered.

"I know."

"You idiot." But there was no venom in it. Just pain. "You threw yourself in front of a shadow-knife. For an Elder you barely know. You could have—"

She broke off. Her whole body was shaking now.

"Lexi." Drevik raised his good arm, brushing his fingers against her cheek. She flinched like his touch burned, but didn't pull away. "I'm okay."

"You're not okay." Her voice cracked. "You have a scar that will never fully fade. The shadow-taint left a mark. Every new moon, it'll ache. You'll feel the cold of that knife for the rest of your life."

"Then I'll feel it." He traced his thumb along her cheekbone, catching a tear before it could fall. "I'd rather feel the cold than watch someone die when I could stop it."

She made a sound that was half-laugh, half-sob. "You stupid, noble—"

She kissed him.

It wasn't like their first kiss—that had been tentative, questioning. This was desperate. Hungry. Her hands fisted in his hair, and he pulled her down against him with his good arm, heedless of his healing shoulder. The pain was distant, irrelevant. All that mattered was her mouth on his, her body pressed against his chest, the taste of her tears on his lips.

"Lexi," he gasped between kisses. "The door—someone might—"

"I don't care." She kissed him again, harder. "I don't care about any of it."

But even as she said it, she was pulling back. Not far—just enough to look at him. Her eyes were red-rimmed but dry now. Her expression had shifted into something more composed, though the rawness still showed at the edges.

"I can't lose another person I care about," she said quietly. "I've already lost too many. My mother. My brother. Friends who didn't make it through their trials." Her voice was steady, but Drevik could see the cost of that steadiness. "I won't lose you too. I can't."

He understood then what she was really saying. Not just that she cared about him. That he'd become essential. That the thought of losing him broke something in her that she didn't know how to fix.

"You won't lose me," he said. "I promise."

"Promises mean nothing in the Order." Her thumb traced along his jaw. "We both know that."

"Then I'll just have to survive anyway."

She laughed—a wet, shaky sound. "You're impossible."

"Yeah." He smiled up at her, exhausted and aching and happier than he had any right to be. "But you like impossible."

She shook her head, but she was almost smiling too. "We should get back. They'll need help with the cleanup."

"Right." He tried to sit up, and she shifted off him, helping him upright. His shoulder throbbed, but the healing was holding. "The assassin—"

"Escaped." Lexi's expression hardened. "You're sure it was a Warden?"

"Saw the insignia. Silver collar pin. Only Wardens have them."

Lexi was quiet for a moment. "That means—"

"It means the traitor is someone high up. Someone who's been in the Order long enough to earn that rank." Drevik reached for his ruined shirt, but Lexi handed him a clean one from the cabinet instead. "And it means no one can be trusted."

She watched him dress, her expression thoughtful. "Except each other."

He met her eyes. "Except each other."

The aftermath of the attack was controlled chaos.

Drevik emerged from the healing room to find the ceremonial chamber strewn with debris. The rifts had been closed, the shadow creatures destroyed, but the Order was in an uproar. Initiates huddled in groups, whispering frantically. Wardens strode through the halls with grim faces, their eyes scanning for threats.

Elder Thornwood was alive. Drevik had seen to that.

The Elder was standing near the dais, deep in conversation with two other Elders. His face was pale, his robes torn where Drevik had shoved him aside, but he was whole. When he saw Drevik approaching, he fell silent.

"Ah," Thornwood said quietly. "The young man who saved my life."

The other Elders turned. Drevik recognized Elder Vane—Seraphina's mother—and Elder Kethros, the stern woman who oversaw combat training. Their expressions were unreadable.

"Elder Thornwood." Drevik bowed, ignoring the protest from his healing shoulder. "I'm glad to see you unharmed."

"Unharmed thanks to you." Thornwood's gaze was sharp, assessing. "I'm told you intercepted a shadow-knife meant for my heart. That you saw the assassin when no one else did."

"I had a vision, Elder. Just seconds of foresight. Enough to know something was wrong."

"A vision." Elder Kethros's voice was flat. "Foresight is a rare gift."

"It's unreliable," Drevik admitted. "I can't control it. But when it comes, the images are clear."

Thornwood exchanged a look with Elder Vane. "And what did you see clearly, young initiate?"

" The assassin's insignia. A Warden's collar pin. Silver, with the eclipsed moon symbol."

The Elders went very still.

"You're certain?" Elder Vane asked quietly.

"I know what I saw."

"A serious accusation," Elder Kethros said. "If true, it means—"

"It means we have a traitor among our Wardens." Thornwood's voice was grim. "Someone who has earned our highest trust."

The words hung in the air. Drevik could feel the weight of them—the implications, the danger. If a Warden was willing to assassinate an Elder, the corruption went deep.

"The attack was a diversion," Lexi said, stepping up beside Drevik. She'd composed herself, but he could still see the tension in her shoulders. "The rifts, the shadow creatures—all of it was meant to create chaos. To give the assassin cover."

"And it would have worked." Elder Vane's eyes were on Drevik. "If not for this young man's gift."

There was something strange in her expression. Drevik couldn't quite read it—approval, perhaps, or something deeper.

"Initiate Markinsoul." Thornwood's voice was formal now. "Your actions tonight demonstrated courage, quick thinking, and loyalty to the Order. You placed yourself in mortal danger to protect one of our own."

"It was the right thing to do."

"Indeed it was." Thornwood glanced at the other Elders, who nodded. "In recognition of your bravery, and in light of the abilities you've demonstrated, I hereby advance you to the rank of Acolyte."

Drevik blinked. "Elder?"

"You've earned it." A faint smile crossed Thornwood's weathered face. "Though I suspect your path forward will be far more dangerous than most. The traitor saw your face. Knows you can identify them."

Drevik hadn't considered that. But of course—the assassin would see him as a threat now. Someone who could expose them.

"I understand, Elder."

"I believe you do." Thornwood clasped his shoulder—gently, on the uninjured side. "Welcome to the Order, Acolyte Markinsoul. May the shadows serve you well."

The ceremony was brief.

Drevik stood before the gathered members—those who'd survived the attack, those who weren't wounded or busy with cleanup—and spoke the words he'd memorized weeks ago. Vows of loyalty, of service, of sacrifice. The traditional oath of the Acolyte.

I swear by shadow and moonlight to serve the Order. To walk between worlds and guard the Veil. To give my life if the balance demands it.

The words felt different now. Heavier. He'd nearly given his life tonight, and he understood in a way he hadn't before what that promise meant.

Lexi watched from the crowd. Her expression was carefully neutral, but Drevik could see the pride underneath.

After the ceremony, there were congratulations. Slaps on the back, nods of respect, a few curious looks from other initiates who'd been at the gathering. Drevik accepted them all with as much grace as he could muster, but his shoulder was aching, and exhaustion was creeping up on him.

He needed to rest. To process. To figure out what came next.

He was almost to his dorm room when the shadows near his door rippled.

A message scroll materialized from the darkness—the Order's way of sending urgent communications. Drevik caught it before it could fall, breaking the seal with practiced ease.

The handwriting was familiar. Seraphina.

They know I talked to you. I have to run. But I left something for you in your dorm room. It will tell you who the traitor is.

Be careful, Drevik. Trust no one.

Drevik's heart hammered as he read the words again. His hand closed around the scroll, crumpling it.

Seraphina was running. She was scared. And she'd left him evidence—evidence that could expose a Warden.

He pushed open his door.

The room was dark. His roommate was out—probably still helping with the attack aftermath. Drevik stepped inside, his newly-healed shoulder throbbing, and reached for the light.

Something was sitting on his pillow.

A small leather journal, bound with black cord. He didn't recognize it—had never seen it before. But when he opened it, he recognized Seraphina's handwriting.

Notes. Names. Dates. A careful record of everything she'd observed.

And on the first page, circled in red ink, a single name.

Drevik stared at it, his blood running cold.

The traitor wasn't just a Warden.

The traitor was someone he knew. Someone who'd been watching him since his first day in the Order.

Someone who, even now, might be wondering if Seraphina had left anything behind.

Drevik looked at the door. Then at the journal.

He had a choice to make.

And whatever he chose, there would be no going back.
