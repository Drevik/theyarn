Electric kettles are simple devices that manufacturers manage to mess up in surprising ways. Bad spouts that splash. Lids that scald your hand when you open them. Heating elements that give up after 18 months. Plastic interiors that make your tea taste like a swimming pool. Here's what actually works.

What Actually Matters in an Electric Kettle

  • Spout type: Regular spouts pour fast and splash — fine for tea, terrible for pour-over coffee. Gooseneck spouts give you precision control over flow rate and direction. If you make pour-over, gooseneck is non-negotiable.
  • Temperature control: If you drink green or white tea, you need variable temperature. Boiling water destroys delicate teas. If you only drink black tea and French press coffee, a basic boil-only kettle is fine.
  • Interior material: Stainless steel or glass. Plastic interiors leach flavor over time and hold onto mineral buildup. Stainless is easiest to clean. Glass lets you see the water level but is fragile.
  • Capacity: 0.8-1L is ideal for one person. 1.0-1.7L for two or more. Kettles over 1.5L are heavy when full and slow to heat.
  • Speed: A 1500W kettle boils 1L in about 4 minutes. That's standard. Anything over 5 minutes is slow. Under 3 minutes means you're probably looking at a lower capacity or higher wattage than usual.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a gooseneck kettle for pour-over coffee?

If you make pour-over coffee more than once a week, yes. A gooseneck spout gives you control over flow rate and direction that a regular kettle can't match. Pour-over requires a slow, circular pour to saturate the grounds evenly — a standard spout dumps water in a wide arc that creates channeling. If you only drink French press or tea, a gooseneck is unnecessary.

What temperature should I heat water to for different drinks?

Black tea: 212°F (100°C). Green tea: 170-185°F (77-85°C) — anything hotter makes it bitter. White tea: 160-175°F (71-80°C). Pour-over coffee: 195-205°F (90-96°C). French press: same as pour-over. Oolong tea: 185-205°F (85-96°C). If you drink more than one of these regularly, a variable temperature kettle is worth the extra money.

Are expensive kettles worth it?

Above $80, you're paying for gooseneck precision, variable temperature, and build quality. A $25 basic kettle boils water just as fast. A $80-120 gooseneck kettle adds pour control that matters for pour-over coffee. A $200+ kettle adds hold-temperature mode, nicer materials, and exact degree control. The sweet spot for most people is $50-100 depending on whether you need gooseneck and variable temperature.

How long should an electric kettle last?

A well-made kettle with stainless steel or glass interior should last 3-5 years with daily use. Kettles with plastic interiors develop mineral buildup and off-flavors faster. The most common failure point is the heating element — when a kettle starts taking noticeably longer to boil, the element is degrading. Descaling every 1-2 months (run a 50/50 white vinegar and water solution) extends the life significantly.

Is stainless steel or glass better for an electric kettle?

Stainless steel is more durable, doesn't break if you drop it, and heats slightly faster. Glass lets you see the water level and boiling action, which is useful for tracking temperature, but it's fragile and heavier. Both are better than plastic, which can leach flavors into the water over time. If you have kids or are clumsy, get stainless steel. If you want to see your pour-over bloom, get glass.
