Core idea

Flirting and cruising are not about trickery or manipulation; they are systems of communication used to gauge interest and negotiate boundaries in real-time. The foundation of successful interaction is a shared ethical assumption: that all parties are competent enough to know their own minds. To engage in these practices effectively, you must cultivate the ability to say "no" without drama, thereby making a "yes" meaningful. By making offers "cheap" (low-stakes) and refusals "safe" (non-punitive), you create a social environment where clear, direct proposals can occur without fear of social or physical retaliation.

Key concepts and terms

  • The Ethical Assumption of Competence: The belief that you should extend credit to others for knowing their own boundaries. You assume that if someone says "no," they are a reliable agent of their own desire, which allows you to take their "yes" at face value.
  • Cheap Offers: Low-stakes propositions or signals that are easy to decline. These are designed to test the waters without forcing the other person into a high-pressure situation or a definitive commitment.
  • Safe Refusal: A way of declining an advance that prioritizes the comfort of the person being rejected. A safe refusal ensures the person who made the offer does not feel shamed, judged, or socially punished for their attempt.
  • Escalation: The progressive increase in the intensity of signals, moving from subtle attention to more explicit verbal or physical propositions.
  • Coming Out Slutty: The strategic disclosure of your relationship structure (e.g., polyamorous, open, solo poly) at a time that is appropriate for the context of the interaction.
  • Cruising: The act of searching for sexual or romantic encounters in specific environments (clubs, parks, bathhouses, etc.), often involving non-verbal signals and specific "scripts" that vary depending on gender identity and sexual orientation.

Techniques worth drilling

  1. The Low-Stakes Signal (The "Cheap Offer")
  • Goal: To test interest without creating social friction.
  • Step 1: Select a non-verbal cue, such as sustained eye contact (the "three-second rule") or a specific way of positioning your body toward someone in a crowded space.
  • Step 2: Observe the response. If the person looks away or turns away, immediately de-escalate your attention to a neutral state.
  • Step 3: If the person returns the gaze or moves closer, move to a verbal "cheap offer," such as a compliment or a brief, low-pressure question.
  1. The Drama-Free Decline (The "Safe Refusal" Practice)
  • Goal: To build the muscle of rejecting offers without making the other person feel "bad" or "creepy."
  • Step 1: When approached by someone you are not interested in, prepare a standard, polite, but firm phrase (e.g., "I'm flattered, but I'm not interested," or "I'm actually here with my partner tonight").
  • Step/Step 2: Deliver the phrase with a neutral or friendly facial expression. Do not roll your eyes, sigh heavily, or use a tone of disgust.
  • Step 3: Immediately pivot the conversation back to a neutral topic or return to your original activity to signal that the rejection is not a "social death sentence" for them.
  1. Strategic Disclosure (Timing the "Coming Out")
  • Goal: To communicate your relationship status in a way that manages expectations without killing the mood.
  • Step 1: Identify the "threshold of intimacy" in your current interaction. This is the moment when the conversation shifts from casual small talk to potential romantic/sexual interest.
  • Step 2: Prepare a concise, one-sentence summary of your status (e.g., "Just so you know, I'm in an open relationship").
  • Step 3: Deliver this information during a natural pause. Do not lead with it as a disclaimer before you have even established rapport, but do not wait until a physical move is being made.
  1. Reading the Environment (Cruising Literacy)
  • Goal: To understand the specific "scripts" and risks of different populations.
  • Step 1: Observe a specific venue (a club, a park, a specific bar) for 20 minutes without participating.
  • Step 2: Identify the "signals" being used by the regulars (e.g., specific clothing, hand gestures, or placement in the room).
  • Step 3: Note the "safety's" of the space—how do people handle rejection there? Is it a space where directness is rewarded or where subtlety is required for safety?

Practice drill

The Feedback Loop Drill Perform this drill for the next four weeks. You will engage in two specific interactions per week.

  • The Schedule: Two reps per week (8 total reps).
  • The Tasks:
  • Rep A (The Offer): Make one explicit, low-stakes offer you genuinely want accepted (e.g., asking for a phone number, suggesting a drink, or a clear verbal compliment).
  • Rep B (The Refusal/Counter-Offer): Practice a clean, drama-free refusal of an approach, or a "counter-offer" (e.g., "I can't do tonight, but I'd be down for coffee next week").
  • The Log: After each rep, open a notebook and record the following three data points:
  1. Fear: What was the specific negative outcome you were afraid of? (e.g., "I thought they would think I'm a creep.")
  2. Reality: What actually happened? (e.g., "They said no, but they smiled and kept talking to me.")
  3. Adjustment: Based on the reaction, what will you change next time? (e.g., "I will use a more direct verbal cue next time to avoid ambiguity.")

Critiques and caveats

  • Safety and Vulnerability: While the book emphasizes "making offers cheap," it is vital to recognize that for marginalized groups (women, trans, and nonbinary people), the "risk" of an offer is often higher due to potential physical or verbal aggression. "Cheap offers" are a luxury of the safe; if you are in a high-risk environment, prioritize physical safety and situational awareness over "testing the waters."
  • The Burden of Refusal: There is a risk in over-emphasizing "safe refusals" that the responsibility for social harmony is placed entirely on the person being approached. While you should practice drama-free declines, the primary ethical burden remains on the person making the offer to read cues and respect boundaries.
  • The "Cleverness" Trap: Avoid the temptation to use "clever" or cryptic signals to avoid the vulnerability of directness. Clarity is the highest form of respect in ethical non-monogamy.

Reading notes

This section serves as the tactical application of the book's broader ethical framework. While earlier chapters establish the "why" of polyamory and ethical non-monogamy, "Flirting and Cruising" provides the "how" for navigating the external world and interacting with people outside of established relationship bubbles.
