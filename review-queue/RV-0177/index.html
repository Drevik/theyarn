The lock did not break. It yielded.

Mira sat at the small work-bench in the corner of the Fold’s workshop, her fingers stained with the black oil of a disassembled Bureau mechanism. The light in the workshop was the low, amber honey of late afternoon, the kind of light that made the dust motes look like spent gold. She did not look up when the heavy door creaked. She did not look up when the weight of the room changed.

Drevik entered the room like a shift in the weather. He did not make a sound, yet the air grew dense, pulled toward him by some unseen gravity. He wore the deep-purple vestments of the Communion, the gold embroidery catching the amber light, and the white-ceramic kitsune mask sat high on his brow, leaving his eyes visible. Those eyes—the galaxy-void of them, with the star-pupils that never stayed still—turned toward her work.

He did not speak. He simply stood, a tall, dark presence between her and the rest of the world.

"The tension is off on the third tumbler," Mira said. She kept her eyes on the spring. She did not allow her pulse to quicken, though the sight of his gloved hand resting on the edge of her bench made the metal feel cold. "It is a cheap piece of work. Aegis Bureau scrap. They build things to be replaced, not maintained."

"Perhaps they believe the replacement is more important than the repair," Drev/ik said. His voice was a low, steady chord.

"Then they are fools. A thing that cannot be repaired is a thing that cannot be trusted."

He moved closer. He did not touch her, but she could feel the warmth radiating from his heavy sleeves. He leaned over the bench, his shadow falling across her tools, swallowing the light. He picked up a small, brass key—one she had been cleaning—and turned it between his fingers. The movement was fluid, a trick of the wrist that spoke of a man who understood the mechanics of even the smallest hinge.

"And what of the Bureau's people, Mira?" he asked. "Are they also things to be replaced?"

Mira straightened. She wiped her hands on a rag, the oil leaving dark smears on the cloth. "The Bureau is a machine. It has parts. If a part fails to hold the tolerance, it is discarded. It is not personal. It is simply math."

"You speak of people as if they are gears."

"In a well-run system, they ought to be."

She looked at him then, truly looked at him. The silver-white hair was swept back from his forehead, and the tall crystal mitre held the void-crystals in a slow, hypnotic orbit. He was a man made of thresholds and secrets, a man who stood at the edge of everything and held the door shut. She hated how much she wanted to know what lay behind the mask. She hated that her entire world—the Bureau, the logic, the cold certainty of a locked door—felt thin and brittle in his presence.

He set the key down. The sound of brass hitting wood was a sharp, lonely note. "There is a fight brewing in the Hall," he said. "Sera is waiting. Vesper is already sharpening her blades. Even Halla has stopped her kneading."

Mira felt a flicker of irritation. "Then they should go. I have work to finish."

"The work will remain when the fight is over," he said. He reached out, his hand hovering near hers. He did not close the distance, but the space between them felt charged, a seam stretched to the point of tearing. "But the hearth needs its pieces. Even the sharp ones."

Mira stood, her chair scraping against the stone floor. She felt the sting of her own pride, a jagged thing that refused to be smoothed. "I am not a piece, Drevik. I am the person who ensures the pieces stay in their proper place."

He leaned in. The scent of him—incense, cold ash, and something like the ozone before a storm—filled her senses. For a moment, the workshop vanished. There was only the weight of his gaze and the terrifying, vast kindness in those star-pupiled eyes. He was a man who could break her, yet he looked at her as if she were the most vital mechanism in the Fold.

"Then come," he said. "Ensure we do not fall apart."

The Great Hall was not a room for peace.

The long table, carved from a single slab of weirwood, was crowded with the others. Sera sat at the head, her expression carved from marble, her fingers tracing the rim of a silver chalice. She was the gate; she was the first, and her silence was a heavy, liturgical thing. Beside her, Vesper sat cleaning a thin stiletto, her movements precise and lethal. Wren was there, too, her fingers moving rhythmly as if she were invisible stitching the very air, her eyes downcast.

And then there was the heat of the argument.

Mira entered to find the air thick with resentment. The tension was not a thing felt; it was a thing heard in the clipped, biting tones of the conversation.

"It is an insult to the Devoted," Sera said. Her voice was low, but it carried the weight of a funeral bell. "To allow the Aegis agents into the outer perimeter. We are a watch, not a marketplace."

"The Aegis provides the logistics we lack," Vesper countered, her blade catching a glint of torchlight. "If we want the thresholds held, we need their steel. We cannot eat liturgy, Sera."

"We do not trade our sanctity for iron," Sera replied.

Mira stepped into the light of the hearth, her face set in a mask of her own. "You are arguing about the casing while the mechanism is grinding to a halt," she said, her voice cutting through the bickering.

The room went still. Even Drevik, who had followed her in with a silent, predatory grace, paused in the shadows of the doorway.

Sera turned her gaze to Mira. It was a cold, searching look, the look of a priestess weighing a soul. "The Bureau's influence is a rot, Mira. You know this. You see the way they look at our borders. They do not see a sanctuary. They see a resource."

"I see a set of tolerances," Mira said, walking toward the table. She felt the eyes of the others on her—Wren’s soft, searching gaze; Vended’s sharp, defensive posture. "You call it rot. I call it friction. Without friction, nothing holds. Without the Bureau's supply lines, the Fold starves. You want to be pure, Sera? Fine. Be pure in a graveyard."

A sharp intake of breath came from Wren. Vesper paused her cleaning.

"That is enough," Drevik said.

He stepped into the light. He did not raise his voice, but the authority in it was absolute. The void crystals in his mitre pulsed once, a low, rhythmic thrum that seemed to settle the very floorboards. He walked to the head of the table and placed his hand on the wood.

"We are not here to debate the cost of survival," Drevik said. "We are here to ensure there is a tomorrow to debate it in. Mira, your precision is noted. Sera, your vigilance is required. But do not let the seams of this house fray because you cannot agree on the thread."

He looked at Mira. There was a challenge in his eyes, a soft, mocking tilt to his lips beneath the mask. He was daring her to stay, daring her to be part of this impossible, broken family.

Mira felt the insult burning in her throat. She wanted to walk out. She wanted to return to her oils and her gears, to the predictable, logical world of the Bureau where things stayed broken when they were meant to be broken.

Instead, she pulled out the heavy oak chair at the far end of the table. The wood groaned under her weight.

"The math does not change," Mira said, her voice hard. "If you want to ignore the reality of the Aegis, do it. But do not expect me to fix the locks when they come to break us."

She sat. She stayed.

The silence that followed was not empty; it was heavy, like the air in a room where a sudden death has occurred. Sera did not acknowledge her presence, but the tension in her shoulders had shifted. Vesper went back to her blade. Wren began to hum, a low, wordless sound that seemed to stitch the edges of the room back together.

Drevik did not sit. He remained standing, a sentinel at the edge of the light. He looked at each of them in turn—the priestess, the blade, the weaver, and the locksmith. He looked at the house he had built from the void, and for a moment, the shadows around him seemed to soften.

Mira watched him from the periphery. She watched the way his hand rested on the pommel of his staff, the way the gold embroidery on his sleeve caught the dying firelight. He was a mystery she could not solve, a lock without a key.

Across the table, Halla moved through the shadows of the periphery, carrying a tray of dark, unleavened bread. She moved with a quiet, heavy efficiency, her face obscured by the gloom. As she passed Mira, her hand lingered on the table for a second too long.

Mira caught a glimpse of Halla’s eyes. They were not watching the argument. They were watching the heavy, iron-bound door of the pantry, a small, dark corner of the Fold that even Drevik rarely entered. There was a flicker of something there—not fear, but a deep, settled dread, like the look of a woman who has found a crack in a foundation and knows she cannot mend it alone.

Halla turned away, her apron rustling in the silence.

Mira looked back at her hands. They were still stained with black oil. She picked up a piece of bread, but it tasted of nothing but ash.
