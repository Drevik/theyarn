Chapter One — The Mouth Between Worlds

The ash went up instead of down.

Drevik had the crowd exactly where he wanted them — bored, faintly hostile, three dozen strong on a market afternoon — when the burnt petitions drifting off the basilica braziers turned and rose in a slow spiral against the wind.

Then the bells went flat.

Not silent. Flat. The noon chord in the tower sagged by a quarter-tone, the way a choir sags when it hears a better harmony and loses its nerve. Pigeons left the eaves in one gray sheet and refused to land. The candle-stalls guttered blue. Above the spire of the Answering Door, where the sky had been an ordinary market blue, a seam opened — a hairline of no-color — and behind the hairline came pressure, the kind a hand feels on the far side of a door it is being told not to open.

Drevik set down his argument mid-sentence.

"Ladies and gentlemen," he said, stepping onto the fountain rim, "I'll pause the sermon. The subject has arrived."

Someone laughed. A fishwife crossed herself with a fish. Beadle Corf shoved through the press with his brass rod already up — down from there, unsanctioned liturgy, the Door's precinct, the Toll Ordinance — but the words died when the hairline parted like lips.

Sound came out of it. A chord. A chord with too many voices in it, all of them agreeing, the way no honest choir ever agrees, and the ash over the square rose faster, and the air went cold as a snuffed candle. Acres of snuffed candle. The pressure leaned.

The crowd did the arithmetic of panic in the wrong order. Half of them ran. The other half stayed to gawk, because a miracle is only a disaster that stops in time.

Drevik tipped his head back. The crystals set in his tall mitre stopped their slow drifting and aimed at the seam, compass needles finding a north that shouldn't exist. Through the eye-slots of the white ceramic half-mask — fox-faced, gold-leafed at the cheekbones, a fixture of the town's mockery for two seasons now — the seam's no-color met his eyes, and the star-shaped pupils in all that deep purple answered it.

He knew the chord. He'd heard it three seams to the north not a month past, and twice before that. The Hollow Choir had been tuning the world's loose stitching all season, pressing the Threshold, looking for a lip to press through.

"Good afternoon," he said to the sky, in the voice of a man greeting a late courier. "You're early."

"The fox-face is negotiating with weather," someone said behind him. It got a laugh, a thin one, the kind people buy to keep from screaming.

"Let him," said Beadle Corf, not lowering the rod.

Drevik turned his staff a quarter-turn, unhurried. The orb at its head brightened. He addressed the seam the way he addressed everything dangerous — plainly, terms first.

"The offer stands as it stood. One verse, ninth hour, once a year, where I can hear you. I'll set a place for you in the watch, and you'll be remembered in the only archive that would have bothered. In exchange, this mouth stays a mouth. It never becomes a throat."

The seam widened a hair. The chord gained a voice. Cold rolled off it, and the ash spiraled up into the gap and did not come back down.

"No," Drevik said. "Not the town. The verse is generous; ask the Choir if the verse isn't generous. And ask the Choir what became of the last mouth that took more than its verse." He let that sit. "Refuse, and I'll do worse than bind you. I'll forget you. Deliberately. I keep the ledger of everything that's ever sung, and you know it, because you've all been trying to get a page in it since before this town had a name. Sing through, and the page burns."

Pressure, gathering — the chord climbing toward a decision, a hall of unseen singers drawing one vast breath behind the door. Then it eased. The hairline held where it was, a mouth deciding to stay a mouth, and Drevik's orb took the chord inside itself and held it the way a bell holds sound after the clapper rests.

"Same time next year," he said. "Tell the Choir my rates haven't changed."

He brought the staff down through the last inch of it, and the sky closed like a mouth that thought better of speaking.

For three heartbeats, nothing. Then the ash he'd bargained out of the seam came back down all at once, sifting gray over the stalls, the fish, the beadle's hat, Drevik's shoulder plates. The bells found their true tone mid-swing, embarrassed. The pigeons forgave the eaves.

The square stood in the ringing quiet, and Drevik, ash-dusted to the collar — a high, stiff collar buttoned to the throat in full market heat, because a man who keeps doors shut does not leave his own seams open — smiled his sly, unhurried smile at the crowd from under the mask.

"The sermon," he said, "concludes. Every world has a Threshold, friends — the shut door between here and everywhere else. Most doors behave. Some break. Behind the broken ones are the Sundered: places that collapsed under their own contradictions and turned hungry. They press. Today one pressed hard enough to part its lips, and I talked it out of a kiss."

"Vagrancy," said Beadle Corf. "Unsanctioned miracle-mongering under the Toll Ordinance, in the precinct of the Answering Door. That's a two-copper fine, and I'll have it now."

A copper came out of the crowd and struck Drevik in the chest, thrown. It bounced off the gold celestial embroidery and rang on the cobbles.

"Miracles," Drevik said, when the laughter settled, "and this is the part no one else in my trade will tell you, so mark it — miracles are not free, and I take no one's hope and give no one's promise without saying the cost out loud. Hear the cost. That seam is not healed. It is held. Held is a job. I have taken the job. I will pay that mouth a verse every year I'm alive, and the year I stop paying, it opens wide and thanks me for my service. That's the whole miracle, priced honestly. Apparently it fetches one thrown copper."

"Two coppers," said the beadle. "The ordinance is the ordinance."

"Then I'm in debt to the sky and the city both. A memorable day."

"Fraud," a voice said near the back, easy and relieved, and the word did the rounds fast because it was cheaper than the alternative. If the man in the mask had truly bought their sky back with a hymn, they owed him something, and Orison was a town that had stopped paying what it owed long ago. Fraud asked nothing of them. Fraud let them go home.

They went. Stalls came down. Dusk began dealing gray over the rooftops, and the square emptied the way water leaves a bath.

Three stayed.

Cassius Renn counted the take sitting on the fountain's second step, strongbox open on his knees, tall hat tipped back, forty years old and built like a door himself — broad, sober, hard to move. He'd handled Drevik's permits, pitches, and collections for three years, the only fixed point in Drevik's wandering trade, and he took insult and income with the same flat, patient face.

"Eleven coppers and a button," Cassius said. "One was thrown, so subtract your dignity."

"The beadle took two."

"Nine and a button." He set the coins in rows, smallest to largest, a habit from his notary days. "Do the math with me, Drevik. In Corveth, brokers pay silver for a table that floats an inch. In Vell, the guild pays silver for rain on order, once a season, for an hour. You shut a mouth between worlds in front of three hundred witnesses, for nine coppers and a button." He closed the box. "You gave a miracle away."

"I charged the sky."

"The sky doesn't pay in silver. Other people will offer to."

"And when they offer, I'll refuse, and you'll be disappointed, and we'll both survive it." Drevik leaned on the staff and watched the spire, where the seam had been. Nothing now. Nothing visible, anyway. "Write it down, if it helps. The square, the fine, the chord. One of us has to."

"I know." Cassius tucked the box under his arm and stood. "That's why you need me. You keep nothing. Somebody has to keep something."

He said it mildly, a fact of the trade between them, and Drevik — who trusted Cassius with precisely this, always had: the hat, the permits, the arithmetic of small crowds, the safe half of the work — clapped him on the shoulder and gave him the lodging money.

The unsafe half of the work, the sky's half, he kept. That had always felt like prudence.

Two of the three stayers came to him before full dark.

Mother Bex, who had poured prayer-candles on Ladgate Row for thirty years, planted herself at arm's length and got to it. "Ninth hour, you told it. Verse owed, once a year."

"Every mouth I shut gets one. It's how they stay shut."

"Then something should burn at the ninth hour while you pay it. I'd pour the candle right, if there's a right." She named her price — nothing but the knowledge — and that was a tithe, though nobody in the square called it one yet.

The second was Tillo, the tower-ringer, a wiry man of thirty with rope-burned hands. "The chord flattened my bells," he said. "A quarter-tone, and I tuned those bells myself last spring. Are bells for you or against you?"

"Neither. Keep them true. A true bell is a seam's least favorite sound."

Tillo nodded slowly, a man filing that where he filed important things, and asked what time the watch kept, and Drevik told him: ninth hour, and come sober. Small mercies, small beginnings. Two candles and a bell. Every church that ever mattered started with less and lied about it later.

The third stayer had not come forward at all.

She stood at the altar across the yard — the Door's own altar, under the basilica's black flank, where the pilgrims hadn't knelt in a generation. Everyone in Orison knew the story: the Answering Door, a standing door of void-glass and ash-dark wood, freestanding at the head of the altar steps, its knockers polished bright by a hundred years of knuckles, its threshold swept daily. Thirty-one years since anyone claimed an answer. The priests had drifted off to richer parishes; the toll went to the beadle now; the candles were stubs that no one lit.

She was sweeping it. At dusk. After a miracle and a riot, with a broom.

Gray work-robe, ash worked into the seams of the sleeves. Dark hair pinned up badly, for practicality rather than vanity. Near his own age, he judged, twenty-nine or thereabouts. A burn had taken the outer edge of one eyebrow once, long ago; the hair grew back white along that strip, a small honest scar she hadn't bothered to hide. Her knuckles were gray to the second joint. She swept like the altar would be swept whether the world ended or not, and she watched him the whole time, unhurried, cataloguing him the way he catalogued seams.

Drevik crossed the yard. She stopped sweeping and set the broom against the altar rail, and did not curtsey, and did not kneel.

He addressed the Door instead of her, because it was easier to be honest to a door.

"Thirty-one years," he said, "and you've never once answered them. I do your job for one afternoon and they fine me two coppers and throw the third at my chest." He tipped his head back at the void-glass, black as a well at midnight, reflecting a masked man in a mitre. "You're the only colleague I have, and you're a fraud, and I mean that professionally."

Behind him, ash settled. Somewhere above, the seam he'd shut held its breath and kept its bargain.

"They think you're the fraud," the woman said.

"They think I'm the fraud because the alternative is owing me. Cheap, sane, and wrong. The beadle thinks I'm a vagrant with a good trick. What do you think?"

"I heard the chord." She came around the rail to the top step, level with him, and looked at the mask rather than through it, which most people couldn't manage. "And I heard what you refused to let it take. Then I heard what you charged yourself for the refusal. Nobody else in that crowd did the arithmetic past the throwing of coins. I did."

"And?"

"And you lied by one word. Up on the fountain, you said held is a job and you've taken the job. That's true but it's not the whole of it." No nerves on her, none he could find; her voice had the cadence of a woman reading lessons she'd already learned by heart. "A job ends. Employment stops. Yours doesn't. What happens to the verses when you die?"

The question sat in the yard like a dropped tool. Nobody had asked it. Crowds don't; crowds want blessings. He'd priced a hundred mouths shut in eleven towns and no one had ever once asked after the debts.

He turned the staff a quarter-turn in his hands, a habit he reserved for doors that argued back.

"They default," he said. "The seams collect. It eats through."

"Then the verses have to outlive the voice that owes them." She said it fast, not gloating — filing, the way Tillo had, but sharper. "That's a liturgy. A rite is the only debt you can hand to the living. It survives the man; that's what rites are for; every dead altar in this city used to know it. I've swept them. I've kept this one, unpaid, for six years, because I had nothing else to hand my faith to, and the Door has never once answered me, and today something finally did, and it wasn't the Door." Her eyes stayed on his. "It was a man stating the cost out loud."

"You're volunteering to inherit my debts," Drevik said. "Most people who approach me want a blessing or a refund."

"Most people watched you talk the sky shut and decided you were a fraud, because a fraud is cheaper. I heard the chord and I heard the price and I did the math. You need someone to build the rite that outlives you. I know every dead observance this Door ever kept. I can raise them. Give me the terms."

There it was, whole and immediate, faith arriving like weather — no courtship, no coyness, belief assembled in an afternoon and handed over like a signed contract. His archive, the ledger he kept instead of paper, noted the white strip in her eyebrow, the gray knuckles, the broom, six years unpaid, and stamped the entry dangerous out of long habit, because anything that came to him already believing was either a blade or a gift, and the trick of his life was that they were usually both.

"Terms," Drevik said. "I say the costs out loud, always, including this one: believe me, and one day you'll watch me hold a door I can't shut, and you'll carry that. The work is ninth hours and cold years and no answers from anyone up high, forever, with me instead of a god, and gods at least have the decency to stay distant. You'd be founding something, not joining it. There's nothing to join yet. There's me, a bell-ringer, a candlemaker, and eleven coppers."

"Nine," she said. "And a button."

He laughed — the first honest laugh the square had got out of him all day — and she watched the mask do it and didn't soften.

"Your name," he said.

"Seraphel. Seraphel Vaunt."

Cassius, he thought, had done his math on the fountain steps and priced him in coppers. The woman at the empty altar had done hers in something Drevik had never once been paid in.

He filed her where he filed the verses owed — the only ledger he kept, the one no thief had ever thought to steal, since it looked like nothing, since it was only a man remembering — and the entry went in beside the debt he owed the sky.

Same time next year, he'd told the seam, and he'd meant it.

Ninth hour, Seraphel Vaunt had told him, and he suspected — the way he suspected all real doors — that this one, once opened, was not going to stay shut.
