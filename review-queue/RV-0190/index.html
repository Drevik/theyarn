There were four locks in the Fold, and by her ninth night Mira had recut three of them.

The garden gate came first. Its lock had grown with the wall, whole and pointless, keyed for a key that had never been cut, guarding rain barrels and the winter kale. A lock with no key was a lie told to a door. She filed the wards to a pattern of her own and oiled the bolt until it stopped grinding, and the kale stood in its bed and appreciated nothing.

The pantry came second. Flour had got into the wards and set there, and Halla's key had to be argued with every morning. She cleaned the flour out, re-seated the wards, and put the key back on the apron string where it lived. Halla paid her in pickled eggs and did not raise the subject again.

The library case came third. The case held the lore that was not for reading after dark.

"It bites," Drevik had said, and left her to it.

She oiled the case lock and let the book keep its teeth.

The fourth lock she had not touched.

It stood in the east corridor, at the end of it, in a door the color of cooled tea. The corridor ran four doors: Sera's, open a hand's width, the smell of the censer living in the wood; Wren's, curtained in cloth she had hemmed herself; Vesper's, shut, the hall around it still carrying blade oil though Vesper had been gone nine days. And then the last door. Its hinges were founder-work. She had seen their like only once before, on Sera's door, and Sera's door had been in the house since the house began.

Nobody had a key. In her twelve nights, nobody had needed to say whose it was, because Drevik had said it the night she came.

"The last room is yours."

"I didn't sign for it."

"No," he had said. That had been the whole of the transaction.

She had laid a hand flat on the door once, the fifth night, the way you test a lock for heat. There was no heat. She had not done it again.

She had cleaned every key on her ring twice instead. Thirty-one keys. The long key to Vault Nine, a lock she had opened once and sealed twice. A key to a door in the Bureau's east wing that had been scrapped the year she cut the copy, and she kept the key, because you keep keys; that was the whole of the trade. The master key to the forms room. The master key to the vaults, which they had taken back when she left, in a tray with numbered slots, the tray being the entire ceremony of her going.

The bad key rode the inside position, where the thumb sits. First cut, done at a school bench at twelve years old, off two thousandths at the third bit. It opened nothing. It had opened nothing for fifteen years. She kept it because a key is the second half of a promise somebody made, and this one was the first promise she had ever made herself.

She was not cleaning keys. She was not going down the east corridor. She went to the kitchen by the long way, through the garden, past the kale.

The household let her do it and worked around her the way a river works around a stone.

There was a bench cushion on the shelf beside the last door, wrapped in oilcloth. Wren had sewn it in the first week, for a bench she had never seen, inside a room nobody had entered, and she hummed while she sewed. The humming was the only argument anyone made.

"You could sit on it out here," Mira said.

"It's for the bench," Wren said, and bit her thread.

Halla left a plate on the library settle every night and took it away in the morning, barely touched, and the portions did not shrink. In a house that rationed everything, that was the sermon.

She found Sera at the last door on a firstday morning, cloth in one hand, working the lintel.

"It came the morning mine did," Sera said, without turning. "The house grew two rooms that day and asked nobody. One door opened. One didn't."

"Then it's a storeroom."

"It's kept better than a storeroom." Sera folded the cloth with the economy of someone who had folded ten thousand of them. "I kept an altar nine years in a town the god had emptied. I know how to keep a thing that doesn't answer."

"Why keep it at all."

"Everything that reaches him in this house reaches through me first." Sera set her palm flat on the lintel, briefly, the way you check a join. "A shut door reaches him. So it comes through me, and I keep it."

Mira looked down the corridor at the three doors that opened and the one that did not. "He opens every door in this house."

"He opens every door that's his." Sera hung the cloth on her belt. "That one he keeps no ledger on. For a man who counts everything, it's the most expensive thing he owns."

He came up the corridor that evening while she stood at the end window. The lamps came up ahead of him one by one and let themselves down behind, and he carried the staff unlit, the way a man carries a stick he likes the weight of. The window held the Fold's own night, coin-struck and slow. She had counted the pattern twice and it came up short both times.

"There's a coin missing," she said.

"Spent."

"On what?"

"Holding."

He stood beside her long enough that the window showed her the mask and the drifting crystals in the mitre and none of what was behind them, and then he moved on. He went past the last door at the same pace he went past all doors. She had watched him do it three nights running, waiting for him to slow.

He did not slow. That was the gift.

"Wren walked past hers for a month," he said, from the corridor's turn. "There's no prize for speed."

On the thirteenth night the library settle would not let her sleep. It had begun to feel like a form in suspense, and a form in suspense was the one thing the Bureau had ever taught her to hate. She put her boots on.

Sera was awake, and the censer was lit.

"He told me it would be tonight," Sera said. "He's wrong twice a year. I didn't think this was one."

They went down the east corridor together, the smoke going ahead of them in a slow rope. At the last door Sera swung the censer once across the threshold and put out her free hand, palm up, for nothing. A formality. She had nine years of formalities behind her and they sat on her like a vestment sits on someone born to it.

"Cost, out loud, before the bolt moves." Her voice had gone into its church register, and the church register in this house was one size and plain. "The room will know you. What you keep in it, the house keeps with you, as long as the house stands. There's no other charge."

"That's the whole toll."

"That's the whole toll."

Mira knelt and took off the ring.

She tried the keys in order, oldest to newest. Not because she believed. Because order was the trade's one liturgy, and she was done pretending she hadn't kept it. Thirty keys went into the lock and came out again without turning, some with the small apology of a key that nearly fits, most without comment. The lock waited. It had been waiting years. It was very good at it.

The last key was the bad one. First cut. Off two thousandths at the third bit.

The wards took it in four soft sets. One. Two. Three. Four. The bolt went back with the sound of a word finished after years of being half said.

Warm wood. Linseed. Her bench height.

The room held a workbench the height of her forearm, which was the only measuring stick she had ever trusted, and a vice set at her grip width. The drawers were felt-lined and cut for her kit. She had never shown her inventory to anyone. There was a window with the garden's late light in it, and a bed in the corner where she would have put one, and by the door a single hook, cut for a weight she knew.

Not her rank. Not her trade. Her first cut.

She hung the ring on the hook and the hook took the weight it had been cut for, all thirty-one keys, the dead locks and the living ones and the bad key at the thumb.

She stood in the middle of the room with her hands empty and let the house know her. It was not worse than she had feared. It was not nothing either. It was like being read by a form that had a place for everything, including the places she had never admitted to — and no, she thought, dropping that comparison with the care she dropped bad keys. The Bureau took. This house kept. The toll was said out loud, and the toll was only what it said.

"I'll leave the censer in the hall tonight," Sera said from the threshold, not crossing it. "The lintel's yours now."

In the morning there was a new lamp bracketed to the wall beside her door, and nobody had lit it, and it was lit.

At breakfast Wren carried the oilcloth into the last room and came back with the cushion on her arm and the cloth folded, and said nothing, and hummed.

Drevik found her in the kitchen at the dead hour of the afternoon. He poured the tea and put the pot back into her hands instead of on the table.

"Your hands are cold," he said. It was not a complaint. She held the pot until her hands were warm, and gave it back, and he set it on the hob where the kitchen could keep it.

"Does the bench fit?" he said. He knew the room down to the grain of the wood. He asked anyway.

"It's a hair low."

"The house will grow it."

"Wood doesn't work like that."

"No," he said, and drank his tea. "This wood does."

Then he said, "You told me once that a lock is a promise somebody made, and the key is the other half of it. You said it in a vault doorway with the wards still humming, and you meant it as an insult."

"I remember what I meant."

"I keep what's said to me." He said it plainly, the way he said prices. "It's a smaller archive than people fear. It only holds what people mean."

He did not ask which key had turned the lock. He knew, the way he knew the bench height: nobody had told him. She would have told him. She was almost sorry he didn't ask, and then she was not sorry, because not being asked and being known were the same gift wearing two coats.

At the long table that evening, Halla asked him about the winter stores, whether they should run to a new mouth.

The answer came a half-breath late. Correct, and late. The star-shapes at the centers of his eyes had gone off their centers, drifting the way the crystals drifted in the mitre. Sera put two fingers on his sleeve without pausing in her own plate. The stars came home. He finished the sentence he had begun, and laughed at Wren's joke after the table's laugh had finished, and the laugh was the right laugh, and it was late.

Mira marked the timing, because marking timing was her trade. A late set in a lock is wear. You don't scrap a worn lock. You watch it, and you keep it oiled, and you say nothing, because a lock that knows it is watched starts to lie.

She wrote it down nowhere. She kept it anyway.

Then the bell over the Threshold rang, once. It was a small bell and it had not rung in thirteen nights. The kettle quieted on the hob. Drevik set his cup down and stood, and nobody had asked anything, and the whole table stood with him.

"A man at the Threshold," he said. "Bureau gray on his coat. He asks for the keeper of the vaults."

Nobody looked at her. That was its own kind of looking.

Sera took the censer down from its peg on the way to the hall. Of course she did. Drevik walked ahead of all of them, and the house attended him down its own length, the lamps rising at his passage, and he filled the Threshold door with his shoulders and did not step out of its frame. The mask took the lamplight. The tall door was the one door, and its grain had old smoke in it, and he opened it himself, on his own count.

Corin Vey stood in the doorway with wet on his shoulders and nothing in his hands.

She had known him eleven years and had never once seen his hands empty. He taught wards at a school bench when she was twelve, and his countersignature sat under every form she had ever filed, and he had a rule about names, and the rule was that the Bureau had employees, not people. The tray that took her master key back had a slot with her number on it, and the number had been his numbering.

He looked past Drevik's shoulder, found her in the hall, and said, "Mira."

The Bureau had never once, in eleven years, used her name.

Her keys hung in the last room. Her hands were empty.
