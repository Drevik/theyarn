The blade in Vesper’s hand was not a weapon, but a tool of measurement. She sat at the small workbench in the armory, the light from the Fold’s hearth-glow catching the edge of the whetstone with a cold, silvered precision. She ran the steel along the grit, a steady, rhythmic scrape that filled the room, carving out a small, private space of order amidst the encroaching chaos. Scrape. Pull. Scrape. Pull.

The sound was the only thing keeping the silence from becoming a weight.

The armory itself felt smaller than it had a week ago, the stone walls pressing inward as if the very architecture of the Fold were shrinking under the pressure of the void. The air smelled of oil, old iron, and the faint, metallic tang of ozone that always lingered near the Threshold. It was a scent of preparation, of a world braced for impact.

The door to the armory did not creak; it simply ceased to and was no longer closed. Drevik entered. He did not bring the biting, unnatural cold of the void with him, though he had been stationed at the Threshold for hours, standing where the light of existence met the hunger of the nothingness. He brought only the presence of a man who had just finished a long, heavy liturgy. His black and deep-purple vestments were stiff with the salt of effort, the gold celestial embroidery dull under a fine dusting of ash—the grey remains of things that had been unmade.

He did not speak. He moved to the corner where his void staff leaned, and he took it. He began to wipe the staff with a cloth, his movements slow and methodical, his focus entirely on the task of cleansing.

Vesper did not look up. She knew the weight of his attention. Even when he was not looking at her, he was part of the room's gravity. The air around him felt thicker, more purposeful, as if his very breathing helped hold the seams of the Fold together. When Drevik was in a room, the concept of drift became harder to maintain; he anchored the space.

"The Unmaking Choir is pressing harder on the/western gate," he said. His voice was plain. It held no tremor, no fear, only the flat, terrifying statement of a fact.

"I heard the bells," Vesper said. She kept her eyes on the steel. She focused on the microscopic way the edge caught the light. "The resonance was off. A dissonant chord. It lacked the harmonic stability of the third watch."

"It is a heavy chord," Drevik replied. He set the cloth down. He walked to the table, his boots silent on the stone, and stood just far enough from her that she could not feel the heat of him, but close enough that the light of his crystal mitre cast a long, shifting shadow over her work.

The crystals in his mitre drifted like slow embers in a dark well. They caught the light of her whetstone and threw fractured, pale shapes against the wall—ghostly geometries that danced in the periphery of her vision.

Vesper paused. The steel was cold against her thumb, a biting reminder of the lethality she was refining. "The bells are not the only thing that is dissonant, Drevik."

He did not ask what she meant. He never did. He simply waited, a pillar of dark silk and silver hair, letting the silence do its work. He was the archive; he knew how to listen to what was not said, to the tremors in the subtext of a breath.

"Corin was at the perimeter," she said. The words felt like small, sharp stones. She dropped them onto the table between them, letting them settle into the space where the light met the shadow. "I saw him. He was not patrolling the line. He was watching the seam. He was watching the way the light leaks through, the way the darkness breaths against the barrier."

Drevik continued to wipe the staff. "Corlin is a loyal man. He follows the Blazon's code with a devotion that few can claim."

"The code is a set of lines on a page, Drevik," Vesper said. She finally looked up. From beneath the edge of her dark hair, she watched his face. The white-ceramic kitsune mask caught the low light, the gold accents gleaming like predatory eyes. He did not look like a man. He looked like a monument to a god that had forgotten how to die. "Lines can be redrawn. A man can follow the letter of the law while he waits for the ink to dry. He can perform every duty, every ritual, while he waits for the moment when the ritual no longer matters."

Drevik stopped moving. He leaned the staff against the bench. He looked at her, and for a moment, the star-taped pupils in his void eyes seemed to pulse, a faint, rhythmic light that matched the beating of her own pulse. He did not reach for her. He did not offer the comfort of a comrade. He only observed, dissecting her confession with the clinical detachment of a scholar.

"You suspected him," he said. It was not a question.

Vesper felt the truth of it ache in her throat. It was a bitter thing, like swallowed ash. "I suspected him a month ago. When the supply counts for the outer wards were found short. Not enough to trigger an alarm, but enough to notice if you were counting the grains. When the seals on the secondary caches showed signs of tampering—not from the outside, a breach of force, but from a key that matched the officers' registry. A key that was clean. No struggle. No theft of the mechanism, only the theft of the contents."

She went back to the stone. Scrape. Pull.

"I did not say anything," she said. Her voice was a low, steady thing, a blade being honed. "I thought... I thought if I gathered enough weight, I could crush the doubt myself. I thought if I tracked the discrepancies, I could present you with a closed circle. I thought I could find the proof without involving the Hearth. I didn't want to cast suspicion on the Blazon's officers if I was merely chasing shadows."

"And did you?" Drevik asked.

"I found the pattern," Vesper said. She looked at the blade. It was bright, clean, and lethal. "The pattern is not a single theft, Drevik. It is not a sudden greed. It is a slow erosion. Someone is thinning the defenses. Removing the redundancies. Cutting the safety lines. It is not done to let the Unmaking in—not yet. It is done to make the Threshold easier to break once the breach is finally made. They are preparing the ground for the harvest."

The room grew still. The fire in the hearth popped, a single spark rising and vanishing into the dark.

Drevik reached out. He did not touch her skin, but his hand hovered near the edge of her workbench, near the blade. His fingers were long, steady, and scarred by a thousand bindings. He moved as if he were navigating a complex piece of architecture, careful not to disturb the balance of the room.

"To betray the Hearth is to betray the self," he said. He spoke the words like a vow, or a funeral rite. "There is no way to hollow out the Fold without the walls falling upon the one who dug the hole. If Corin is the architect of this erosion, he is merely building his own tomb."

"He isn't building a tomb," Vesper whispered, her voice cracking for the briefest of moments. "He's building a doorway."

Drevik pulled his hand back and straightened. He looked toward the door, toward the infinite dark of the void that pressed against the boundaries of their home. He looked like a man who was already calculating the cost of the next repair, weighing the lives of the many against the integrity of the few.

"I will watch the perimeter," Drevik said. His tone had returned to its habitual neutrality. "You will watch the blades."

"The blades are ready," Vesper said.

He turned to leave. As he passed the threshold of the armory, his silhouette eclipsed the light from the hall. For a moment, the room felt hollowed out, as if he had taken the very concept of safety with him when he stepped away.

Vesper picked up the whetstone. She had a task. She had a duty. She had a heavy, cold weight in her chest that no amount of sharpening could remove.

She did not know that the pattern was already complete. She did not know that the erosion had already reached the foundation, that the very stone she sat upon was being undermined by a hand she trusted.

She only knew the steel, the stone, and the silence that followed him into the dark.

The armory was quiet, save for the rhythmic, lonely sound of the stone against the metal. Scrape. Pull. Scrape. Pull.

Then, from the deeper recesses of the Fold, far beyond the armory, came a new sound. It was not the chime of a bell, nor the heavy footfall of a guard. It was a soft, wet sliding sound, like something heavy being dragged across silk. Then, the sound of a door clicking shut.

It was a small sound, a common sound, the mundane noise of a household at rest. But in the sudden, vacuum-like silence that followed, it sounded like a seal breaking. It sounded like the start of an inversion.
