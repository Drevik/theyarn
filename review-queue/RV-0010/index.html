Core idea

The avoidant attachment style is characterized by a fundamental drive to maintain autonomy and a deep-seated belief that closeness is a threat to personal freedom. You likely view yourself as a self-reliant, competent "lonesome traveler" who handles life's challenges without needing others. However, this independence is often a defensive posture used to prevent the vulnerability that true intimacy requires. To protect your independence, you unconsciously employ strategies to keep partners at a distance, creating a state of being "together but apart" that prevents genuine connection.

Key concepts and terms

  • Avoidant Attachment: A style defined by a tendency to minimize the importance of intimacy and a high valuation of self-reliance, often used to suppress attachment anxiety.
  • Deactivating Strategies: Unconscious psychological maneuvers used to create distance and suppress attachment needs. These include scanning for flaws, focusing on work, or withdrawing when a partner gets too close.
  • The Phantom Ex: A mental construct where you remain emotionally tethered to a past relationship or a "perfect" former lover. This allows you to feel the "idea" of love without the actual vulnerability of a present partner.
  • The Fantasy Soulmate: The pursuit of an idealized, "perfect" person who possesses no flaws. This serves as a barrier to accepting a real, flawed human being in the present.
  • Pre-emptive Defense: The act of withdrawing or creating distance before a partner has the opportunity to reject or overwhelm you.
  • Self-Reliance (as a defense): The habit of suppressing needs for support to avoid the "cost" of dependency.

Techniques worth drilling

  1. Interrupting Deactivating Strategies
  • Step 1: Monitor your internal monologue for "flaw-finding" (e.g., "They talk too much," "They are too needy").
  • Step 2: When you catch yourself nitpicking a partner's personality, pause and ask: "Am I seeing a real flaw, or am I trying to create distance because things are getting serious?"
  • Step 3: Consciously choose not to act on the impulse to withdraw or criticize.
  1. Shifting from Self-Reliance to Mutual Support
  • Step 1: Identify a low-stakes task or emotional burden you currently handle entirely alone.
  • Step 2: Deliberately invite your partner to assist or simply to be present while you handle it.
  • Step 3: Practice verbalizing a need, however small (e.g., "I'm feeling stressed; can we just sit together for ten minutes?").
  1. Retiring the Idealized Past/Future
  • Step 1: Identify when you are comparing your current partner to an "ex" or a "dream person."
  • Step 2: List three concrete, present-day strengths of your current partner that have nothing to do with your fantasies.
  • Step 3: Re-center your focus on the tangible reality of the person in front of you.
  1. Using Shared Activity as a Bridge
  • Step 1: Choose an activity that requires parallel focus (e.g., cooking, hiking, playing a game) rather than direct, intense eye contact or heavy conversation.
  • Step 2: Use the "side-by-side" nature of the task to slowly lower your guard.
  • Step 3: Allow small moments of vulnerability to emerge naturally through the shared task.

Practice drill

The Seven-Day Deactivation Log

For the next seven days, you will select two actions from the "Techniques worth drilling" list above to run concurrently. Every time you notice a "deactivating move"—such as a sudden surge of irritation at a partner's flaw, a sudden craving for solitude immediately following a moment of closeness, or an impulse to check out mentally—you must record it in a log.

At the end of each day, review your entries. For every instance of deactivation, write down:

  1. The Impulse: What was the specific thought or feeling? (e.g., "I felt suffocated when they asked about my weekend.")
  2. The Story: What was the narrative you attached to it? (e.g., "I felt like I was losing my autonomy and my weekend was being 'invaded'.")
  3. The Pivot: What did you do instead of withdrawing? (e.g., "I stayed in the room and shared one small detail about my weekend instead of making an excuse to leave.")

The goal is not to eliminate the impulse—which is a deeply ingrained habit—but to build the muscle of recognizing it and choosing a different response.

Critiques and caveats

  • The Risk of "Forced Intimacy": If you are working with a partner who is Anxious, pushing yourself too hard into "closeness" without gradual steps can trigger an even stronger deactivating response. Progress must be incremental.
  • The Partner's Role is Critical: Change is significantly more difficult if your partner uses "protest behavior" (clinging, anger, or manipulation) in response to your distance. The book notes that avoidants change best when paired with a Secure partner who does not retaliate for the distance.
  • Avoidance of Self-Gaslighting: While you must question your negative readings of your partner, do not use this drill to ignore genuine compatibility issues or toxic behaviors. The focus is on distinguishing between attachment-based criticism and character-based incompatibility.

Reading notes

Chapter 6 serves as the essential "counterpoint" to the Anxious attachment profile discussed in previous chapters. While the book moves from the volatility of the Anxious-Avoidant trap toward the stability of Secure attachment, this chapter provides the specific toolkit for identifying the subtle, often invisible, walls that prevent the Secure connection from ever forming.
