The last ward I cut was the eleventh. I saved it for last because the liability clause is the one that has to be perfect, and because a tired hand cuts honest. Eleven hours at the bench, and the void-glass took the engraving clean, letter by letter, and I checked the depth of every cut against my memory of the wording twice, and then a third time, because this lock had one chance to be what I meant it to be.

The Fold had grown me the workshop the week I arrived with no intention of staying. A room off the kitchen passage: stone bench, one lamp, a wall of pegs for my key ring, a door that locks from the inside only. That last I never asked for. The Fold learned it off me, the way the house learns everything off us, and I forgave it.

The eleven wards rode in an oil-cloth Wren had sewn herself, hemmed with silk she said came off a drowned stall in the Bazaar. They rode without one chime. Glass that doesn't chime is a courtesy in a house of women who sleep light. On the third peg hung a canvas apron, waxed at the hem, cut to my measure. Halla hung it there my first week, before I had decided anything. I had planned to resent it on principle for a year at least. It kept my lapels clean.

Vesper had told me once that what I build are duels-of-law with the fighting already finished. She meant it as praise. It is the only kind she issues.

Sera came in the morning with a stoppered vial and stood at the edge of the lamp's light like a guest. In her own chapel she is not a guest. In my shop she made herself one, and from Sera that is a courtesy with weight behind it.

"Salted oil," she said. "One grain of the censer's ash in with it. The ash isn't holy. It's habit. Doors seat quieter for the rite's habits the way hinges take oil." She set the vial on the bench before I asked. "I don't bless locks. I blessed the oil. It goes in at the oil."

She looked over the wards laid in their order, and she looked longest at the eleventh.

"When the door stands open," she said, "he can answer no questions and see no faces. Someone stands at his right hand, hands him the wards in order, and asks him nothing. That was my place. It's yours now. It has been yours for weeks; I have only just stopped carrying it."

"I know how to hand a man a tool," I said.

"A tool, yes," she said. "He is not one. That's the whole of the instruction."

She went out past the pegs, and her finger touched the apron's hem on the way, the way you check a stitch you already trust.

Drevik came at dusk with the staff left leaning in the kitchen passage, which meant he was not here on the Communion's business. The lamp did not gutter when he entered. Nothing in the Fold is afraid of him. I put my graver down anyway. That is what he does to a bench.

He took the corner opposite me and gave me the Charter from memory, at dictation pace, clause by clause, and I checked every engraving against his mouth. That was the accounting of our winter, if anyone wants it read out: his memory against mine, and neither of us blinking.

He stopped at the eleventh. "Their clerks read a comma after answers," he said. "Their assessors read it without. The comma is the whole war, and both sides believe they're winning it."

"I know. I filed the complaint about it the year before I left. It came back stamped received."

"Which reading did you cut?"

"Theirs. Without. A lock owes its owners their own honesty, no more. Without the comma, the clause closes: the Bureau answers for what it holds, and holds what it answers for. In a clerk's mouth that's a circle. In steel it's a ratchet. It turns one way, and every turning sets it deeper."

The crystals in the mitre had stopped their drifting. I have learned that is him deciding.

"Cut me a second eleventh," he said. "With the comma in. Keep it in the apron pocket. If they ever amend their Charter honest, I want us re-seated within the hour, and I want them never to know we were ready to be fair to them."

That is the kind of man he is. He keeps a spare forgiveness, oiled.

I told him the rest of it while I cut the spare. Every instrument of the Bureau carries three hands: the assessor's, the Signet's, and the Lockmistress's. They never replaced me. I had checked through a steward who knows a clerk in their registry. For three years the third line on their writs has sat empty, or wrong, or borrowed. Writs don't expire at the Bureau. They wait.

The lock takes two truths and no keys. My memory cut the wards. His recitation seats them. Neither of us can service it alone; I insisted on that before I agreed to build it. A lock with one keeper is a door with one hinge, and I do not make those. Keyed to their Charter, it is a lock only they can ask to open — and what it opens is them.

"The rite opens the door," he said. "It stands open for the time of eleven clauses. That is the cost. There is no smaller one, and I won't pretend at one. Say it back."

"The door stands open for the time of eleven clauses," I said.

"And you will not be quick," he said. "Quick is the enemy of exact. You'll be exact."

"I am always exact."

"Yes," he said, and the smirk arrived, and he was gone past the pegs before I had found the answer I wanted.

The chapel at night is one candle and Sera's hymn and the door behind the altar, which is plain painted wood, and which is the seam of the world. I have mostly stopped finding that funny.

He had sent the Devoted home. The chapel held five of us and one open seam: Vesper at the foot of the aisle with her blade unbelted in her hand, watching the dark past the door the way you watch an opponent you have already measured. Wren at the seam's edge with the cloth in both hands, humming one low note; as long as the note held, the press held. Sera at the altar with the censer going. Me at my bench with the wards in order. Him at the door.

He set his shoulder to the wood and the staff across the jamb, the hymn went up a third, and the door came open on the cold between worlds, and the cold came off it in a steady pull, like a draft down a deep stair.

Under the hymn there was a hum with no key in it. That is them, the Sundered, the ones that press. It has no key because the place it comes from has none. The hymn has one. Sera kept it and did not share.

I oiled the first seat with the salted oil and handed him the first ward, and he pressed it into the strike and said its clause into the glass, and the glass took the words the way wax takes a seal, and the bell above the altar counted once. Jurisdiction. Two, definitions. Three, the signet and its forms. Four, the marshals and their bounds. Five, inventory. Six, liability of inventory. I handed and he pressed and the bell counted, and the cold pulled, and nobody hurried, because hurrying is how hands get hurt at a seam.

On the ninth clause his voice went flat. The words were exact; I will swear that by my trade until they melt the bench out from under me. But the man behind the words came thinner, like a chord with the third gone out of it.

Sera crossed the altar at her own pace and hooked the censer's chain once around his fingers. He closed his hand on it. The tenth clause came back with the clerk's dryness in it. He looked at me across the bench and it was him looking. That is all I can say about it, and it is all I want to.

The eleventh seated at the bell's last count, and the click ran the length of the door, and Sera let the hymn down, and he shut the Threshold like a man shutting a good gate: full weight, no hurry, home. Then he thanked the bell by its name, and the bell, being a bell, said nothing back, and he laughed at his own joke alone, and we let him.

Halla fed us after at the long table, in the Fold's standing late afternoon. She served me first, which is her order of precedence: whoever stood nearest the door eats first. I had planned to argue the point on principle. The bread was warm and salted right, and I ate.

Drevik took one piece and salted it twice and tasted neither salt. Sera watched his hand, not his face. I watched Sera watch. Nobody said a word, and nobody was fooled, and the bread was warm, and I held my peace with both hands like everyone else.

"Seconds," Halla said, and set mine down before I answered, which is the matriarch's whole grammar: never ask a tired woman whether she wants more. Decide.

My workshop door has a bolt I cut myself. It cannot be picked by anyone who is not me, and he did not pick it. He knocked. That was the first strange thing.

The staff was still in the kitchen passage. He had the mask on and the mitre on and every fastening of the vestments closed to the throat, which is his way of arriving nowhere in particular.

"The lock is seated," he said.

"It is."

"I came to check the settling."

"The settling doesn't want checking for a month."

"I'm not checking the lock," he said.

He stops joking at thresholds, so there was no joke after that. His sentences go short at doors, shorter at altars, shortest at bodies, and we were standing at all three.

The vestments open at the nape, four hooks and a stud under the collar. The fourth hook turns a quarter left before it lifts. He had been fighting it straight-on since before I knew him, and I had known about the turn for nine days and said nothing, because I was raised by a Bureau and I hoard my tolerances. I said it now.

He turned the hook a quarter left and it lifted, and he laughed once, low, with his whole chest, and I decided I was done being a woman who withholds fastenings from a man who says his costs out loud.

The mitre went on the third peg over the apron, and the crystals banked as it left him, all at once, lamps going down in a house going to bed. The mask came off last. He set it face-down among my files, where nothing in the Fold touches anything, and left it there among the gravers, and I let it stay. That was the whole of the asking, both directions.

He asked with his hands. I answered with mine. Neither of us asked twice.

He is broad in the shoulders and heavy in the hand and careful with the weight of himself, and I told him I was not a tray of cups, and he said, "Noted," and set the weight down anyway, and I held it. Holding is my trade. It went to my head how well the two of us fit on the first try, no forcing, his breath along my jaw, the bench cold under me and his back not cold at all. The kitchen clock through the wall kept Fold time, which is nobody's time, and counted for us anyway.

I will not write down what he is like. The Bureau files everything and knows nothing. I know, and I file nothing, and that is my loyalty now, and I learned it off him.

He slept on the bench with his cheek on Halla's apron and his arm under my key ring so it wouldn't ring. I trued the eleventh ward's seat a second time, because my hands wanted work while I looked at his face doing nothing at all. It is the finest mechanism in the Fold. I did not say so. Some tolerances you keep.

In the morning the key ring by the door held one key more than I had cut. New brass, warm-keyed, cut by no hand I know. In the east wall of the workshop stood a door that had not stood there: hung, painted, sill swept. It opens from my side. I have not opened it yet. Neither of us mentioned it at breakfast.

One night in the Fold is three days out in a hard season. The Bureau used all three.

We were at the toast when the crystals in his mitre stopped drifting. He set his cup down without a sound and stood, and the mask was on before he was upright, which is its own kind of sentence.

"They're early," he said. "Bring the apron. It's cold in the chapel."

Sera was already reaching for the censer. Vesper had the blades. Wren had the cloth. Halla put bread in the apron pocket without asking, for after, because there is always an after and it is always hungry.

The morning office was at the third hymn when we came in, and the Devoted held it like a roof holds a house, and then the west doors opened and the hymn held anyway, because Sera's rule is that the hymn never stops for paper.

Grey coats came up the aisle. Grey gloves. A writ-case chained to the wrist of an assessor I did not know. And behind him, third from the left, walking like a man in his own house, the one who taught my hands everything a pen can do. Master Corvel keeps the Bureau's seal the way Sera keeps the hymn. I sat at that man's bench six years. He called my hand the best forgery-proof in the Bureau. I believed him. I was the proof.

At the altar step the assessor unchained the case and read the title aloud, unhurried, the way paper moves when the men carrying it know they are lawful. "By order of the Aegis Bureau, under its founding Charter: for the conveyance of custody, liability, and inventory of the asset registered as Iron-Nine, to the society styled the Veiled Communion—"

I was already reading the foot of the page upside down, because they trained me and the training stays. The third line. The countersignature. Three years empty, or wrong, or borrowed.

The hand was mine. The date was wrong. The wax was theirs.

Drevik had come in behind us with the staff unlit, and he stood at the pew-end watching weather come up a valley, and that is him at his most dangerous, and I have decided to find it steadying rather than frightening, which I suppose is what joining this household means.

I had cut the lock to read the Bureau's paper and refuse every lie in it. I had not cut it to refuse my own hand. The wards would match. The pins would sit true. That was the trouble.

I put my thumb on the seam of the door and checked the seating one more time, which for me is the same as praying. The lock was true. My hand was on their paper, and their paper was coming up the aisle to my door.

Corvel reached the altar and took off one grey glove, finger by finger, the way you do when you are about to handle something you have already signed.
