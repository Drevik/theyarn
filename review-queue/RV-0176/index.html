The east door took forty-one pins, and I was re-cutting every one of them by lamplight, because a drawing of them was loose in the world.

That was the arithmetic. I had surveyed the doors of the Fold at the thaw, for the maintenance and for the love of the work. It was harvest now, and three nights ago Vesper had taken a courier at the toll-house on the Argent road with my seams folded in his boot. So every lock in the house had to come off its door and go back on new. There was no part of the work I minded. I minded the hand that had assigned it.

The summons came up at moonrise, one pull on the table-bell. Wren's humming had gone out of the house two days before, three rooms off, and I had heard it go. The duty steward had taken to bowing to me from one door further away than his habit. People keep their distance in the shape of more courtesy. I had read that bow for six years in the Bureau's halls. I had come here to retire from it.

I racked the files, wiped the pin-oil from my hands, and went down.

The long table had been cleared to its wood. In the middle, on a grey cloth, lay the map.

Not my map. I looked for the lie the way you look for the fault in a lock: not at the wards, at the keyway. The lie was neat. A good copy, drawn in Bureau notation, in the standard hand, tolerances ruled into the margins the way I rule them, and the east seam put wrong two fingers south, the way I had put it wrong on purpose. Beneath it lay a disbursement chit, black-waxed, drawn on the Third Vault. Thirty marks, fee for carriage. Countersigned under a key-matrix. My matrix, pressed into wax I had never softened over any flame of mine.

Vesper sat with her hands flat on the wood, blade-side down, the way she sits when she is holding a court in her head. Wren sat with the corner of the copy turned toward her and her needle in her fist, though there was nothing in that room to mend. Sera stood. Sera stands when a thing is being weighed; I had learned that at evening hymn, watching her weigh the incense in her palm.

The chair they had left open was the one nearest the door. I did not sit in it.

"Three facts," Vesper said. "I owe you their order. First, the copy is in your hand. Second, the chit carries your matrix. Third, the courier took his packet at our shrine-door on the Argent road at the evening hymn, and it was warm off the press when I emptied his boot. That is the case. I lay it flat because I do not think you did it, and because that has nothing to do with whether it is a case."

"The hand is Bureau standard," I said. "Sixty wardens alive draw in it. I can draw you the hand of every one from memory, and the list will prove nothing except that the Bureau teaches its people to be identical and then cannot tell them apart."

I set my signet case on the cloth and opened it, because a thing on the table is worth ten in the pocket. "The matrix. They cast my seal at my commissioning and kept the mold. The Matron told me so herself while I cut my first blank under her eye. We keep our molds, little key, she said. Nothing leaves the Bureau until the Bureau can make it again. I was twenty. I laughed, because I thought she was talking about keys." I turned the signet once under the lamp. "And here is the joke's other face. I never filed my release. The release is filed in person, before the Matron, and I did not go back in person. In the Bureau's books I am seconded, not gone. Their chit is in order. By their law, that countersign is mine."

Nobody reached for the case.

"The courier," I said. "Ask him the wax. Ask him the hand that filled his palm. He will tell you black wax and a hood, because blind couriers are paid to stay blind, and whoever chose him wanted a blind hour to go with the blind man."

"The warmth is the fact," Vesper said. "Warm means pressed that morning. The Argent road is two days from any door of ours."

"Pressed from what they hold," I said. "Copies of copies. The Bureau has had something of mine since before harvest, and they print what they hold when they need it. That is what bureaus are."

Then Wren turned the copy over and tilted her needle to the light. "Pricked," she said. "Someone laid this against the original and carried the lines through with needle and pounce. It is pattern-work. It is how you move a drawn ground onto cloth for stitching; it is how I moved the stars onto the processional." She measured a pinhole against her needle's width, then put the needle down on the grey cloth, where it lay like an answer nobody had asked for. "Curtain-weight. Like mine. It was traced off the original, Mira. At your board."

"Or you traced your own survey," Vesper said, "to dress the sale as a theft. If you are Bureau-trained, it is what I would coach you to do."

"Then search me." My voice held level; I was the only one in the room surprised by that. "Take the ring tonight. Turn my room. Inventory my blanks against my cuts and tell me which is missing. Ask me for every hour since the thaw and I will give it, and you will find no hour unspent, because the Bureau taught me to account, and the teaching never once set me down."

I put my hands flat on the table, the way Vesper had hers.

"Here is what I know about drawers. I kept files for six years. A drawer with a name in it never closes all the way. The Bureau taught me that at the Matron's knee, and I came here to unlearn it, and the first thing this house has put in a drawer is me." The chit lay with its wax up, my sign in it, pressed by whoever keeps the mold of me. "You want to know what it proves. It proves the Bureau holds my matrix. Under the sun it proves nothing else. But it is in your drawer now, and I know the size of that drawer better than any of you, and I know that being searched is how the Bureau says it cares."

Wren's needle stayed on the cloth. Nobody picked it up.

Then the door opened, and the light of his staff came in ahead of him and lay on the map first, so the map was the brightest thing in the room before he reached it. Vesper's hands left the table. Wren stood up. Sera turned to the door, and I had turned with them, all of us without a word, the way a congregation turns toward the gospel.

Ash from the Threshold walk was on his boots. He had not taken off the mask.

He did not sit either. He held the chit to the lamp, then the copy, reading the pinholes against the flame.

"The east seam is wrong," he said. "Deliberately?"

"Two fingers south. A stolen drawing with no fault in it gets trusted whole. A stolen drawing with one fault keeps the thief digging at a door that isn't there."

"Is the fault in the copy?"

"It is."

"Good," he said. "Then the Bureau paid thirty marks for a map to a door that does not exist, and they bought it in this house's own needlework. We will spend that. Before spring I want a door standing where they think the seam runs, and I want it holding something worth their digging." He set the copy down the way a man sets down another's tools. "The Bureau makes its people in molds. Molds carry no hands, and hands carry habits, and habits do not copy clean. Whoever traced this stood inside this house. So the house changes its hands."

"You would give the suspected wright the whole of it," Vesper said.

"I would give the wright the whole of it," he said, and turned to Sera. "First wife."

"She made the reliquary's lock at midsummer," Sera said. "When it was finished she cut the blank in two and gave me the halves. I keep them on the altar in the censer-dish. The Bureau keeps its molds. She breaks her blanks. I have watched her break three. Let her walk the doors."

"Then it is settled," he said. "Every lock in this house goes to her bench and comes off it new. She walks the doors with me, beginning at the north door tonight. At matins she reports the wards to me, at the morning office, and Sera holds the hour. And I will say the rest of the cost out loud, since it is mine to say. I priced this woman at the thaw. Not her hands; anyone can hire hands. I priced the risk of her, and I paid, and the payment is spent. Tonight the bill lands on the house. I do not buy back what I have already paid for."

"I will stand the watch," Vesper said. "By the Blazon's form. Surety. If she runs, hunt me with her."

"Give me the chit," I said. "Molds carry their tool-marks into the wax. I can read the impression against my signet to the tooth. If the Bureau pressed it, their kept mold left its signature in mine."

"And if you find no mold-marks?" Vesper said.

"Then somebody pressed my signet itself," I said, "and it does not leave my belt, and you may all go on wondering which of you it was."

That went where I aimed it. Vesper put the chit in my palm and did not hold on to it.

"North door at moonset," he said. "Bring your lamps." Then, to me, with the mask still on: "Eat first. I could hear your files from the shrine. You have been cutting since noon."

My workshop had been sealed while I sat at the table. I had not been back to my own door in two days; I had slept at the east door with the files racked beside me. The hasp on my door was fresh-mounted, the screws still holding their oil, and hanging from it was a ten-mark, four-pin, factory-sprung thing, the sort the Bureau buys by the crate. That was the insult, and it was exact. Not the sealing; any house may seal a door pending its hearing, and I would have ordered the same. The insult was the four pins. A lock on a locksmith's door should be good enough to embarrass her into respect.

Wren's needle had put a date on the wound, at the table: before harvest. Everyone I loved in this house had been in this house before harvest.

I stood in the corridor and did the old arithmetic before I could stop it, out of training. What do they have on you. What will they believe. Where does the file live. The Bureau drills that into its people, and I had carried mine out with the rest of my luggage. The worst of it was not the drawer with my name. The worst was the half-second at the table, before Vesper spoke, when I saw the grey cloth and knew whose hand the map would be in, and was ready. Six years of forms teach you to fill your own out in advance. There was no version of this house I had not already suspected of suspecting me. I had believed belonging here was a lease with an inspection clause, because everything I had ever been given had one. That was the fear, said flat: not that they suspected me. That I had been right, all along, about what houses are.

He came up the corridor with a lamp in one hand and a plate in the other: bread, salted butter, the heel of the wheel still warm from a kitchen that never cools. The ash on his boots was three days deep, and his warmth came in a step behind him, the way a stove comes back to heat after the draft is shut. It comes slower every season. I have never said so.

"It was sealed on Vesper's order, the night the chit came home," he said. "I countersigned it. I said you would not find it by yourself." He put the seal's key in my palm. "Yours now. Melt the four-pin down. It offends me more than it offends you."

"Ask me," I said.

"Ask you what?"

"The question. The one the whole table carried in its pockets."

"I don't carry it," he said. "I priced you at the thaw. Ask me the one I need answered. Can this house be re-keyed before spring?"

"By me, yes."

"Then that is the whole of the file I keep."

I have looked for his ledger. Out of training, the first month I was here, I went through his study like a professional. Nothing. He writes nothing down and remembers everything said to him, and I had sat at that table for an hour being read, and he had spent the hour reading the chit. There is no book his certainty lives in. It comes out of him without accounting, and I am a wright who trusts nothing she cannot take apart, and I have stopped trying to take him apart.

He took the mask off at my bench and set it faceup among the files, the gold holding the lamp. Then he was a man at my bench with ash on his boots, eating bread. The Devoted expect more under the mask. I have never told them otherwise.

The high collar has its clasp under the stiff rim at the back, where a man cannot reach without a mirror, and he never asks for help in front of anyone. I unhooked it for him. Fastening-work; my hands did not fumble, they had no cause to. He went on eating while I worked it loose, and thanked me the way you thank the one who holds the lamp, which is a wage I would not trade. On the bench the mitre's crystals had stopped their slow walking. I did not ask when. Some things you leave filed where they are.

I cut a key while he ate. A fresh blank, teeth cut to my judgment and to no door in this house, because no door in this house fit it yet. He watched the whole cut without a word, and when I filed the last tooth he said, "For which door?"

"The next one," I said. "This house grows rooms. It has never once asked my leave."

He turned the key over once and put it inside his collar, where the clasp had been. Nobody said the word for staying. The lamp burned down to its oil, and neither of us went for more.

The garden had changed by morning. In the west wall, between the fig and the bell-stand, a doorframe stood where no doorframe had been at supper. No door in it. No room behind it. The stone was warm the way the Fold's stone is warm on a day it has grown something.

Wren stood before it, humming, the first humming in the house in three days. Sera came with the smell of the office on her and said the frame was in the evening's arithmetic, which is liturgy and means what it pleases. Vesper rapped the stone with her knuckles and called it honest. Nobody said what all of us had counted by then: the house had grown a door the night I decided to stay.

I hung the door that afternoon. Oak on stone, iron strap, and the lock I had been cutting since the table. The new door took forty-one pins on purpose; every tooth was fresh, nothing reused. A number can be a vow, if you cut it into enough brass. Behind the frame there was no room yet, only the Fold's own dark, unhung and unlit, and I did not fear it. I have had my hands in this house's walls. That dark is a room's promise, not its absence.

I keyed the new door to my ring and cut one more key, and did not know yet whose it would be. I have built for bureaus. I know what a frame means. A frame means a room has been promised somewhere, by whatever draws the Fold's rooms, and when the room comes it will find the door hung, the lock pinned, and the wright on the inside.

I oiled the bolt, put on my apron, and went in to matins with the night's wards by heart. Nothing written. Nothing owed to any form. The Bureau keeps its molds. I break my blanks. I stayed.
