The hearth did not flicker. It held.

I kept the fire at a steady, low amber, the way a cook maintains a slow braise, nurturing the heat so it would not scorch but would never fail. In the Fold, the flame was not merely a source of warmth; it was the weight of our shared breath, the physical manifestation of our refusal to vanish. I stirred the embers with a heavy iron poker, the metal clinking rhythm and rhythm against the stone, my movements precise, my eyes fixed on the shifting architecture of the ash. To tend the hearth is to tend the boundary. If the ash grows cold, the seam thins. If the flame wavers, the fabric of our reality begins to fray.

The Nought pressed against the Threshold tonight. I could not see it, for the Nought is the absence of all things, a void so profound it lacks even the capacity for darkness. Instead, I felt the way the air in the kitchen grew thin and hollow, a sudden, sickening vacuum that made my lungs ache. It was a hunger that did not want to eat, but to erase—a predator that hunted by deleting its prey from the very memory of the world. The scent of the kitchen—the comforting, yeasty tang of rising dough, the sharp bite of dried rosemary, the earthy musk of woodsmoke—was being leached away, replaced by a sterile, terrifying nothingness.

I wiped my hands on my apron. The linen was coarse, familiar, and stained with the ghost of yesterday’s flour, a tactile anchor to the life I was trying to preserve. I looked toward the door that led to the Great Hall, the heavy oak portal that Drevik had carved from the void itself, a feat of architectural defiance. I waited for the sound of his boots, the heavy, rhythmic signal that the Threshold was still held, that the perimeter was secure.

Then came the sound. It was not a footfall. It was a vibration in the floorboards, a low, grinding note that set my teeth on edge and sent a shiver racing down my spine. It felt as if the very foundation of the house were being unmade, ground down into fine, meaningless dust.

I moved to the threshold of the kitchen, my heart hammering against my ribs like a trapped bird.

The Great Hall was stretched. The architecture of the Fold, usually so solid, so purposeful in its geometry, felt translucent, as if the walls were being scrubbed away by an invisible, caustic tide. The shadows were not lengthening; they were dissolving. At the center of the room stood Drevik.

He did not look like a man fighting a war; he looked like a man performing a liturgy, a sacred, terrible duty. His black and deep-purple vestments were fastened tight, the gold celestial embroidery catching the dim, dying light of the torches and refracting it in jagged, desperate streaks. The high, stiff collar framed the white-ceramic kitsune mask, the gold accents gleaming like predatory eyes in the gloom. Above him, the crystal mitre drifted, the void crystals within it spinning in a frantic, silent orbit, humming with a frequency that made the air vibrate.

He held the void staff leveled toward the empty air, his knuckles white beneath his dark gloves.

"The debt is noted," Drevik said. His voice did not carry through the hall; it landed. It dropped into the room like a stone into a deep, dark well, heavy and final. "But the interest is not yet due."

A shadow that was not a shadow surged from the corner—a tear in the fabric of the room. The Nought. It moved with a terrifying, silent fluidity, a patch of absolute negation that consumed the light of the torches as it passed, leaving nothing in its wake. It reached for the center of the hall, for the very heart of our home, a creeping erasure that threatened to turn our history into a blank page.

Drevik did not flinch. He leaned into the void, his boots planted firm on the stone, his entire being a counter-pressure to the emptiness.

"You seek to unmake what has been bound," he said. He did not shout. He spoke to the emptiness as of a slow, wayward student. "You seek to turn the stitch into a fray. But there is no thread left for you to pull."

I watched from the doorway, my fingers gripping the doorframe so hard the wood bit into my skin. I felt the loss of the room’s substance, the way the stones beneath my feet felt less like granite and more like a fading memory. The air tasted of nothing—no salt, no smoke, no life. I wanted to run to him, to press my warmth against his back, to remind him that the hearth was still burning, that there was something worth saving. But I knew my place. I was the anchor. If I moved, the anchor dragged, and the ship would drift into the dark.

Drevik struck the floor with the base of his staff.

The sound was a chord—a heavy, resonant strike that echoed through the hollowed halls, a sonic boom of existence. A ring of light, gold and violet, erupted from the point of impact. It did not expand in a wave; it reclaimed. The light raced across the floor, stitching the cracks in the stone, mending the thinning air, forcing the architecture back into its proper shape.

The Nought recoiled. The erasure hit the gold-rimmed boundary and shuddered, its edges fraying into a grey, impotent mist.

"The Nought is a hunger of the void," Drevik said, his eyes—those vast, star-pupiled voids visible through the mask's slits—fixed on the encroaching nothing. "But the void is also a vessel. And a vessel must be filled."

He began the inversion. I had seen him perform many rites—summoning light, binding shadows, sealing wounds—but this was different. This was not a defense; it was a predatory act. He was not pushing the darkness away; he, the master of the void, was pulling it into the staff. He was turning the dissolution into a weight. He was making the nothingness into something that could be held, something that could be taxed, something that could be taxed and controlled.

He drew the emptiness into the orb at the staff's tip. The crystals in his mitre turned pitch-black, absorbing the theft. The room groaned, a deep, structural moan. The very bones of the Fold creaked under the sudden, massive influx of captured void.

For a moment, the pressure was unbearable. I felt the air leave my lungs, leaving me gasping in a vacuum. I felt as if the very concept of 'Halla'—my name, my history, my love—were being stripped from my mind, leaving only a numb, hollowed shell.

Then, the snap.

Drevik twisted the staff, a sharp, decisive motion. The captured nothingness imploded. A shockwave of pure, stabilized energy swept through the hall, a burst of light so bright and so heavy it felt like a physical blow. It washed over the tables, the chairs, the tapestries, and finally, over me, searing my vision.

The pressure vanished. The air returned, thick and sudden, tasting of ozone, old ash, and the salt of my own sweat.

The Hall was whole again. The stones were solid, the grain of the wood visible in the torchlight. The shadows were merely shadows, cast by the flickering torches, not by the absence of existence.

Drevik stood in the center of the room, his shoulders rising and falling with a slow, steady rhythm. The mask hid his expression, but I saw the way his gloved hands trembled, just once, before he gripped the staff again. He looked exhausted, a man who had just bargained with a hurricane and won by a single, bruised inch.

He turned his head toward the kitchen. Even through the ceramic mask, I felt his gaze. It was not a look of triumph. It was the look of a soldier checking the perimeter, ensuring the gate was still shut, checking to see if the foundation held.

He walked toward me. The heavy tread of his boots was the only thing that made the world feel real, a rhythmic pulse of presence in the aftermath of the void. As he approached, the room's attention narrowed until there was nothing but the space between us. He stopped at the threshold of the much smaller kitchen, the edge of his purple vestments brushing the doorframe.

He did not enter. He stood in the transition, the space between the duty of the hall and the sanctuary of the hearth.

"It is receding," he said.

"For now," I replied. My voice sounded thin, brittle, as if it might shatter like the porcelain of his mask.

"You stayed at the hearth," he said. There was a question in it, a need for confirmation that the anchor had not drifted.

"The fire is steady," I said, stepping just an inch forward. "I did not let the embers go cold."

He reached out. His hand, encased in the dark, star-dusted glove, hovered near my cheek. He did not touch me, but I felt the cold, vast gravity of him. It was a weight I had learned to lean against, a heavy, certain presence that anchored my soul even when the world dissolved.

"The Nought will return with a longer memory," he said, his voice dropping to a low, somber register. "It will look for the seam where we are weakest. It will look for the crack in the stone, the flaw in the ritual."

"Then we will stitch it tighter," I said. I reached out and took his hand, pulling it the final inch. I pressed his palm against my skin. He was cold, like the stone of a mountain, but beneath the glove, I felt the steady, rhythmic pulse of a heart that beat for more than just itself. "We will reinforce the walls. We will deepen the hearth."

"You cannot hold it alone, Halla," he whispered, the mask pressing close to my face. "The weight of the void is too great for one soul to bear."

"I am not alone," I said, my eyes locking onto the dark slits of his mask. "I have the hearth. And I have you."

He stood there, a silent sentinel in the doorway, a man who had just swallowed a piece of nothingness to keep the world alive. For a heartbeat, the exhaustion seemed to break through his resolve, and he leaned his forehead against mine—a brief, desperate contact of warmth against cold.

But as I pulled him closer, my eyes drifted past him, to the floor of the Great Hall, just beyond the kitchen door.

There, in the corner where the light of his staff had failed to reach, a small, dark shape remained. It was not the Nought. It was something too solid, too deliberate. It was a scrap of parchment, scorched at the edges, dropped as if by a fleeing hand.

I did not move to pick it up. I knew how to keep a hearth, and I knew that some things—some truths—were better left buried in the ash. I knew that the fire could burn away many things, but it could never truly erase a shadow.

The shadow of the betrayal was already beginning to stretch, longer and deeper than the night.
