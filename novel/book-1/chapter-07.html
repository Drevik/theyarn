Chapter 7: New Moon Patrol

Chapter 7: New Moon Patrol

The absence of the moon felt like a hole in Drevik's chest.

He stood at the compound's eastern exit, flexing his fingers against the strange emptiness where his power usually hummed. During the waxing moon, shadow magic responded to his call like an eager hound. Tonight, under the black sky of the new moon, it lay dormant—buried beneath layers of mortal weakness that made him feel suddenly, acutely human.

"You're fidgeting." Lexi appeared at his shoulder, her auburn hair pulled back in a tight braid, her combat leathers creaking softly as she moved. "Stop it. You're making me nervous, and I don't get nervous."

"Everyone gets nervous."

"I don't." Her green eyes held his for a moment too long. "Especially not on routine patrol duty."

Routine. That was the word that sat wrong in his stomach. After the warning from Elder Thornwood—after the old man had looked at him with those calculating eyes and suggested tonight's patrol might be "eventful"—routine felt like a lie wrapped in comfortable paper.

"Maybe I should stay behind," Drevik said. "Without my powers—"

"You're not useless just because you can't shadow-walk." Lexi checked her blade, a short sword forged from cold iron that gleamed dully in the corridor's ambient light. "You have eyes. You have a brain. And apparently you have something the Elders find interesting, even if they won't tell us what."

She said us like she belonged to the same category. Like they were both outsiders navigating a world that didn't quite accept them. Drevik wondered if that was true, or if Lexi Thornwood—daughter of an Elder, raised within the Order's embrace—could ever understand what it meant to be truly on the outside.

"The breach parameters are standard," Lexi continued, all business now. "We walk the perimeter, check the anchor points, report anything unusual. In and out in two hours. You stay behind me, you do exactly what I say, and we both come back alive." A pause. "Clear?"

"Crystal."

"Good." She turned toward the exit, then stopped, her voice dropping. "Drevik. I know my father spoke to you. I know he warned you about tonight."

The admission hung between them.

"He told me the patrol might be dangerous."

"He told you to refuse." Lexi's jaw tightened. "He does that. Creates tests, watches how people react. It's his way of sorting the useful from the expendable." Her eyes met his again, and something flickered there—something that looked almost like concern. "You should have refused. You're still learning. Your powers are unreliable. This isn't fair to you."

"Life isn't fair. I thought you knew that."

A ghost of a smile crossed her face. "Lesson one, and you've already mastered it." She gestured toward the exit. "Come on, acolyte. Let's see if you survive the night."

The campus at midnight wore a different face than the daylight version Drevik had come to know. Familiar buildings loomed like sleeping giants, their windows dark, their shadows pooling in unnatural depths that seemed to breathe. Without the moon's light, the Veil between worlds grew thin—and the darkness grew hungry.

Lexi moved through the shadows like water through cracks, her footsteps silent, her body low and ready. Drevik followed as quietly as he could, acutely aware of every rustle of his jacket, every soft impact of his boots on concrete. He felt clumsy beside her. Exposed.

"Anchor point one is ahead," she murmured, barely louder than breath. "The old humanities building. There's a basement access that leads to one of the oldest Veil boundaries. Watch for cold spots. Watch for—"

She stopped. Her body went rigid.

Drevik almost collided with her back, catching himself at the last moment. "What is it?"

"Quiet."

He strained his senses, trying to see what she saw. The air had changed—not colder exactly, but thinner, like the oxygen was being pulled from his lungs. A smell reached him, faint and wrong: something like rusted copper and rotting flowers.

"Shadowlands breach," Lexi whispered. "Small, but active. Stay close."

She drew her blade and moved forward, and Drevik had no choice but to follow.

The breach was invisible to normal sight—a tear in reality that revealed itself through sensation rather than vision. The cold intensified as they approached, seeping through Drevik's clothes and into his bones. The darkness in the alley behind the humanities building seemed to pulse with a rhythm that matched his heartbeat, and he realized with growing horror that the shadows were moving on their own.

"Lexi—"

"I see it." Her voice was steady, but he caught the tension in her shoulders. "Stay back. This shouldn't be—"

A shape emerged from the darkness.

It was wrong in ways that defied description. The creature had too many limbs, joints bending in directions they shouldn't, its body seeming to flicker between solid and shadow like a corrupted video file. Its head—if it could be called a head—was a mass of writhing tendrils that opened to reveal a mouth lined with teeth that existed in multiple dimensions at once. Looking at them made Drevik's eyes water, made his brain ache with the effort of processing something that shouldn't exist in this reality.

"That's not a standard breach entity," Lexi said, her blade coming up. "That's not supposed to be here."

The creature turned toward them. It had no eyes, but Drevik felt its attention like ice water on his spine.

"Run," Lexi commanded. "Get back to the compound. Warn the others."

"I'm not leaving you—"

"Go!" She launched herself at the creature, blade flashing in the non-light of the breach.

Drevik's feet stayed rooted to the concrete. He watched Lexi dance around the entity's lashing limbs, her movements precise and deadly, her blade carving lines of silver light through the darkness. She was magnificent. She was also outmatched.

The creature was fast—faster than anything that size should be. One tendril whipped toward Lexi's head, and she ducked barely in time. Another came from her blind side, and she twisted away, but not cleanly. The edge of the tendril caught her shoulder, tearing through leather and flesh. She hissed in pain but kept moving, kept fighting.

Drevik's hands clenched at his sides. He reached for his power, for the shadow magic that usually flowed so readily, but found only emptiness. He was mortal tonight. Helpless.

No, something inside him whispered. Not helpless. Different.

And then the visions hit.

One moment he was standing in the alley, watching Lexi fight. The next, he was drowning in possibility.

He saw her die in three different futures, each one crystalline in its clarity:

In the first, she overextended, reaching for a killing blow, and the creature's tendrils wrapped around her throat. She crumpled, her blade clattering uselessly to the ground.

In the second, she retreated toward the wall, and the entity shifted tactics, driving her backward until she had nowhere to go. It consumed her slowly, deliberately, its teeth working through her body while she screamed.

In the third, she called for Drevik to help, distracting herself for a single heartbeat. That heartbeat was all it took. The tendril pierced her chest, and she looked at him with surprised eyes as the light faded.

Three deaths. Three futures that ended in blood and silence.

But there was a fourth.

Drevik saw himself moving, his body acting without conscious thought. He saw himself sprint toward the fight, toward the creature's left flank. He saw himself grab Lexi's arm at the precise moment the entity's attention shifted, pulling her down and to the side as a tendril whipped through the space where her head had been. He saw them tumble together into a narrow alcove between buildings, pressed together in the darkness, the creature's limbs scraping uselessly against the stone walls.

In that future, they survived.

The visions collapsed back into the present, and Drevik moved.

He didn't think. He didn't plan. He simply acted, his body following the path his foresight had laid out like a map. Sprint. Left flank. Grab.

"Drevik, no—!"

His hand closed around Lexi's arm. He yanked, pulling her off-balance, sending them both tumbling toward the narrow gap between buildings. They hit the ground hard, Drevik's shoulder slamming into concrete, Lexi's weight landing half on top of him as they rolled into the alcove.

The creature screamed—a sound that wasn't a sound, that bypassed the ears and struck directly at the mind. Its tendrils lashed at the walls around them, scraping stone, searching for purchase, but the alcove was too narrow. It couldn't reach them.

They lay tangled together in the darkness, breathing hard. Lexi's face was inches from his, her green eyes wide with shock and fury and something else—something vulnerable that she quickly buried beneath anger.

"What the hell was that?" she demanded. "You could have gotten us both killed!"

"You were going to die." Drevik's voice came out hoarse. "I saw it. Three different ways. This was the only one where you lived."

Lexi stared at him. "You saw—"

"Possible futures. I think." He swallowed hard. "It's happened before. But never like this. Never so clearly."

Above them, the creature writhed and screamed, its tendrils probing the alcove's entrance but unable to squeeze through. It was trapped on this side of the Veil, and it knew it. The breach behind it was already beginning to close, the Shadowlands pulling back like a receding tide.

"We need to move," Lexi said quietly. "Before it finds another way in."

"Can you walk?"

"Can you stop asking stupid questions?" She pushed herself up, wincing as her injured shoulder took weight. Drevik saw the blood now, dark against the torn leather, and reached for her instinctively.

"Your arm—"

"Is fine." She caught his wrist before he could touch her. Her grip was firm, but not unkind. "I've had worse. Much worse." A pause. "Thank you."

The words seemed to cost her something. Drevik understood. Gratitude didn't come naturally to people like Lexi—people who built their lives around being the strong one, the capable one, the one who saved others.

"You don't have to—"

"I know." She released his wrist and rose, pulling him up with her. "Come on. We need to report this."

The walk back to the compound was silent, but not uncomfortable. They moved together through the shadows, Lexi's pace slightly slower than before, her wounded arm held carefully against her side. Drevik stayed close—not close enough to crowd her, but close enough to catch her if she stumbled.

He kept waiting for her to dismiss what had happened. To chalk his foresight up to luck or instinct. But she said nothing, and the silence between them felt heavy with meaning.

At the compound's entrance, she finally spoke.

"What you did back there. The visions." She turned to face him, her expression unreadable in the darkness. "That's not a common ability. Among the Order, maybe one in a hundred have any precognitive talent. And what you described—seeing multiple futures clearly enough to choose between them—"

"I know." Drevik's throat was dry. "I know it's not normal."

"Nothing about you is normal." Her voice was soft now, almost gentle. "You know that, right? The shadow-walking, the way you adapted to the Order so quickly, and now this. The Elders aren't just interested in you, Drevik. They're watching you. And that's not always a good thing."

"Is that a warning?"

"It's a fact." She stepped closer, and for a moment, Drevik thought she might reach for him. Her eyes held his, and the air between them seemed to thicken. "You saved my life tonight. I won't forget that."

"I'd do it again."

"I know." A ghost of a smile. "That's what scares me."

Before he could respond, she turned and entered the compound, leaving him standing in the doorway with his heart pounding and his thoughts racing.

The Elder's chamber was cold, lit only by candles that cast dancing shadows on the walls. Elder Thornwood sat behind his desk, his weathered face unreadable as Lexi delivered her report. Drevik stood beside her, acutely aware of every word, every pause, every flicker of the Elder's eyes toward him.

"A non-standard entity," Thornwood repeated. "During a new moon patrol."

"Yes, sir. It came through a spontaneous breach. We weren't able to determine the cause before it sealed."

"But you neutralized the threat."

Lexi hesitated. "The creature was trapped on this side of the Veil when the breach closed. It couldn't reach us in the alcove. We waited until it... dispersed."

"Dispersed." Thornwood's gaze shifted to Drevik. "And you, acolyte. What role did you play in this engagement?"

Drevik felt the weight of the question. "I provided support, sir. And I..." He trailed off, uncertain how to describe what had happened.

"He saw the creature's movements before they happened," Lexi said flatly. "He predicted where it would strike and pulled me out of the way. If he hadn't, I'd be dead."

Thornwood's expression didn't change, but something flickered in his eyes—something that looked almost like satisfaction.

"Foresight," he said. "Rare. Powerful. And manifesting during a new moon, when your other abilities are suppressed." He leaned back in his chair. "You survived. Interesting."

"Sir?" Lexi's voice was sharp. "You knew something would happen tonight. You warned Drevik—"

"I suggested the patrol might be eventful. I did not arrange for a breach." Thornwood's tone was mild, but his eyes were hard. "The Veil is weakening, Lexi. This is the third non-standard entity this month. Someone—or something—is testing our boundaries. Drevik's abilities may prove crucial in the days to come."

Drevik felt a chill that had nothing to do with the room's temperature.

"You're using him as a tool," Lexi said. "He's still an acolyte. He's still learning. You can't just—"

"I can." Thornwood rose, his tall frame casting long shadows in the candlelight. "And I will. The Order's survival depends on using every resource at our disposal. Your feelings for the boy do not change that."

Lexi's jaw tightened. "My feelings—"

"Are obvious to anyone who watches you." Thornwood's gaze moved between them. "I suggest you both rest. Tomorrow will bring new challenges, and I expect you to face them with clear heads."

The dismissal was clear. Lexi turned sharply and strode from the room, and Drevik had no choice but to follow.

She stopped him in the corridor outside, her hand catching his arm. Her grip was tight, her eyes bright with frustration and something else—something that made his pulse quicken.

"Don't let him use you," she said. "My father. He'll sacrifice anyone for what he calls the greater good. He's done it before."

"I'm not planning to be sacrificed."

"No one plans it." She stepped closer, and Drevik could smell the copper tang of her blood, the faint trace of something floral beneath it. Her breath was warm against his face. "You did well tonight. Better than I expected. But that doesn't make you safe."

"Nothing makes me safe." He held her gaze. "I learned that lesson before I joined the Order."

Something shifted in her expression. The anger drained away, leaving behind a rawness that made his chest ache.

"I don't want to see you hurt," she whispered. "I know I've been hard on you. I know I pushed you away. But tonight, when that thing had me cornered—when I thought I was going to die—"

"You weren't going to die. Not if I could stop it."

"Why?" The word came out broken. "Why would you risk yourself for me?"

Drevik reached up, brushing a strand of hair from her face. His fingers lingered at her temple, and he felt her tremble.

"Because you're worth saving," he said simply.

Lexi made a sound that might have been a laugh or a sob. She leaned into his touch, her eyes closing, and for a moment they stood there—two people who had been fighting each other for weeks, finally letting their guards down.

Then she pulled away, and the walls came back up.

"Get some sleep, acolyte." Her voice was steady again, but something had changed. The distance between them was smaller now. "Tomorrow, we train. If you're going to survive whatever my father has planned, you need to be ready."

"Lexi—"

"Good night, Drevik."

She walked away, and he watched her go, his fingers still tingling where they'd touched her skin.

In his room, Drevik sat on the edge of his bed and tried to make sense of the night. The visions had faded, but he could still feel their echoes—three futures where Lexi died, and one where she lived. The weight of that knowledge pressed down on him like a physical burden.

He had saved her. That was what mattered.

But Elder Thornwood's words lingered, cold and sharp. The Veil is weakening. Someone—or something—is testing our boundaries.

And Lexi's warning echoed alongside them: He'll sacrifice anyone for what he calls the greater good.

Drevik lay back against the pillow, staring at the ceiling. His powers were growing, evolving in ways he didn't fully understand. The Order had accepted him, but that acceptance came with expectations—and with dangers he was only beginning to see.

Outside his window, the new moon hung invisible in the sky. Somewhere beyond the Veil, shadow creatures prowled and plotted. Somewhere within the compound, Elders made plans that might or might not include his survival.

And somewhere down the hall, Lexi Thornwood was probably lying awake too, her wounded shoulder throbbing, her thoughts as tangled as his own.

Drevik closed his eyes and let the darkness take him. Tomorrow would bring new challenges. Tonight, he had done enough.

He had saved her.

For now, that would have to be enough.
