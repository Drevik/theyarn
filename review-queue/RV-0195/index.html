The flour was low in the bin, and the salt was damp from the humidity of the kitchen. I measured the grain by the weight of the scoop, a steady, rhythmic pulse that served as the only reliable ledger of the larder. One scoop for the rye, two for the barley, a pinch of the fine white for the morning’s pastries. A house is only as strong as its stores, and a household is only as whole as its anchor. To lose track of the grain was to lose track of time; to lose track of time was to invite the unraveling.

Drevik stood by the hearth, even though the fire had not yet been lit for the morning. He did not move like a man preparing for a journey; he moved like a man who had already departed, leaving only the shell of himself to attend to the formalities. He was a ghost inhabiting his own skin. The silver-white of his hair caught the grey, bruised light of the Fold’s perpetual afternoon, and the gold accents on his kitsune mask glinted with a cold, sharp precision that seemed to cut through the kitchen’s gloom.

He did not look at the cupboards. He did not look at the heavy oak table where the others would soon gather to break bread and trade rumors. He looked only at the space where the Threshold met the air, his gaze fixed on the seam of the world.

"The Nought is pressing," he said.

His voice was stripped of inflection, a flat note that vibrated in the marrow of my bones. I did not look up from the dough. I focused on the resistance of the mass beneath my palms, the way the gluten gave way and then fought back.

"The stores are sufficient for a month," I replied, my tone measured, professional. "If you return within the moon, we will not need to ration the meat. The barley is holding well, and the cellar is cool."

"The Nought does not care for your rations, Halla." He leaned his void staff against the stone of the hearth. The glowing orb at its tip pulsed—a slow, rhythmic beat that matched no living thing, a heartbeat from a dead dimension. "It seeks to unmake the seam. If the seam fails, there is no month. There is only the end of the counting."

I smoothed the dough, pressing it flat against the floured board. It was heavy, resilient, and required a constant, steady hand to keep it from tearing or becoming too airy. It was much like our lives here. "Then you must bind it. You have always found the cost of binding to be worth the weight of the debt."

He turned then. I could not see his eyes behind the white ceramic of the mask, but I felt the sudden, crushing gravity of his attention. It was a physical weight in the room, the way a heavy stone settles into soft earth, displacing the air. He did not smile, but the slight, practiced tilt of his head suggested the sly confidence the others called his grace. To me, it was simply the way the house held its breath when he entered a room.

"I take no one's hope," he said, his voice dropping to a low, dangerous register, "and I give no one my promise without stating the cost."

I wiped my hands on my apron, the linen stiff with starch and the ghosts of a thousand meals. "The cost is already paid, Drevik. You pay for the Fold with your absence. We pay for the Fold by keeping the hearth warm and the doors bolted. That is the arrangement."

He stepped toward me. He did not touch me. He never did in the kitchen, where the heat of the ovens and the sanctity of the work demanded a certain distance of propriety. He stood close enough that I could smell the scent of him—not the salt of sweat or the musk of leather, but something like ozone and old, cold starlight. It was a scent that belonged to the spaces between the stars, a scent that made the warmth of the kitchen feel suddenly fragile.

He reached out and touched the edge of the heavy wooden table. His fingers, gloved in deep, bruised purple, traced the grain of the heavy oak. He looked at the table as if he were checking the structural integrity of a cathedral's foundation.

"Are the others prepared?" he asked, his gaze remaining on the wood.

"They are as prepared as they can be," I said. "Kael is sharpening the blades. Mira is tending to the wards. They do not speak of it, but they feel the shift in the air."

"And you?" He looked up, the void crystals in his mitre swirling like trapped smoke. "Do you feel the shift, Halla? Or are you too buried in your flour and your salt to notice the floor dissolving beneath you?"

I met the mask with a steady gaze. "I feel the dampness in the salt. I feel the weight of the dough. I feel the need to ensure that when you return, there is still a house to return to. If I look too closely at the void, I cannot tend the flame."

"A dangerous way to live," he murmured. "To ignore the shadow until it is standing in the doorway."

"The shadow is only a lack of light," I countered. "And light is something we can control. The Nought is not."

"The Nought is the hunger that eats the cupboard itself," he said, pulling his hand back. He straightened, his tall, crystalline mitre catching the dim light, making him appear vast, a figure carved from the very architecture of the Fold, too large for the domesticity of the kitchen, yet the only thing making the kitchen a sanctuary.

He turned and walked toward the Threshold. Each step of his heavy boots on the stone floor sounded like a closing gate, a finality that echoed in the corners of the room.

I watched his back. The black and deep-purple vestments were fastened tight, the gold embroidery shimmering like dying embers in the twilight. He did not look back. He never looked back when he left. To look back would be to acknowledge that there was something worth staying for, and Drevik lived by the law of the departure. He was a man of the threshold, belonging to neither side.

He reached the door—the single, heavy, iron-bound door that separated the Fold from the void between worlds. He placed his hand on the latch.

"The salt is damp, Hallera," he said, his voice trailing behind him, unheard by anyone but the stone.

"I will replace it," I said, my voice small in the vastness of the room.

"See that you do. We cannot have the preserves spoiling. We cannot afford to lose what we have left."

He pulled the latch.

The Threshold opened.

There was no explosion of light, no roar of wind or sudden gale. There was only a sudden, profound absence. The air in the kitchen seemed to thin, the atmospheric pressure dropping so sharply that my ears popped, as if a lung had been punctured. For a single, terrifying second, the room felt hollow, a mere shell with the meat stripped away, a ghost of a kitchen. The light from the doorway was not light; it was a lack of color, a grey, hungry smudge of nothingness that pushed against the warmth of the hearth, trying to find a way in.

Then, the door swung shut.

The click of the latch was the loudest sound I had ever heard. It was a small, mechanical sound—a simple piece of iron meeting wood—but in the sudden vacuum, it sounded like a bone snapping in the dark.

I stood alone. The dough sat on the board, unworked and cold. The flour bin was low. The salt was damp. The silence of the house rushed in to fill the space Drevik had vacated, a heavy, suffocating shroud.

I picked up the dough. I began to knead. I kneaded to maintain the rhythm. I kneaded to ensure the structure remained. I kneaded to convince myself that the world was still solid.

The Fold was silent. It was the silence of a room after the candles have been extinguished, but the wick was still warm and the smoke was still rising. It was a silence that did not rest; it watched.

I moved to the larder to check the preserves. I needed the movement. I needed the checklist. I checked the seals on the honey jars, ensuring the wax was unbroken. I checked the tension of the ropes in the grain store, making sure the weight was distributed correctly. I checked the locks on the cellar door. Everything was as it should be. Every gate was engaged. Every weight was balanced.

But as I reached for the jar of pickled beets, a sensation crawled up my spine—a prickle of wrongness that had nothing to do with the cold.

I noticed the seam.

It was a small thing. A tiny, hairline fracture in the dark wood of the pantry shelf, no wider than a strand of thread. I had wiped the dust away from this exact spot only an hour ago. It had not been there. It was a clean break, a sudden separation of fibers that looked as though the wood had simply decided, in a moment of profound indecision, to stop being a single piece.

I reached out, my finger trembling slightly, and touched the crack.

The wood was not merely cold. It was a dry, hollow cold—the kind of temperature that exists only in the center of a dead star, a heatless chill that seemed to suck the very life from my fingertip. It felt as though the shelf were no longer made of matter, but of an idea of matter that was slowly fading.

I pulled my hand away, gasping. I looked at the jar, then at the shelf, then at the heavy kitchen door.

The silence in the kitchen grew heavier. It pressed against my eardrums like rising water, making my pulse thud in my neck. I looked toward the hearth, where the fire had not yet been lit, and I realized with a jolt of pure, primal terror that the darkness behind the wood was not the usual, comforting dark of a sleeping house. It was a darkness that had begun to settle, a thick, viscous weight that was waiting for the light to fail.

The Nought was pressing. I could not see the void, I could not see the hunger, but I could feel the math of the house changing. The numbers no longer added up. The weight of the room was decreasing. The stability was hemorrhaging.

I picked up the cloth and began to wipe the table. I wiped the same spot over and over, scrubbing with a frantic, mindless intensity, trying to erase the emptiness, trying to scrub the crack out of existence. But the wood remained smooth, cold, and utterly, terrifyingly alone.
