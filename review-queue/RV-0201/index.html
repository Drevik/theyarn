The seventh lock turned a quarter-hair early. That was all.

Mira measured it twice. The Bureau had taught her that a thing measured once is a story and a thing measured twice is a number, and she did not tell herself stories about locks. The drift held. It was not wear. She had cut these wards herself, three winters back, and cut them to answer her hand and no other. A lock is a promise a door makes. This one was keeping its promise before being asked.

Sixteen keyed doors in the Fold, sixteen keys, one ring, and the ring rode her hip from supper to psalm. The Threshold door was not on the ring. The Threshold was his and had never been on anyone's ring. Everything else in the house that shut, shut to her. She had decided some time ago — slowly, the way a woman decides a thing so slowly she believes she always knew it — that this was her office in the household. She was the one who kept count of what held.

She finished the round in the Bureau's order, because order was the thing the Bureau had gotten right. The garden rang its hour; the garden has one hour, and it keeps it. The bread from supper still warmed the long table. Wren had thanked the oven, as she does, and no one had found it strange in three years.

The tenth key turned with a hair of play it had not had on Monday. The pantry lock — she had made it the most spiteful lock in the house, forty-one wards, a small war against thieves — met her key and turned smoothly. Too smoothly. Nothing picks a lock by making it kind.

A lock hangs in a frame; a frame stands on a floor; a floor lies on the house; and the house, under the garden, hangs on Wren. That was the arithmetic. So when her ring told her the same small lie at four doors, she did not go to bed. She went down the garden stair to wake the weaver.

Under the garden there is a hall with no lamps; the Fold keeps it dark so the void-glass can be seen. Panes in the floor, and below the panes the void, and hung beneath the void on anchor-seams: Wren's wall. Ash-thread warp, and for weft nothing you could see — her hum, laid in and holding. It hung under the house the way a net hangs under a hull, between the boat and the deep water. She had hung it the winter of the Glass Tide, when the Threshold's hinge wept glass and the house needed a lining. Three years the void had stayed off the Fold's belly because Wren had sung it so. It was beautiful. Mira had looked for a better word in three languages and had not found one.

Mira had never been afraid of that hall. She had audited it like any other floor. Tonight the panes showed the same void as always, and she stood on it a moment longer than an audit takes.

Wren came down with her shears on her belt and her hair tied back, humming before her feet found the last stair. She hums at everything. She hums at bread. In three years Mira had never once heard her hum a wrong note.

"You never call on me before the psalm," Wren said.

"The seventh lock turned early. A quarter-hair. The tenth has play. The pantry's gone kind." Mira held up the gauge. "Four doors. One drift."

Wren knelt on the glass, laid her palm flat on the weave, and hummed the plain note the wall always answers.

The wall answered her a quarter-tone flat.

Wren stopped humming. In three years Mira had heard her stop twice.

"Get the lamp," Wren said.

She went out over the glass on her knees, hand above hand, following the flat place outward toward the far seam where the wall carries the house's anchor. The flat went flatter. Then it went to nothing: a stretch of weft with no answer in it, thread that was still thread and no longer song.

"Here," Wren said. "Mira. Here."

The knot was a weaver's mercy-knot, tied to be untied. Its tail lay an arm's length long, and it had been fed through the weave to the void-side, where it hung down into the dark like a rope left in a well.

"I tied this," Wren said. She said it to the weave, not to Mira. "The first night. The wall was a bandage then, not a wall, and I was two people about this house. A weaver keeps a tail. I meant to cut it after. I meant to cut it every year." Her hand lay flat on the knot. She did not press. "And I didn't, because cutting it meant I would never need it, and I wasn't ready to mean that. Then it was three years. That's all. That's everything."

Beside the knot, one thread of the weft was gone. Not cut. Cut thread has two ends and a story about a blade. This one had no ends. The weave held its shape around the absence, the way a room keeps warm for a while after the fire goes out.

"Unmade," Mira said. She had read that word in a Bureau file once, years ago, and had put the file down and not believed it.

Wren lifted the tail's end from the glass and smelled it and went still in a way Mira had no file for.

"My wax," Wren said. "Bazaar black. Tar and orange peel. I brought two cakes when I came here. One is in my kit. My kit lives in my room." She stopped.

Mira said the number for her. "Eleventh key."

"Yes."

"You don't lose keys."

"No."

"You don't lend keys."

"No."

Neither of them said the rest of it: that the wax had been used over seasons; that someone had kept Wren's running road oiled for her, kindly, out of her own kit, behind the eleventh lock. One answer fit every fact, and it was the oldest answer in this house. Mira set it down behind her, out of the light, and did not look at it, and did not say it in front of Wren. A woman's files are the one country she governs. Nothing was going in any file while Wren sat on the glass.

He came down the stair without the stair sounding, which he did. The underhall did what rooms did around him: it got quieter and nearer. Wren's lamp steadied. The keys on Mira's ring rang once against each other and settled. He wore the Threshold to the chin — collar fastened, mitre on, the crystals in slow orbits — and there was ash on his boots, though none of them had been near ash.

He did not ask. He read the floor: the lamp, the shears, the tail laid out straight on the glass, the stretch of weft with no song in it.

"Say it to me in order," he said. "Wren first."

Wren said it in order. When she reached the wax he closed his eyes for the length of one word and opened them again.

"Then here is the cost, out loud, because that is the rule of my house," he said. "The wall is Wren's. The flaw is Wren's. The mend is Wren's — and it can't be mended. It has to come down whole and be hung new, and only her hands can do it, and no hands do that work tired. So: tonight we hold, tomorrow she cuts, and until the new weave hangs I keep the Threshold with both hands full. You will find me less at the table than you are used to. Less patient." He paused. "Maybe less kind. That's the price. It's my price. Nobody argues with me about my prices."

He smiled when he said it. The smile was the trick, same as ever: it made the price sound already paid.

Then he knelt on the glass, level with her. "You left a door for yourself. Everyone I have ever kept left a door. Mine is older than yours and made of worse thread, and it is still holding, worse luck." He put two fingers on the mercy-knot, and under his hand the weave hummed one note, true. He lifted his fingers, and the note fell flat again. "The question was never whether you left it. The question is what we do now that it has an address."

"I cut it tonight," Wren said.

"Tomorrow. With rested hands."

"I won't sleep."

"Then sit up with us. That one isn't an order. It's a request, and you're allowed to refuse me."

He turned to Mira last and held out his hand, and she gave him the audit: gauges, drifts, four doors, the order she had walked them. He said her numbers back to her. All of them, to the hair, in her own order — three years of evening audits he had never been shown. He wrote nothing down; everyone in the house knew he wrote nothing down. She understood for the first time what that meant. She had been filing her reports with him for three years, and he had never once lost one.

Mira looked at her gauge for a while after that. It had nothing to tell her.

Wren sat down on the glass with her back against her own wall. She sat the way a person sits against a body. After a while she asked whether the kitchen had bread, in the voice of a guest.

Mira went up and cut two slices and brought them down, and Wren held hers and did not eat it, and held it. That was worse than weeping, which she was not doing.

Sera came down with the censer and the ash bowl. On the stair Drevik stood aside for her, and she went to Wren first. She knelt, put ash on the backs of Wren's crossed hands, and said the short words she keeps for foundations. Mira had heard them over a bread oven, over two weddings, over a door that would not shut. They were old words, and Sera spent them like a miser: on everything that mattered. Only when they were done did Drevik cross the glass and sit, and Wren put her back against his arm the way she had put it against the wall. He waited for Sera. He always waited for Sera. Mira had filed that under manners once. She filed it under architecture now.

While they sat, the void below the panes pressed. You could watch him hold it. The staff's orb dimmed as a lamp dims with a door standing open in the room, and the crystals of the mitre stopped drifting, all of them, all at once. Sera asked him whether he was warm enough. He said yes. The kindness arrived in his face a quarter-hair late — the same drift Mira had measured in her seventh lock — and then his hand found Sera's after the sentence was done, and the crystals moved again.

Mira measured that too. She filed nothing. There was nowhere left in her to put it.

Vesper came down at the second hour with her sword on and asked the only question she ever asks of anything new. "Can it bleed?"

"No," Wren said.

"Then tell me what to hold, and I'll hold it."

"The stair. Anything that comes for us has to come down the stair."

Vesper took the top stair and sat the night there with her blades bare across her knees, the whetstone going in slow strokes, so that anything that came down would have to announce itself to iron.

Mira did the one useful thing she knew. She took her spare gauge from her kit, set it to the wall's tension, and folded Wren's fingers around it.

"Hold that," she said. "It reads the weave, and your hands know this wall better than my instrument does. I need both of you tonight."

It was true. It was also a rope, and Wren's rope had been taken away from her tonight, so it was rope for rope. Wren looked at the gauge, and folded her hand around it, and after that her hands were working hands, which was the only mercy Mira knew how to make.

They worked past the garden's hour. The flat place grew. The threads did not break; they went out, one by one, the way lamps went out in the Bureau's corridors at shift's end — on a schedule no one could find. Wren marked each dead thread with a length of red silk, so the morning would know what to take. Mira carried the eleventh key around inside her skull all night and kept it out of every sentence she said. The fact sat in her boot like a stone.

· · ·

She walked the ring once more before the cutting. That was her office: keeper of what holds. If the wall was coming down today, she wanted her sixteen promises kept twice over before noon.

The first door behaved. The second took its key a quarter-hair early. At the third, the lock turned before she let the key turn. At the fifth she touched the plate with a fingertip, and the wards asked her nothing and opened. At the seventh — her seventh lock, her first liar of the evening before — she stood with the key in her hand and the door already open.

The pantry opened to her knuckle. The library stair opened before her shadow reached it. The chapel door, which in four years had opened to no hand but Sera's, opened to Mira's palm on the first touch. The eleventh door, Wren's door, opened with the rest. She did not go in.

None of them were broken. That was the thing to hold, and she held it. Nothing in her house was broken. Every lock still worked. Every lock worked for everyone. She had built hers jealous on purpose; a lock is a difference, held. The Nought — she used the Bureau's word in her own head now — had spent the night teaching her house that differences are optional.

Then she came to the end of the ring and counted her doors in the Bureau's order, and the count came out wrong.

Sixteen doors she kept. The corridor held seventeen. The seventeenth stood where the plaster had always been, under the small stars Sera paints above the lintels. It had not been cut. There was no cut. The plaster ran into the frame like water finding its level, and the frame into the plaster, without a seam, and Mira knew seams now; Wren had taught her seams. The door was shut. It had no keyhole. It did not need one. It was shut the way a fact is shut. Not locked. True.

Faint through the floor, the shears kept their schedule. Wren was cutting her wall down ahead of the hour he had set her, rested hands or not, thread by thread, and the weave sang as it came down. Behind Mira, all down the corridor, her sixteen doors stood open, courteous, holding nothing.

She did not call him. He was holding the Threshold with both hands while the wall was down; she had heard him price that holding at less kind, and she was not ready to spend him yet.

Mira stood alone at the end of her ring with sixteen keys and no door left that wanted one, and she did the only thing the Fold had taught her that the Bureau never had. She put her palm flat against the door, the way Wren put her palm on the wall, and she hummed. One note. Badly, in a dialect she had no right to.

The door answered her. A quarter-tone flat.
