Core idea

When moving from dyadic (two-person) thinking to polyamorous structures, you face the structural gravity of "couple privilege." This section addresses how established pairs often unintentionally create hierarchies that marginalize others, whether they are metamours or single individuals. You must learn to navigate the logistical and emotional complexities of multi-person networks without using your primary partnership as a fortress. The goal is to transition from a mindset of "protecting the unit" to "nurturing the web," ensuring that every person in your network—regardless of their attachment status—is treated with autonomy and respect.

Key concepts and terms

  • Couple Privilege: The unearned social and structural advantages held by a pair in a relationship, such as legal recognition, shared housing, and the "default" status in social circles, which can be used to overshadow or exclude others.
  • Default Hierarchy: The tendency for the most established or "senior" relationship in a network to become the central authority, often dictating the rules, schedules, and emotional priorities for all other participants.
  • Metamour: A relatively modern term referring to your partner’s other partners (your "lover's lovers").
  • The Fortress Mentality: A defensive psychological state where a couple uses their bond to shut out outside lovers or to avoid the vulnerability required to integrate new people into their lives.
  • The Single Slut: A term used here to describe an individual who navigates polyamory without a primary partner, emphasizing their autonomy and the specific ethical responsibilities they hold within a network.
  • Rainbow of Connections: The various structural configurations in a network, ranging from single-to-single connections and partner-to-partner connections to the complex dynamics of a lover interacting with an established couple.

Techniques worth drilling

  1. The Metamour Etiquette Protocol

To prevent friction in the "web," you must practice proactive boundary-setting with metamours.

  • Step 1: The Pre-Meeting Ask. Never show up unannounced to a metamour's space or a group setting where they are present. Always send a low-pressure text: "I'll be at [Location] with [Partner]; would you be open to a brief hello, or would you prefer space?"
  • Step 2: Responsibility Transfer Prevention. Never make a metamour responsible for the safety or emotional regulation of your third party. If you bring a new lover to a group, you are the primary person responsible for their logistics, consent, and comfort.
  • Step 3: Sacred Date Preservation. When meeting metamours or group members, do not allow "couple talk" or logistics about your primary partner to bleed into the time reserved for the other person. Treat the date as a standalone entity.
  1. Deconstructing the Fortress

You must actively audit your partnership to ensure it is a gateway, not a wall.

  • Step 1: Identify the Wall. List moments in the last month where you and your partner made a decision (logistical or emotional) that automatically excluded or sidelined a non-partner.
  • sStep 2: Open the Gate. For every "wall" identified, create a protocol for consultation. Instead of "We decided X," practice "We are considering X; how does this impact your ability to participate in the group?"
  • Step 3: Decentralize Authority. Practice making decisions about group gatherings or shared social calendars without assuming your "seniority" as a couple grants you the final vote.
  1. The Single Slut’s Autonomy Drill

If you are single, you must practice asserting your "Bill of Rights" to prevent becoming an "appendage" to a couple.

  • Step 1: Verbalize the Right to Refusal. Practice saying "No" to a rescheduled date or a group plan that inconveniences you, without offering a long-winded apology.
  • Step 2: Demand Presence. If a partner or a couple treats your time as secondary to their "seniority," practice the phrase: "I value our time together, but I need our plans to be honored as much as your primary commitments are."
  • Step 3: Asserting Emotional Visibility. Practice stating your needs clearly: "I am here as an individual with my own feelings, not just as a guest in your relationship."

Practice drill

The Rights-and-Responsibilities Audit

This week, you will perform a dual-layer written audit of your current primary relationship or your most frequent connection.

Part A: The Draft On a piece of paper, divide a page into two columns: "My Rights" and "My Responsibilities."

  • If you are coupled, write down the rights you believe you have as a partner (e.g., "The right to have my needs prioritized during our dates") and the responsibilities you owe to your metamours (e.g., "The responsibility to manage my partner's jealousy so it doesn't affect you").
  • If you are single, write down your rights as an autonomous agent (e.g., "The right to have my plans honored") and your responsibilities to the network (e.g., "The responsibility to communicate my boundaries clearly").

Part B: The Audit Select one specific person in your network. Write a brief, honest assessment of how your current interactions measure up to your drafted list.

  • Example: "In my relationship with [Name], I am currently failing my responsibility to 'Ask before meeting metamours' because I have been showing up to group events without checking in. Conversely, they are failing my right to 'Have my dates honored' because they frequently cancel due to 'couple' obligations."

Goal: Do not use this audit to confront the other person yet. Use it to identify where you are using "privilege" or "singleness" as an excuse to bypass ethical engagement.

Critiques and caveats

  • The Risk of Over-Correction: While deconstructing "couple privilege" is essential, avoid a reactionary "anti-couple" stance that devalues the stability and unique intimacy that long-term partnerships provide. The goal is equity, not the destruction of the dyad.
  • The Burden of the Single: While the "Single Slut" has a Bill of Rights, the text acknowledges that singles often carry a heavier "emotional labor" load to maintain visibility. Be wary of entering networks where you are expected to do all the work to "integrate" yourself.
  • Safety Warning: When practicing "The Pre-Meeting Ask" or asserting rights, always assess your physical and emotional safety. If a partner or group is prone to volatility, use these techniques with a pre-planned exit strategy.

Reading notes

These chapters serve as the structural blueprint for the book's broader philosophy. While earlier chapters focus on internal emotional work (communication, jealousy, etc.), Chapters 20-21 apply those internal tools to the external, social architecture of polyamory, moving the focus from "me and you" to "us and the web."
