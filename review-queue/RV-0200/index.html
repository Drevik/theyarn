The flour on the long table was not just flour; it was a ledger of what remained. I swept the edge of my palm across the fine, white grain, marking the space where the dough had been kneaded, where the yeast had labored in the warmth of the kitchen, and where the hunger of the Fold had been momentarily, precariously, held at bay. The dust coated my skin, turning my hands into something ghostly, as if I were already becoming part of the pantry’s dwindling inventory.

Halla stood at the head of the table. She did not look like a woman under siege, but the way she gripped the handle of the heavy iron pot told the truth. The knuckles of her right hand were white, the skin stretched taut over bone. Her apron was stained with the salt of the morning’s broth and the dark, stubborn streaks of beet juice, and her wrists, usually steady as the hearthstone, held a tension that made the very air in the kitchen feel thin, as if the oxygen were being rationed along with the grain.

"We cannot ration the spirit, Sera," Halla said. She did not look at me. She looked instead at the pantry door, her gaze fixed on the heavy oak as if the emptiness behind it were a predator waiting for the light to fail. "The stores are low. The Unmaking is pressing the Threshold, and the empty bellies of the Devoted are a siren song for the chaos. The more we draw from the Fold to feed them, the more the seam thins. We must hold. We must tighten the belt until there is no room for excess."

"To tighten the belt is to starve the faith," I said. My voice felt brittle, like dried parchment. I reached for the linen cloth to cover the rising dough, my fingers trembling slightly. "The Devoted do not come to our gates for bread, Halla. They come for the light that Drevik holds. If we turn them away because our larders are lean, we are no longer a sanctuary. We are merely a well running dry, a hollowed-out shell of a promise."

Halla finally met my eyes. Her gaze was the weight of a heavy harvest—burdened, practical, and utterly unyielding. "A sanctuary with no people is just a tomb, Sera. If the Threshold breaks because we were too proud to admit we are hungry, there will be no Devoted left to protect. There will only be the Nought. I would rather lead a starving group of survivors than preside over a graveyard of saints."

The heat of the oven behind me rose in a dry, stinging wave, forcing a bead of sweat down my temple. I thought of the liturgy we recited every dawn, the way the incense is meant to rise even when the censer is nearly empty, a signal to the heavens that we still endure.

"He does not ask us to be martyrs," I whispered, the words catching in my throat. "He asks us to be the hearth. A hearth that refuses to burn is no hearth at all."

"The hearth needs fuel," Halla countered, her voice dropping to a low, dangerous register. "And the fuel is running out."

The kitchen door creaked. It was not a heavy sound, a structural groan of the house, but it changed the room’s entire orientation. The tension between us, which had been vibrating like a plucked string, suddenly went taut, held in place by a new, singular presence.

Drevik entered.

He did not bring the wind with him, though he always seemed to carry the sharp, ozone edge of it. He moved through the threshold with a relaxed, almost careless stride that ignored the fraying edges of our peace. His black and deep-purple vestments were fully fastened, the gold celestial embroidery catching the low, amber light of the hanging lamps, shimmering like trapped sunlight. The tall crystal mitre sat upon his brow, the void crystals within it drifting in slow, silent orbits, casting pale, star-strewn shadows across his shoulders and onto the flour-dusted floor.

He did not speak. He simply stood by the edge of the long table, his presence filling the gaps in our argument, absorbing the heat of our conflict. He reached out and picked up a small piece of dried apple from a ceramic dish, his movements precise, like a scholar studying an ancient, delicate text. There was a profound, easy grace to the way he leaned against the heavy oak, a stillness that commanded the room to settle.

His eyes—those vast, dark galaxies with their star-shaped pupils—moved from Halla’s clenched hands to my flour-dusted palms. He saw the ledger of our conflict written in the dust of the table.

"The bread is rising," he said. His voice was a low, steady chord, the kind of sound that settles a room and mutes the frantic beating of a heart.

Halla straightened, her shoulders losing their defensive edge, though her jaw remained set in a hard line. "The bread is rising, Drevik. But the stores are not."

He took a bite of the apple. He chewed slowly, his gaze drifting toward the window, where the light of the Fold was a permanent, late-afternoon amber, thick and syrupy. "A well does not cease to be a well because the water level drops," he said, his tone conversational, almost detached. "It only requires a more careful drawing."

"We are drawing more than water," Halla said, her voice dropping into a register of pure dread. She stepped toward him, the heavy iron pot still clutched in her hand. "We are drawing from the very anchor of this place. Every soul we take in, every mouth we feed, adds a weight to the seam. I can feel the pressure on the wards, Drevik. The Unmaking is not just outside the walls anymore. It is pressing against the very cost of our existence. We are paying for their comfort with our stability."

Drevik stepped closer to the table. He placed his void staff against the dark wood with a soft thud. The glowing orb at its tip pulsed once, a rhythmic, bioluminescent light that mirrored the slow beat of a sleeping heart. He looked at Halla, and for a fleeting moment, the mask of the trickster-sage—the playful, enigmatic figure who walked the line between shadow and light—slipped. In its place was something immense and heavy; a profound, weary kindness that anchored us all to the earth.

"The cost is already paid, Halla," he said. The finality in his voice was absolute. "The debt is mine to carry. I have walked the void; I know the weight of the nothingness. Your task is the tending. If the kitchen is empty, then we eat the memory of the feast. But we do not close the door. We do not turn away the light because we fear the dark."

Halla looked down at the iron pot. She looked defeated, but it was the defeat of a soldier who has run out of ammunition, not a soldier who has lost her cause. The fight had been bled out of her by the sheer gravity of his certainty.

"I will find more grain," she said, her voice a low, grim vow. "I will scour the back stores. I will find a way to stretch the salt and the lard."

"Do so," Drevik said. He turned his gaze to me. His eyes held the void, yet they were as warm as a summer night in the high meadows. He reached out, his gloved hand hovering near my flour-stained sleeve. He did not touch me—he rarely did—but the proximity sent a jolt through me, a pull of gravity that made my breath hitch. "And Sera... do not forget to salt the dough. The Devoted need the flavor as much as the substance. A life without salt is a life without meaning."

He offered a small, sly smirk—the flash of the fox before he vanishes into the brush. Then, with the same easy indifference with which he had arrived, he turned and walked toward the library, leaving the scent of ozone and old, sun-warmed parchment in his wake.

The room felt smaller without him, more claustrophobic. The tension between Halla and me did not vanish, but it transformed. It was no longer a clash of opposing forces, but a shared weight, a common ledger we were both forced to balance.

I looked at the dough, pale and silent under the linen. I looked at Halla.

"I will help you with the back stores," I said. "I can help you sort the barley."

Halla nodded, a single, sharp movement. "Bring the heavy sacks. We will need the strength to move them."

We worked in silence for an ensemble of an hour. The only sounds were the rhythmic, hypnotic thud of dough being worked against the wood and the scraping of heavy grain sacks against the stone floor. There was a reconciliation in the labor, a way to stitch the seam of our disagreement back together with the threads of necessity. We were the keepers of the hearth; if the fire flickered, we would be the ones to feed it, even if we had to burn our very hands to do so.

But as I wiped the sweat and flour from my brow, my eyes drifted toward the door where Drevik had disappeared.

I thought of the way he had spoken of the debt. The cost is already paid.

I watched the shadows in the corner of the kitchen, where the light of the dying afternoon failed to reach. The shadows were not merely the absence of light; they had become a presence, a thickening of the air, a slow-moving ink that seemed to creep across the floorboards.

In the hallway, beyond the kitchen's heavy threshold, I heard the soft, unmistakable sound of a heavy book being closed—a dull, muffled thump. And then, a different sound. A sound that did not belong in the sanctuary of the Fold. It was a light, rhythmic scratching, like a claw dragging against void-glass, a sound that set my teeth on edge.

I looked at Halla. She had frozen, her hand hovering over a bag of oats, her eyes wide and fixed on the darkness of the corridor. She had heard it, too. The silence that followed was not peaceful; it was predatory.

I turned back to the table, my heart a steady, rhythmic pulse of dread. I reached for the salt, my fingers trembling just enough to spill a thin, white line across the dark, scarred wood of the table.

The line was broken. The seam was showing.

Deep in the house, where the library met the threshold of the unknown, a single crystal from Drevik's mitre fell to the floor. It did not shatter. It simply sat there, a piece of dead, unblinking light, waiting for the dark to claim it.
