The door opened in the north wall of the grange, where no door had ever been, and Halla came down the ladder with the last sack on her shoulder.

Forty pounds. She had weighed it at dawn; it had weighed forty-one on Monday, and the string had never been cut, and no mouse in Ossel Ford could get through her seals. She had stopped reading the numbers aloud in the second week, after the miller's wife wept over three ounces. A keeper who frightens her town does the famine's work for it.

Tansy waited at the foot of the ladder with the mess keys flat on both palms, which is how you hand over a thing you don't want to be seen taking. Halla counted them onto her own palm. Gate, cellar, smokehouse, salt-room, the strong box with the second string.

"Everyone eats once before anyone eats twice," she said. "Feed the children first anyway. And don't let anyone tell you the binding makes it safe to waste."

"Six days and the flour's held its weight," Tansy said. "Is that held enough for you?"

"It's held enough for the town."

Steward Osric had come up from the ford with the morning relief, four carts of the Devoted's grain, and stood at the tailboard counting sacks into a tally-book with a pencil that never needed the eraser. He was a spare, courteous man, clean in the way that made her want to check the corners.

"A gift for the hearth's stores." He set a sack into her arms before she could rule on it. "From the relief. The Ascendant's household shouldn't arrive hungry."

"The household takes no provision," she said.

"Everything has a cart," he said, "if a road can be found." He said it pleasantly and shut the tally-book.

The sack weighed what his tally said. Her hands disagreed. Her hands had been right about her own flour two weeks before the steelyard admitted anything. She thanked him, because his carts had fed her town through the worst of it, and you don't call a man's gift a liar in front of his carters. She put the gift sack with her own, over the second shoulder. You don't leave a sack you don't trust in a cellar you can't watch. Osric watched her take it and made no note, and she made a note of that.

Then the door filled.

He came through it and the yard turned to face him: carters stopped mid-count, the children on the wall sat down where they stood, the gulls off the fish-carts sat down on the fish-carts. He was dressed as no granary had ever seen — black and deep purple, fastened to the throat, gold worked over the seams in stars and lines. A white ceramic mask with gold at the cheeks. A crystal mitre a hand taller than any head had business under, small dark stones adrift in it. A staff whose orb lit the flour dust on his shoulder without warming it.

"You're owed," he said.

"I fed you," she said. "That's not a debt. That's a table."

"Then I'll make a promise and say the cost out loud. It's the only way I make them."

She shifted the sacks and waited. A man who states costs can be traded with. She had met too few.

"The house I keep has a table that has never gone short, and it never will," he said. "That's the promise. The cost is the house. It holds a door the world needs shut, and it stands on the ones who live in it. Come in and you're one of the things it stands on. When the door is pressed, you'll feel it in the beams. There's no being quit of it after. And it will never again be only yours. You'll be the table other people are fed at, and you won't be able to stop."

"Nobody ever taught me how," she said.

He held the door. "Then come be fed for once."

She had done this sum weeks ago, the third night of the binding, when his crew had held the Nought's mouth shut for three days and her stores had stood them upright through it. Some sums you finish early and carry. He reached for her sack. She moved it to the other shoulder. The smirk arrived under the mask, tilted, and he let her carry it, which was the correct answer.

She stepped over the sill.

Inside the wall there was no wall. There was a seam of cold, and then air that smelled of bread and rain, and light the color of a garden in the last hour of afternoon. She crossed with eighty pounds on her shoulders. Behind her the seam closed clean, and Ossel Ford went on without her, and she did not look back.

A woman in gray vestments stood at the inner sill with a small board: a heel of bread, a dish of salt, incense already gone over the lintel. "First the road, then the bread," the woman said. "I'm Sera. The house was warmer the minute you crossed. I felt it at the altar, and I don't say that about many people."

Halla dragged the heel through the salt and ate standing, the way she had always eaten. Sera watched and did not offer her a chair, which was the correct welcome.

"The room started growing the night you fed the crew," Sera said. "The house knew before we did."

The corridor had held four doors. It held five now, and the fifth had wood shavings still against the frame, the way a room leaves its work out when it is not done being new.

The kitchen fire had never gone out and never would, she was told. She stood in front of it a while. Then she banked it anyway. Some hands don't know another way to keep a fire. It burned steadier for it, and she had expected no less of herself.

At the long table Wren sat cross-legged on the end bench with a torn seam open in her lap, humming at it. Vesper cleaned a blade at the door end with the door in her eye-line and did not stop cleaning it, which was its own courtesy. Mira had three rings of keys out on the wood and a printed form she was reading the way you bite a coin.

Wren reached over and touched the burn on the apron's hem. "I could mend this tonight."

"The burn stays," Halla said. "It's inventory."

The table had six places. He served all five and took no plate.

At Ossel Ford, on the third night of the binding, he had eaten three helpings and asked her for the burnt edge of the loaf. She set a plate for him anyway. He took one bite of it, after Sera's hand found his sleeve, and she could not have said who the bite was for. She had fed men through two hard winters and a siege of nothing, and she knew the difference between a man who wasn't hungry and a man who was paying.

Sera came down the table with the teapot and said, low, by her ear, "He counts who sets his plate. Don't stop setting it."

There was a stew of the Fold's own barley, and bread with a crust that crackled when it was set down, and butter she would have sworn was churned that morning, in a house with no cow in sight. She had fed five hundred in worse and eaten standing. She sat. That was the house's doing, and she let it.

"Osric saw you off," Drevik said. It wasn't a question.

"He gave the hearth a sack of his relief grain."

"He asked you on the fourth day of the binding how the hearth was provisioned. You told him it wasn't. He said, everything has a cart, if a road can be found. He said it to your Tansy again on the ninth day. On the twelfth he asked a carter what grain the hearth takes, and the carter said none, and Osric wrote that down." He set the ladle back in the pot. "I never write anything down. So I notice who does."

"You think he's counting the house."

"I think his numbers are too clean for a man feeding a famine. Your flour lost weight ugly and honest. His grain loses nothing on his books." He looked at her hands, the ones that had noticed. "Salt a fistful of his gift. Weigh it. Write me what your hands say."

"The jar will be in my book by midnight," she said.

"Good," he said. "Paper survives me. If anything ever happens to my head, the count still lives somewhere."

She let that lie where it fell. Some sentences he said that way, and you learned not to pick them up.

She salted the fistful at the kitchen bench before she went down the corridor: grain, brine, sealed jar. Weighed on her own steelyard. Eleven ounces even. She wrote it in the column she ruled for gifts, and under where she wrote Ossel Ford, and she did not yet know she would be crossing that word out.

He walked her to the fifth door and stopped at the sill and did not go in. "Yours," he said. "I only open them once."

The room smelled of new wood and incense. Hooks in a row. A stove of dark iron, unlit, warm anyway. A table of one plank. A window with the garden's late light lying across it, the light that would lie there at midnight and at noon and every hour between. The room asked nothing of her. It had hooks and a stove and a plank, and that was the whole of its creed.

She hung the apron on the first hook. The door swung to behind them, and the latch seated itself with a small iron sound, and that was the taking.

He stood where the stove's warmth reached him and lifted the mask off and set it on the sill, face to the wall. The face under it was a young man's, tired in the jaw, and his eyes were the dark the door was made of, with stars in it. She did not look away.

The mitre went on the shelf above the stove. The stones in it kept their slow drift. Nothing in this house hurried.

The collar had eleven hooks. She had done small work all her life and she did it now, and his breath went out slow at the fifth, and neither of them said anything else worth saying for a while.

He came to her careful of his weight, the way she had taught a hundred kitchen girls to carry a full tray. She told him so, against his jaw, and he laughed once, low, and after that there was warmth, and weight, and the stove ticking the way a banked fire ticks when it is kept right, and that sound held her through it.

The vestments stayed, loosened to the waist. She had rationed harder things than hunger. He didn't ask, and she didn't explain.

She woke once in the deep of the night. He was awake, eyes open on the beams, his thumb moving on her shoulder the way a hand tells beads. She let him count. She slept again.

In the morning the room had grown a pantry. Three shelves of plain pine, a covered crock of salt on the lowest one, full, and a jar of plums in brine she had never put up. She checked the salt's lid twice, because a keeper checks, and set her tally-book on the middle shelf where her hand went first. She wrote everything, that book and thirty years of books before it. She did not write the night. Some entries a keeper holds in the head, like him.

Before the kettle was on, the door rang. Not knocked: rang, one low note, a bell let into the house.

Drevik went down the corridor and opened it a hand's width. She heard nothing through it. No voice, no wind, no ford. He shut it and came back wearing the mask.

"Weirbridge," he said. "Their bell went flat in the night. It's losing its bronze a note at a time, and the ringers are still pulling. That's a mouth."

"The Nought again," Sera said.

"Nothing else unlearns a bell that fast." He said the costs the way he said everything: out loud. "Six days in the world. Supper here. Keep the table, keep the fires, and no one crosses the Threshold alone. Not with anyone. Not for anything."

Then, to her, plain: "Weigh the gift tonight. If it's lost anything at all, mark it for me."

"I'll have a column ruled for it," she said.

Sera laid her hand flat on his chest. He covered it with his own. No one spoke. Then he went through, and the seam shut, and the garden's light didn't move.

Halla stayed.

She put the water on for bread and took the jar down from the shelf.

At midnight it had weighed eleven ounces even, sealed. She hung the steelyard, steadied it, and read it twice, because a keeper checks.

Ten and three-quarters.

It was a quarter of an ounce. She had seen famines start smaller.

She sat with the book a long time before she opened it, and then she wrote the number in a clean column, and under where she crossed out Ossel Ford and wrote: The Fold.

Whatever it was had come into his house salted, sealed, and under her eye, and it was still subtracting, and the man who held doors was six days in the world and one supper away. She turned the jar's lid a half-turn tighter, the way you do against anything that wants in, and set it on the window sill in the garden's light, where she could watch it while she worked.

Then she wrote the number down, and ruled the column for tomorrow. Paper survives.
