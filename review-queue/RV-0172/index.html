The workshop was not a gift; it was an inventory.

Mira stood in the center of the new room, her fingers tracing the rough, unfinished edge of a heavy iron workbench. The surface was cold, unmarred by the deep gouges of old use, and the light from the high, narrow windows fell in straight, sterile lines across the floor. It was a room of tolerances and measurements. It was a room of precision.

Everything in the Fold was precisely curated, but this room felt weighted, as if the very air had been measured to hold the scent of oil, cold metal, and the stale breath of a tomb. It was too clean. It lacked the lived-in chaos of the lower levels, the comforting layers of dust and grease that Mira associated with true work.

She looked at the tool racks. They were empty, their dark wooden silhouettes stretching upward like the ribs of a carcass. There were no calipers here, no fine-toothed files, no heavy-duty lathe. There was only the hollow, echoing space where they ought to be. The vacuum of the room pressed against her eardrums, a silent demand for purpose.

"The dimensions are correct," a voice said.

Mira did not jump. She did not have the luxury of being startled; in the Fold, a startle was a vulnerability, a crack in the armor. She simply straightened her spine, smoothing the front of her grease-stained apron, and turned.

Drevik stood in the doorway. He did not enter so much as he occupied the threshold, his presence acting as a dam against the light from the hallway. The dark purple of his vestments seemed to pull the light from the corridor, absorbing the warmth, while the gold embroidery caught the stray glints of the afternoon sun like trapped, dying sparks. He held his staff loosely, the glowing orb at its tip casting a rhythmic, low-frequency pulse against the stone floor—a heartbeat of light that made the shadows dance in time with his breathing. The white-ceramic kitsune mask hid his face, leaving only the sharp, intelligent tilt of his head and the silver-white sweep of his hair visible.

"The floor is level," Mira said. She kept her voice flat, the way one speaks to a ledger or a corpse. "The ventilation is sufficient for small-scale brazing. But the equipment is absent."

"I thought a person of your talents might prefer to select their own instruments," Drevik said. He leaned against the doorframe, his posture deceptively relaxed. He did not move to enter. He simply waited, a gravity that forced the room to acknowledge him. "A locksmith of the Bureau does and does not use a commoner’s wrench. I suspect you find the latter quite insulting."

"I am not a locksmith for the Bureau anymore," Mira said. She turned back to the workbench, her hands gripping the edge of the wood until the grain bit into her palms. "And I do not require a workshop to be useful to the Fold. My tasks are already set. I have enough to do in the existing repair bays."

"The tasks are set, but the tools are lacking," he said.

She heard the soft, rhythmic thud of his staff as he finally stepped into the room. He moved with a confidence that always felt like a challenge, a deliberate intrusion. He did not tread heavily; he moved as if the floor were merely a suggestion, a temporary obstacle in his path.

"You are denying the necessity of the room," he said. He was standing near the tool rack now, tapping a long, gloved finger against the empty wood. "You treat the expansion of this house as if it were a debt you are being forced to pay. As if every new door we open is just another way for the cold to get in."

"I treat it as a complication," Mira said. She wiped a smudge of old grease from her thumb onto her apron, a nervous habit she couldn't quite suppress. "Every new room requires more maintenance. More heating. More guarding. We are already spread thin against the Aegis Bureau's movements. We shouldn't be building monuments to our own vanity while the walls are closing in."

Drevik turned his head, the gold accents of his mask flashing in the dim light. "The Bureau moves in straight lines, Mira. They follow the law and the map. They march because they have been told where the borders lie. We move in the seams. We move in the spaces they have forgotten to measure. But a seam cannot be held if the needle is broken."

He reached into the heavy, voluminous folds of his sleeve and withdrew a small, leather-bound kit. He placed it on the workbench between them. The kit was small, but when it hit the wood, the weight of it made the heavy iron surface groan faintly.

"I found these in the lower bazaar," he said. "The balance is true. The steel is tempered."

Mira looked at the kit. She did not look at him. She could feel the warmth of his presence pressing against her side, a steady, heat-bearing weight that made the air in the room feel dense and difficult to breathe. She knew the kit contained what she lacked. She knew the calipers within were precision-grade, and that the files would have the bite of a predator. The hunger for them was a physical ache in her chest.

"I did not ask for these," she said, her voice brittle.

"I did not bring them as a request," Drevik said. His voice dropped, losing its playful, enigmatic edge, becoming something plain and heavy. He moved closer, stepping into her personal space, forcing her to confront the stillness of the room. "I brought them as an anchor. You keep the keys to the Bach's security, Mira. You ensure the doors do not fail when the Bureau comes knocking with their legal writs and their iron-clad warrants. You cannot do that with broken instruments and a spirit of denial."

Mira felt the pull of his words—the sudden, sharp desire to tear the leather strap from the kit, to feel the familiar, biting edge of a well-made file against her palm. It was a need for the order of the metal, for the absolute, unyielding certainty of a perfect fit.

"The Bureau is looking for more than just warrants," she said, her voice betraying a tremor she tried to stifle by biting her lip. "They are looking for the Threshold. They are looking for you. If we build more rooms, we only create more places for them to find us."

"Let them look," Drevik said. He moved even closer. He was so close now that she could see the star-shaped pupils drifting within the void of his eyes behind the porcelain mask. He was a vastness she could not map, a person who held the very edge of the world shut with nothing but his sheer, terrifying will. "They can search every door in the city, every cellar and every spire, but they cannot search the space between the breaths. They cannot find what they do not believe exists."

He reached out. He did not touch her face, but his hand hovered near her shoulder, the heat from his skin radiating through her thin sleeve like a brand.

"Use the tools, Mira," he commanded, his voice a soft, low vibration. "Build the locks. Secure the hinges. Reinforce the seams. Do not let the House grow larger than your ability to defend it. If you refuse to build the defenses, you are simply building our coffin."

Mira looked down at the leather kit. Her heart did not race; it simply felt heavy, like a lead weight dropped into the gears of a precision clock. She thought of the Bureau’s agents, their polished black boots and their unyielding, suffocating bureaucracy, and then she looked at the empty, waiting room.

"I will use them," she said, her voice a low, grim vow. "But I will not call it a piece of your charity. I will not call it a gift."

"A gift is something given without expectation," Drevik said. He began to back toward the door, the sly, enigmatic smirk returning to the set of his jaw. "A tool is an investment. I am merely hedging my bets."

He turned and left the room, leaving the door slightly ajar. The light from the hallway spilled across the floor, cutting a jagged path through the stillness, a reminder of the world outside the workshop.

Mira stood alone in the silence. The workshop felt even larger now, more predatory. She reached out, her fingers trembling slightly, and unbuckled the leather strap of the kit. She pulled the cover back.

The tools lay in their velvet cradles, gleaming, cold, and perfect. They caught the light from the high windows, throwing sharp, silver glints against the dark wood of the workbench. She picked up a pair of calipers. They were heavy. They were true. They were exactly what she had been pretending she didn't need.

She did not feel like a person being supported or cared for; she felt like a component being fitted into a larger, more dangerous machine. She felt like a gear being polished for a clock that was counting down to zero.

The silence of the room was broken not by the soft footfall of Drevik, but by a sudden, sharp sound from the hallway—a sound that didn't belong to the Fold.

It was the heavy, rhythmic clatter of armored boots. It was the unmistakable, synchronized strike of metal on stone, a sound of law and order approaching with a singular, crushing intent. It wasn't the wandering step of a servant or the light tread of a thief. It was a phalanx.

The sound was too coordinated. It was too loud. It was a percussion of war.

Mira dropped the calipers. They hit the iron workbench with a sharp, ringing note that echoed violently against the empty, unpainted walls.

Through the open door, the hallway was no longer filled with the warm glow of the Fold. It was filled with the harsh, flickering light of torches and the shadow of advancing shields.

The Aegis Bureau had found the seam.
