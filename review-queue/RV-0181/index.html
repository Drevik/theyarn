The writ arrived on the third hymn.

Mira had oiled the street door of the mission herself that week, hinge by hinge, and the knock came through her work without a sound. The Bureau had timed it for the rest between verses. She knew it the way she knew a cut: by the spacing.

Forty of the Devoted had come for the sealing of the season. Sera had preached the arithmetic at matins: eleven weeks to the turn, ash to bank, hymns to renumber, the mission's clock kept running by the hands that tithed the hours. The flagstones had been swept of the week's incense. The hymn board read 3, and then, in chalk, 4.

Drevik stood at the reading pillar with his staff. When the knock came, the hymn folded up mid-line, every voice at once, and that was the room answering him and not the knock.

He set the staff down across the inside of the sill and opened the door himself. He always opened doors himself. That was the morning's whole geography: the Bureau on the step, the house in its doorway, the staff between.

On the step stood an adjutant, gray to the throat, with two porters behind him carrying a document box between them like a stretcher, and behind the porters a fourth man. She knew the fourth by his heels before she let herself have his face. Nobody in the Bureau walked on the outsides of his heels but Tobin.

"Service," the adjutant said. "Form twelve, under the Reach Compact. Is the accused within?"

"She is," Drevik said. "So is the cost, I expect. Say it from the step."

The adjutant read. He read well; Bureau readers were taught to read a charge flat, so no word landed harder than its neighbor.

"Mira Valen. Sometime master of the pattern-shop at the Cairn. Charged: theft of Bureau property. Charged: breach of the Cairn's deep row. Charged: the deaths of nine wardens and twelve hands." He turned the page. "Evidence: her oath-form, five years standing. Evidence: the service log of the Cairn door, bearing her stamp. Evidence: affidavits of four hands, Form thirty-three. Evidence: a lift of the key recovered from the deep row's lock, Form forty-one." He turned the page again. "Demand: rendition of the accused at the ninth bell of the third day. On refusal, publication under Form eighty-eight." He lowered the warrant one hand's width. "Appended: the party styled the Veiled Communion answerable in abetment, its principal named."

"Read the name," Drevik said.

"The principal of the Veiled Communion, the Ascendant, styled Drevik."

"Noted. Now read the publication clause whole. This house doesn't take summaries."

The adjutant read it whole: the packet posted entire to the guild doors, the market boards, the chapel porches of the reach — oath, log, affidavits, lift — where anyone could read her tolerances off a wall. Somewhere in the third pew a hymnal dropped. The steward picked it up and squared it on the rail. While the clause was being read, two families left, walking softly, the way people leave a sickroom.

Then Form forty-one came across the sill. The adjutant lifted it from the box himself and put it into Drevik's hand, and Drevik passed it down the doorway to her without reading it. A porter's brace took her cuff on the exchange, and the seam went with a small sound, and she had no hand free for it.

"You first," he said.

The lift was a good one: lampblack on oiled paper, burnished with the stroke, not against it. The bitting ran six cuts. The spacing held to the thousandth. And the shoulder was hers, the steep one she had cut since her first year, the one the shop had called the Valen shoulder, and she had let them call it that.

"That's my shoulder," she said. "Nobody files that shoulder but me."

"So the form says," the adjutant said.

She heard the room take it in. Forty of the Devoted, and she had held the exhibit up to the light for them herself, in her own voice.

Above the mask, the crystals in the mitre had stopped drifting. She had watched them a year, in argument and in joke, and they had never once stopped. They hung in the glass like keys on a board nobody is allowed to touch.

"Adjutant," Drevik said, "you'll have your answer at the ninth bell of the third day. She gives it from behind my door, with a second of her choosing, and your writ waits outside with the weather. The Communion doesn't render people. It renders accounts."

"The Compact provides a surety," the adjutant said. "A bearer remains as bond until the answer."

"Then leave a bearer."

"Second Officer Tobin remains."

Tobin took one step to the rail and squared his feet and looked at no one. The adjutant had not glanced at him when he said it. A man chooses like that when the choosing was done for him.

"And a courtesy, Adjutant Crane," Drevik said. "In the spring your office filed for this mission's floor plans. Your clerk misdated the return and corrected it in the margin, in a hand that shakes on its sevens. I don't forget hands. Draft your own warrants against this house. You'll be longer in the work and shorter in the lies."

He turned inside, and the smirk arrived then, half a beat late. She had never once known it to arrive late.

"Costs out loud," he said to the mission. "That's the law of this house, and it doesn't bend for grief. Pending the answer, the accused stands outside the ring. Her keys go into trust with the first wife. Her workshop stands shut by our seal, not theirs. No Devoted turns her from the table, and no Devoted sits her a stranger. That's the whole of the cost. You've heard it said."

She unclipped the ring at her belt. Seven keys and a blank: the street door, the vestry, the cabinet of blanks, the garden gate, the workshop, the library stacks, the winder for the outer door's wards. And the blank she had ruined her first day in the pattern-shop and kept six years for luck, thumbed smooth.

She could have handed the ring to him. She gave it to Sera, because if he took it her face would go, and her face was the only tool left in her keeping.

Sera hung it on the censer-cord, below the chain, where it swung when the smoke swung. "It'll get the incense," she said. "Nothing rusts that gets the incense."

Vesper had come up on her left while the ring changed hands. A duel of law asks that the accused be offered a second; Vesper had become one without asking, and stood where a second stands, half a step back on the off-hand side.

"Nine wardens and twelve hands," Halla said from the end of the first pew. "Twenty-one. I'll want their names before the third day. The table keeps a ledger."

Wren's hum had stopped. Mira missed it the way you miss a stopped clock.

Sera turned to the Devoted last and lifted the censer, and the keys swung with it. "The watch doesn't stop for a form," she said. What the congregation made of that, Mira couldn't have said. She had her eyes on the cord.

The kitchen fire was banked to embers when she came down that night. Nobody banked that fire. In a year of nights it had never once been banked; it burned because the hearth was the hearth. She stood a moment with her hand out to it. Then she lifted the iron half-lid, built the flame back, and laid the service copies along the long table, and lit the lamps, and worked.

A form is a confession with the fear filed off it. She had told her apprentices that for years. She read the packet the way she'd have read any lock: for the tolerance, the place where a hand gets tired, or hurried, or kind. Lies have tolerances. Find the tolerance, find the hand.

The fold came first. The copies arrived folded chancery, thirds and one across, and she took the affidavit to the lamp and read the creases instead of the words. Under the chancery fold ran a set of quarter creases, pressed flat, then pressed over. Field hands fold quarters; a quarter fold rides in a coat and survives a river. Somebody had folded this packet in the habit of years and then dressed it for the chancery.

Second, the ink. Chancery ink is walnut and clove, ground and strained and worth stealing. This ink smelled of lamp oil and gall: the canteen's barrel, issued by the dram to hands who write outdoors. The affidavit swore it was sworn in the Cairn's chancery. The ink said written at a stove.

Third, the stamp. The service log bore her stamp, and the stamp was perfect, and she turned the copy over and looked a long while at the rim. Her stamp had a nick, top left, from the Cairn's back stair, six years ago, the day she carried too many blanks because she wouldn't make two trips. She never re-cut the rim. She kept the nick the way you keep a scar that paid for something. The log showed a whole rim. And under the log, printed, in its tidy place, the Bureau's answer waited: stamp re-issued upon separation. They had re-issued her before she had finished leaving.

Fourth, the lift. The best lie in the packet, and she had praised it in front of forty people.

Her own cuts came back. A hand cuts, checks, returns for its correction; half her keys carried a double stroke at the shoulder, because her hand always returned there. The lifted key's cuts never came back. Six cuts, spacing to the thousandth, no double stroke, no chatter, no fatigue. No hand holds to the thousandth. A jig holds to the thousandth.

She had seen the jig once, on a bench by the Cairn's canteen: brass and case-iron, a cradle for the blank, a frame for the file. Tobin built it. Tobin was proud of it. He called it the nights-key, because it cut her patterns without her and let the nights pass without waking the master. She had laughed at the brass. She had told him it would cut like a copy of her, because that was what it was.

Seven years of her lifts sat in the Bureau's archive, every key she had ever cut, lifted and filed, because that was the Bureau's law and her own pride had agreed with it. The jig hadn't copied her hand. It had copied her record. The Matriarch had a Mira in a drawer, filed lift by lift, much of it filed by Mira.

To prove a jig, you produce a jig. The jig sat behind Bureau doors, in Bureau property, under Bureau forms, producible if and only if the inquest went somewhere the Bureau had not pre-answered. Her proof lived in the one place her keys had never opened. She sat with that a while. It was very well made. That was the worst of it: she kept admiring the work. The packet wasn't an accusation. It was an invitation, addressed to the only reader in the reach who couldn't refuse the language.

Her own door had begun to stick. She oiled the hinge by lamplight, because her hands wanted a piece of work that said yes, and then she went to the study.

His door opened before she reached it. He was standing clear of the desk with the star charts pushed to one side, and the star in each of his pupils had burned down to a pinprick, lamps trimmed to last the night.

"The fold's wrong," she said. "The ink's canteen. The stamp's answered before I asked. The cuts are a jig's, and there's one jig."

"I don't need you innocent," he said. "I need you exact. Go be exact at him."

He opened the Threshold door onto the mission's vestry and left it open behind her. In a year she had never once heard that door stay open. It stayed open.

Sera was banking the censer's ash for the night watch. She took the ruined blank off the cord and pressed it into Mira's hand. "Luck doesn't keep on a hook," she said. "Carried or nothing."

Tobin sat in the gate room with his kit squared on the sill and his boots by the wall, the way a man squares things in a cell. The tea in front of him had gone cold with the pot's lid still crooked. She set the service copies on the table and sat across from him.

"Fold it," she said.

"Form twelve's chancery," he said.

"Fold it."

He folded. Quarters first. He caught it on the second crease and pressed it flat and left his hand flat on the paper, and looked at the cold tea.

"Field hands fold quarters," she said. "You wrote the affidavit in canteen ink and swore it past a chancery stove. You filed your own habits into evidence, and I'm the only reader in the reach who'd have caught either."

"You were always going to catch it," he said. "That's not my care."

"Whose, then."

His hand stayed flat on the fold. "She knows how you read. Why do you think the fold's wrong?"

"The jig cut the key," she said. It was not a question.

"Two years ago." He said it to the tea. "She had me sign for the blanks the same morning you resigned. The jig was commissioned the week before. She files ahead of everybody she keeps. She had your leaving on record before you did."

"Then say the errand under the errand. You were never a courier, Tobin."

"Come back." He looked up. "That's the errand under it. The packet publishes in two days, or it goes in a drawer, and the drawer has a condition. Your bench is still yours. She never let anybody sit it."

"And the Cairn?"

His shoulders moved. "The deep row's tenant is out. It left with Warden Oss's voice, and it's been asking after people by name. The Bureau can't recage what it can't key, and can't key what it can't cut, and can't cut without the master of the pattern-shop." He looked at the fold under his hand. "She doesn't want you punished, Mira. She wants you back. The accusation is the retrieval form."

"Who taught you the chancery fold?" she said.

"You did."

"Then you folded it wrong on purpose. Ninth bell, third day. You'll be called."

"Called as what?"

"Witness," she said, "or evidence. Fold yourself accordingly."

She took the copies and left him with the cold tea and the crease he hadn't dared flatten twice.

The Threshold door stood open, the way she had left it and he had left it. Wren was in the vestry with the banked ash, needle already threaded. She held out her hand for the coat arm without a word and sewed the torn cuff while the smoke settled: small, even stitches, crossed twice at the seam, which was Wren angry.

The house had answered the form in its four hands: a second at her left shoulder, a ledger open for twenty-one names, luck carried in a pocket, stitches at the cuff.

Two days. Somewhere in the reach, a thing with a dead warden's voice was learning new sentences, and the Bureau had written its asking as an accusation and signed it with her cuts. She trimmed the lamp to a working flame and began with the fold.
