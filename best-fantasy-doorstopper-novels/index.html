Best Fantasy Doorstopper Novels of 2026: Five Huge Books Worth Finishing

Published October 13, 2026 | 5 novels tested cover to cover by Elena Voss

Eleven days. That's how long it took me to read all 1,344 pages of Wind and Truth, and my right forearm ached for two of them. Worth it. A thousand-page novel that keeps you up past midnight is one of the cheap thrills left in this hobby, and I've spent six years as an indie bookseller putting doorstoppers into exactly the right hands.

Here's my problem with most "best fantasy books" lists: they recommend novels nobody on the list actually finished. I've read every book below cover to cover, some twice, in the formats I'm recommending them in. I read in bed, I read on a commute, I read the ending of The Priory of the Orange Tree at a laundromat because I genuinely could not put it down long enough to fold a towel. That's the test.

What Makes a Doorstopper Worth 900 Pages?

Long is not a virtue by itself. Plenty of 900-page fantasy novels are a 350-page novel wearing a trench coat. When I judge a big book (on this site and in my book club, which has survived four years, ask me how), I look for three things:

  • The middle earns its length. The first 100 pages and the last 100 sell almost any competent epic. The test is page 450. If the author is still revealing new information and not just shuffling characters between locations, the length is justified.
  • Series investment makes sense. A doorstopper is a time commitment multiplied by sequel count. I note where each series stands: finished arcs get credit, cliffhangers get warnings.
  • Re-read value. The best big books hide things on page 60 that detonate on page 800. If I finish and never want to revisit, it's a rental, not a purchase.

One more thing before the picks. If you're the person who says "I'll just wait for the paperback," I love you and you're mostly right. For my format math, see the buying guide below. The short version: a 1,300-page hardcover is a two-pound wrist problem.

The Top Picks for 2026

🏆 Best Overall

Wind and Truth by Brandon Sanderson

1,344 pages, Tor Books. The finale of the first arc of the Stormlight Archive, and the single most-read epic fantasy novel of the decade so far. The setup: Dalinar Kholin has gambled the fate of the world on a contest of champions against the god Odium, with a ten-day countdown ticking under every chapter. That ticking clock is doing a lot of work in a book this size, and it mostly works. Kaladin's storyline, escorting the broken assassin Szeth through his homeland, is the emotional spine, and the final payoff for his character is the most satisfying thing Sanderson has written. Amazon reviewers rate it 4.7 stars across 31,000-plus ratings, and the hardcover has been sitting around $18, which pencils out to roughly 1.3 cents per page. Try finding better value in a movie ticket.

  • Pages: 1,344 (hardcover is the current mass printing)
  • Requires: Reading books one through four first, plus the two novellas. Non-negotiable.
  • Best part: A ten-day countdown structure that forces momentum even at novel-breaking length
  • Series status: Arc one closed. A second Stormlight arc begins later.
  • Critic rating vibe: 4.36 on Goodreads across 162,000 ratings. Enormous book, enormous verdict split.

Watch out: The dialogue gets self-helpy in stretches. The "therapy arc" jokes about Kaladin have some truth to them, and a handful of the Dalinar chapters drift. If you haven't read Rhythm of War recently, skim a summary first.

🚪 Best Series Starter

The Will of the Many by James Islington

774 pages, Saga Press. The book I handsell hardest to people who finished Red Rising and felt hollow afterward. Vis Telimus is an orphan hiding a dangerous secret, surviving in the Catenan Republic, an empire that runs on literally borrowing the life energy ("Will") of its lower classes. He's recruited into the elite Academy, and the school competition framing makes the Roman-flavored world feel propulsive instead of stuffy. The twists in the last third are the kind that make you flip back to earlier chapters with your mouth open. As of late 2026, this and its sequel hold the top two spots on Amazon's historical fantasy chart, which tells you the recommendation engine of the fantasy internet has spoken. The paperback runs about $17, and the sequel, The Strength of the Few, is already out, so you can binge.

  • Pages: 774 (hardcover), trade paperback widely available
  • Vibe: Roman republic + murder academy + one extremely competent narrator
  • Best part: An end-of-book twist that recontextualizes the entire premise
  • Series status: Book two, The Strength of the Few (736 pages), published November 2025. Book three pending.

Watch out: The cliffhanger is genuinely mean, in the way that had my entire book club filing complaints. First-person narration means you never leave Vis's head, which some readers find claustrophobic over 774 pages.

💸 Best Budget Epic

The Priory of the Orange Tree by Samantha Shannon

832 pages, Bloomsbury. Every fantasy fan gets one moment where they look up from this book and realize it's 2 a.m. Mine was the laundromat. A queendom that has kept an ancient dragon-god asleep through a thousand years of ritual suddenly faces its heirs' line failing, and the story runs on two continents: East and West, with different magic, different dragons, and mutual distrust. The dragon-riding scenes live up to the cover. This is the rare standalone (well, standalone-plus-prequels) epic, so you get a complete story in one spine. The trade paperback has been hovering under $10 on Amazon lately, which for 832 pages works out to about a penny a page. Amazon shows a 4.5-star average across 25,000-plus ratings for the paperback. That's a lot of people agreeing.

  • Pages: 832 (trade paperback), the chunkiest paperback on this list
  • Vibe: Feminist reimagining of Saint George and the Dragon, dragons on two continents
  • Best part: The last 200 pages, where four storylines converge
  • Series status: Complete as a standalone. Prequels (A Day of Fallen Night, Among the Burning Flowers) exist if you want more.

Watch out: The middle third sagged for me. Shannon rotates four points of view and two of them move slowly for a couple hundred pages. Also, four POV voices means it took me sixty pages to sort out who was who, and the paperback's tiny family-tree pages live in the back where nobody checks until it's too late.

🗡️ Best Standalone Punch

The Sword of Kaigen by M.L. Wang

649 pages, self-published. The r/Fantasy community's favorite standalone for years running, and the book I recommend when someone says they don't need another 1,300-page commitment. It takes place in one village on the edge of an empire, in the weeks around a catastrophic war. The magic system is water-and-ice blade work, and it is the rare fantasy war story where the person who wrecks me emotionally is the protagonist's mother, Misaki, a housewife who buried her swordsmanship under a decade of dinner parties. The final act is devastating in a way most published-by-Big-Five fantasy doesn't dare. The Kindle edition famously sells for $2.99, and the paperback runs around $17.

  • Pages: 649 (paperback)
  • Vibe: Japanese-inspired military fantasy, intimate scale, brutal honesty
  • Best part: Misaki. The mother's chapters are worth the price alone.
  • Series status: Complete standalone. Wang's Blood Over Bright Haven is a separate great read.

Watch out: It's self-published, and it shows in places: typos survive in some printings, the cover art is serviceable rather than lovely, and the print quality of the paperback is fine but not collector-grade. Buy it for the words, not the objet. The war violence is heavy.

🎰 Best to Binge

Dungeon Crawler Carl by Matt Dinniman

446 pages to start, Ace. Yes, this is the alien-game-show-LitRPG where a man and his ex-girlfriend's prize-winning Persian cat fight their way up a dungeon that used to be Earth. I resisted it for two years. Then I read book one in three sittings and immediately bought the next four. The joke has a spine of real stakes: the humor works because the danger is genuine and the grief is genuine, and Princess Donut the talking cat is one of the great comedic characters in modern genre fiction. The series sits at 4.6 stars across 120,000-plus Amazon ratings, and the audiobooks by Jeff Hays are famous for good reason. Ace is reissuing the whole run in print, with eight books out and the eighth, A Parade of Horribles, landing this year at around $13 for the trade paperback. All of it is on Kindle Unlimited, if you subscribe.

  • Pages: 446 for book one; the series quickly becomes your personality
  • Vibe: Terry Pratchett energy in a survival-horror arena, with stat windows
  • Best part: Donut. Also the footnotes of the AI run the show's captions.
  • Series status: Eight books in print, ongoing, on Kindle Unlimited.

Watch out: The gore and crude humor are not for everyone, and if you primarily read on an E Ink e-reader, the stat blocks and game-window text render awkwardly at small font sizes. The chapter cliffhangers are engineered to make you buy the next book at 1 a.m. They work on me.

Format Math: Hardcover, Paperback, or E-Reader?

I tested this the dumb way, by reading the same chapters of Wind and Truth in three formats. Here's what I learned, since doorstoppers are where format choice actually matters:

  • Hardcover: Over two pounds at 1,300 pages. Two chapters in bed and your wrist files a complaint. But the typesetting is roomy and the footnotes are readable, which matters for Sanderson's margin jokes. Buy hardcover if you're building a shelf, not if you're reading one.
  • Trade paperback: The sweet spot for most doorstoppers. Reads one-handed, fits a tote, the built-in spine crack doubles as a bookmark. Slightly dense typesetting on the longest books, so I use a bright task lamp for late chapters (my picks live in our late-night book light guide).
  • E-reader: The weight problem vanishes, and seeing "14%" instead of a brick of unread pages is genuinely motivating. On Kindle, X-Ray keeps a 40-character cast sorted; Kobo sideloads EPUBs and ties into library lending through OverDrive, which matters because a 40-book fantasy habit on one store gets expensive. I do most of my ebook reading on a 7-inch or larger screen with warm lighting, since 1,000 pages of cool blue-white light at midnight wrecks my sleep. If you're choosing hardware, start with our Kindle vs Kobo comparison.

Bottom line from my experiment: paperback for the couch, e-reader for the commute and the nightstand, hardcover for the bookshelf trophy case. The shelf copies are pretty. The trade paperback is the workhorse.

How to Actually Finish a Doorstopper

After six years of bookselling and one too many abandoned IT paperbacks (twice, same page), here's the system that works:

  • The 100-page rule, inverted. Don't judge a 900-page book at page 100; you're still in prologue land for most epics. Judge at 200. If it hasn't hooked you by two full days of reading, return it guilt-free. Life's short and Stephen King is patient.
  • Aim low, daily. Twenty pages a day finishes a 1,000-page book in under two months. Ambitious 80-page Sundays followed by six days of nothing is how doorstoppers die on nightstands.
  • Pair the audiobook. For Wind and Truth I alternated print chapters with audio at 1.2x, and the commute chapters kept the print momentum alive. Whispersync-style pairing is the single best trick for 60-hour books.
  • Write one line per session. Where you stopped, what was happening. Future-you at 11:30 p.m. will thank present-you, especially in a book with five POVs and a dramatis personae longer than some phone books.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to read the first four Stormlight Archive books before Wind and Truth?
Yes. It's the direct finale of the first five-book arc and assumes you know every major event from The Way of Kings through Rhythm of War, including the Edgedancer and Dawnshard novellas. Starting here is like walking into a TV finale cold: you'll recognize the faces but miss every payoff.
Should I buy books this long in hardcover or paperback?
For actual reading, trade paperback wins. An 800-page hardcover weighs over two pounds, which is rough on one-handed bed reading. Hardcover is for the shelf and for collectors, trade paperback is the workhorse reading copy, and mass market paperbacks are cheapest but the type shrinks.
Is it better to read doorstoppers on an e-reader?
For most people, at least some of the time. An e-reader removes the weight problem, and a percentage counter is psychologically easier than a 900-page spine. Kindle locks you into Amazon's store; Kobo handles EPUB natively and connects to library lending through OverDrive. Many readers split it: ebook for travel, physical copy for rereading.
How do you actually finish a 900-page novel without stalling?
Commit to a small daily minimum rather than an ambitious one: 20 pages a day finishes a 1,000-page book in about seven weeks. Judge the book at page 200, not page 100. Pairing the print book with the audiobook on commutes keeps momentum through slow middles.
What order should I read the Stormlight Archive in?
Publication order: The Way of Kings, Words of Radiance, the novella Edgedancer, Oathbringer, the novella Dawnshard, Rhythm of War, then Wind and Truth. The novellas are short and contain referenced events, so skipping them only buys you confusion later.
Is The Will of the Many a finished series?
Not yet, but book two, The Strength of the Few, published in November 2025, so new readers can binge the first two volumes back to back. Fair warning: book one ends on a serious cliffhanger that had my entire book club filing complaints.
Is Dungeon Crawler Carl actually good or just a meme?
Genuinely good. Book one averages 4.6 stars across more than 120,000 Amazon ratings, and the Jeff Hays audiobooks are among the best productions in the industry. It's Pratchett-flavored humor wrapped around a sincere survival story, but the gore is real, so expect tonal whiplash.
If I only read one standalone, Priory or The Sword of Kaigen?
Priory for immersion: 832 pages of dual-continent politics, dragons, and a complete payoff. Kaigen for emotional impact on a shorter clock: 649 pages, one village, and a mother you will not be prepared for. If you want dragons, pick Priory. If you want to feel things, pick Kaigen.

Ready to commit to something enormous?

If you've already read the first four Stormlight books, Wind and Truth is the payoff you've been waiting 8,000 pages for, at around $18 for 1,344 pages of closing arguments. If you haven't touched the series, start The Will of the Many instead and thank me at the cliffhanger.
