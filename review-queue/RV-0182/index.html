The house ring hung on its nail beside the kitchen door, four keys and a blank. Wren's bow was worn to silk. Vesper's smelled of blade oil under the flour that gets into everything at that table. Sera's carried censer resin. Halla's smelled of dough, and it turned smoothest of the four, because Halla greases what she loves.

Every night for half a season I had taken that ring down, weighed it in my palm, and hung it back with the blank uncut. Tonight I asked Drevik for the alley behind my father's shop, and he said yes before I finished asking. That is the trick of him, and he admits it: three moves ahead, the truth told plainly, and everyone else spending the whole ride deciding whether to swallow it.

He cut the door himself. The seam opened in the Fold's wall onto frost and the far end of Bellmaker's Row, and the cold came off it like the cold off a well in summer — wrong for the season, wrong for both worlds at once. He held the seam with the staff, and the orb at its head dimmed while the door stood open. He stood a shade too still doing it. Sera has words for that stillness. I have one: wear.

"The shop is watched," he said. "Two men on the cisterns, paid through the month. Paid men watch badly. Also, they're watching the roads out of the city, because the Bureau thinks you'll run, and running people take roads." The mask took what little light the alley had. "You take doors."

"Say the rest."

"Whatever your brother tells you in there, you carry it back through this one. I don't take memories out of heads. I only keep mine."

That was the whole cost, and he had said it out loud before taking anything, which is more than any man at the Aegis Bureau ever did for me.

I went in. Behind me the seam shut to a line, and the line to nothing.

The bell over the shop door rang its two notes, wind first and customer second. My father could tell you which was which by the lag between them. Tonight both notes belonged to me.

Inside, the lamp sat low. Filings lay across the bench like black sand. The vise kept its chewed jaw; the drawer of blanks kept its felt. Tobin stood up too fast, and the blank in the jaws jumped, and he caught it against his chest and set it down flat, both hands.

"Mira." Then: "You look fed. Good."

He wore our father's apron. Ink sat on the first joints of his fingers, the way it sits on men who sign things all day. And on the bench, at his elbow, a pattern-plate with wards I had filed at twenty-three, in the Division, on the Bureau's iron. My wards, at my father's bench, in a Bureau vise.

"What are you cutting?"

"Bureau order." He never once lied to me. He kept things, the way men keep things, at the size of forms, where nobody has to call them lies. "Vault keys." He turned the lamp down a notch, which in our family is what we do instead of shouting.

I took my gloves off and laid them on the side bench, by my files. My files. Oiled, every one, seated in their slots. My father's rule was a season between oilings, no longer, and Tobin had kept my father's rule through a year of my being gone. He had sold me and kept my tools. I looked for the form that could hold both of those, and there isn't one, and I stopped looking.

"They came the week after we buried Mother," he said, at the bench, not at me. "Two men and a folder. The shop's name on the top line. A line at the bottom. The man said the bottom line was mine or the magistrate's, my pick, and he was kind about it, Mira. He offered me tea."

"The letters."

"Before that. They brought your letters in a folder. Somebody had underlined the measurements in red and put little flags in the margins. They asked was the hand yours. I said yes. They asked were the tolerances yours. I said yes. They thanked me. Like I'd done a civic duty."

"You gave them my letters."

"I confirmed my sister's hand to strangers." He picked up the file and put it down again. "And they went away happy, and the shop stayed open, and you were eleven months gone and listed taken. Taken, like a fever takes a person. They had a doctor's word for it. On a form."

"Those are my wards on your plate."

"They issued them to me as a work order. I asked why the Bureau wanted shop iron for vault keys. The man said the Division's benches were full. The Division's benches are never full. I know full benches. They wanted my hands on your wards for the record, Mira. A photograph would have done it. They wanted a signature."

The lamp hissed a little. That was the wick, not him.

"Then they brought drawings," he said. "A key with no door on this earth to fit it. Big bow. Seven pins. Wards like cathedral windows. I asked what it opened. The man said, 'Nothing yet.' And he laughed, and I cut it anyway, because by then refusing looked like knowing, and knowing looked like guilt."

"You cut it."

"I cut it." He put his hands flat on the bench. "Say the rest for me, so it comes from the bench and not from a file."

"There was a page in with the drawings. Not mine, they said. Not theirs either, they said. A kitchen. A long table, bench on the near side, chairs on the far. A door with five panels. A lamp on a hook by the stove. I never described any kitchen to any man alive." He looked up. "Do you know it?"

I knew it. The lamp on its hook. Five panels. I had oiled the hook's hinge my first week in that house, without being asked, and nobody had thanked me, and I had liked that better than thanks.

"I know it."

"Then somebody inside that house talks to them." His voice went down where the filings were. "And it isn't you, and it isn't me, and I don't sleep now, and I trim lamps for one."

He slid the page across the bench. "They had three copies. I kept the one handled the most. Take it. I'd rather it lived in your house than in theirs."

"You kept a page from the Aegis Bureau."

"I kept my sister's kitchen," he said. "Whatever it is."

"The vault," I said.

"Moves on the dark of the moon. Tomorrow. They don't tell me where. They pay me and keep me small." He wiped his hands on the apron our father mended twice. "Come in with me at first light. Testified. Delisted. I'll stand beside you while you sign, and I'll watch the clerk the whole time so he can't add lines. We lose the shop—"

"We."

"It's your shop. It was always going to be your shop. You're the better bench hand. Father said so, and he never said anything twice."

I chose that moment. At the bench, out loud, where he could hear the sound it made.

"I'm not coming back to the shop, Tobin. I'm not going back to the Bureau either. I chose that house in the spring, and I choose it again tonight, standing at your bench with my own wards under your lamp. I stay."

"You were a form in a drawer to those people. One form, one drawer. I watched it happen."

"You were watching. You never once asked me what I saw." My voice stayed level, which cost me. "A man said the cost out loud before he asked me for anything. Nobody at the Bureau ever did. That was the whole of my conversion. It was enough."

He stood a while with his hands flat on the bench, and the filings sat where they sat, and neither of us swept them.

"You sent coin every month," he said at last. "Coin doesn't trim lamps. I read your letters like they'd answer, all winter, and then men with a folder taught my sister's hand to strangers."

"Keep the bench. When your key turns on that door that opens on nothing, don't walk through it. Send for me."

"Where?"

"You know every door in this city our father cut. I'll know how to be found."

At the shop door I put my hand on the frame, where his height marks were, where mine had stopped climbing. The bell gave its two notes behind me. Wind first, customer second.

The cisterns held two sleeping watchers. Whatever the Bureau pays for watching, it bought sleep.

Drevik stood at the seam with the door open on the cold and the orb burning low and the crystals in the mitre drifting the way they drift when he is being patient on purpose. He did not ask what my brother said.

"Did it cost?" he said.

"Yes."

"Good," he said. "Cheap reunions lie."

I tucked my gloves in my belt and held out my bare hand. "Cut me onto the ring."

He unhooked a fold of the vestment and set the blank on my palm, and his hand stayed under mine one breath longer than the trade needed. The blank was warm from being carried against him, and polished at the ears from being carried long. Longer than my half season of weighing it.

"How long have you had it?"

"Since you oiled the pantry hinge without being asked," he said. "I keep everything. It's the only archive I run."

"Say the cost of the ring." He never takes without saying. I wanted to hear it once when it was me.

"By spring the Bureau lists you hostile. The shop forfeits. Your brother signs or hangs by association. Your name goes into their ledgers and stays."

"They had my name in their ledgers a year," I said. "It kept badly. Keep it here."

"I'll keep it," he said. No smirk. Plain.

We went in. I closed my hand around the blank, and it held his warmth the length of the crossing, and I did not open my hand again until the hearth. Behind us the seam shut to a line, and the line to nothing, and the frost stayed on his sleeve like ivy from the wrong season. He let the kitchen's warmth alone. He stood in the doorway a moment, taking nothing. Sera keeps count of such things. He calls them nothing.

When he came into the kitchen, the room did what rooms do when he enters: everything turned, filings to a magnet. The kettle first, then the lamp, then us.

Halla had bread down and dripping cut. She put a plate in front of me and went back to the stove without watching, which from Halla is a rite. Through the garden door Wren hummed over her seed trays, mid-night outside and late afternoon in here, that hour, always.

Sera held out her hand. I put the blank in it, warm side down. She turned it over twice, the way she turns a psalter page, and gave it back.

"Out loud?"

"At the door."

"Then it's said." She lifted the ring off its nail and laid it flat across both palms a moment, and hung it back. "The house grew you a room in your third week of staying and held it dark on your word. Third door past the library. It put in a chimney. Go see if the vise fits."

"There's a vice wants carrying up from the cellar," Halla said from the stove.

I carried my plate to the basin first and ate everything on it standing. In spring I had argued flour weights with that woman to the gram, every night. Since then I had done her table's arithmetic: what she gives away costs her, and she gives it anyway. The numbers do not close. You call that love and stop auditing.

I carried the vice.

The room past the library had a chimney that drew, a bench of gray Fold-wood, and the light at its long slant. I set the vice at the bench's left end, where my father would have set it, and put the kitchen page under the lamp's foot.

The page had been handled by gloves, and by them, once, by a bare hand. Thumb-heavy. Working crease. Bureau hands are gloved; I cut keys for that Bureau long enough to know its gloves. I know the hand-widths of everyone I love, and this was none of ours. The page had been out of the Bureau before it came to the Bureau. I laid it face-down and got to work.

I built a detector, because a lock that refuses a wrong key tells you nothing, and a lock that tells you is a friend. Bolt, levers, the small toothed lever that trips when a stranger's key over-lifts it. False gates, cut exactly where my own wards would have put true gates — because the Bureau cuts from my tolerances, and I know how my tolerances lie. A curtain to blunt the light. I filed, hardened, quenched, oiled.

Then I set the lock's wards to take the four keys before I cut the fifth. Wren's slid through, silk-worn bow. Vesper's went in slow and came out slow, blade oil, no argument. Sera's turned like a page. Halla's turned best of all. I learned each one the way you learn a hand before you cut its key, and that is the whole of how a stranger becomes a household: measure twice, keep everything, hang nothing crooked.

Then I cut mine to follow them. Seven pins. The bow filed where my thumb sits, the way a key should know its hand. I threaded it onto the ring beside Wren's. Five keys. No blank. I hung the ring back on its nail in the kitchen, where the house would find it in the morning light that is always late afternoon.

The lock took my key the way a latch takes a voice it knows. One click. The bolt went home.

Tobin had said the vault moves on the dark of the moon, and the moon's dark was tomorrow, and somewhere men with a jar were growing a door toward nothing, with a key my brother cut and wards I filed at twenty-three. Vesper kept the watch at the hall where the lock would hang. I meant to be under that door before their vault turned a wheel, and after that the year could bring what it liked.

I put out the lamp. The light in the high window went on being late afternoon, because here it always is, and somewhere it was the dark of the moon.

My hands were steady.
