Chapter 1: New Duties

Chapter One: New Duties

The shadow swallowed him whole.

Drevik Markinsoul stepped through the darkness between one heartbeat and the next, feeling the familiar cold kiss of the Shadowlands brush against his skin before he emerged on the other side of the ritual chamber. The temperature dropped twenty degrees in an instant. Candle flames flickered as his arrival displaced the air.

Three Elders watched him from their curved seats carved into the stone walls. Their faces remained half-hidden in the perpetual twilight that clung to this underground sanctuary, but Drevik could feel their eyes assessing him. Measuring. Judging.

"Acolyte Markinsoul." The voice of Elder Thornwood—Lexi's grandmother—carried the weight of centuries. "You stand before us having survived your first year. Few initiates can claim the same."

Drevik kept his posture straight, hands clasped behind his back. The green-gold eyes that marked him as unusual even among the Order met each Elder's gaze in turn. "I had good teachers."

A soft sound from somewhere behind him. He didn't need to turn to know Lexi had made it—a small exhale that might have been amusement or annoyance. With her, the difference was often academic.

"Flattery." Elder Marsh's voice dripped with skepticism. "How... expected."

"Truth," Drevik countered, keeping his tone respectful but firm. "The Order doesn't coddle its members. Anything I've learned came from those willing to teach."

Silence stretched through the chamber. The shadows in the corners seemed to deepen, reaching toward him before retreating. Since his initiation, Drevik had noticed the shadows responded to him differently. They recognized something in him that he was still trying to understand.

Elder Thornwood rose from her seat, descending the three steps to stand before him. Up close, the family resemblance to Lexi was unmistakable—the same auburn hair shot through with silver, the same sharp green eyes that seemed to see through every defense. But where Lexi's features held a fierce warmth, her grandmother's remained carved from ice.

"Your abilities have grown faster than any of us anticipated," she said, circling him slowly. "Your shadow-walking has become almost instantaneous. Your veil sight extends further than any acolyte in fifty years. And then there's the other matter."

She stopped in front of him, her gaze boring into his.

"The glimpses of possible futures. The paths that might unfold."

Drevik said nothing. That particular talent had manifested during the crisis that ended his initiate year—moments where reality seemed to split, showing him consequences before they occurred. He still didn't fully understand it.

"We have discussed your advancement at length," Elder Thornwood continued. "The Order's path is not for everyone. Some remain acolytes for decades before they are ready for the next step. Others..." She paused, her expression unreadable. "Others rise faster than wisdom might recommend."

"Grandmother." Lexi's voice cut through the chamber. "If you have something to say about Drevik's worthiness—"

"I was speaking of history, granddaughter. Not the boy before us." Elder Thornwood's tone carried a sharp warning. "Your defense of him is noted. As is your... attachment."

Drevik felt heat creep up the back of his neck. The bond forming between him and Lexi had become common knowledge in the Order, though neither of them had put a name to it yet. Heart bonds were rare—deep magical connections that tied two members together in ways that went beyond ordinary romance.

Elder Thornwood returned to her seat, her movements precise and controlled. "We have reached a decision. Drevik Markinsoul, you will retain your rank as Acolyte, but your duties will expand. You will take on solo patrols of the campus perimeter. You will report directly to the council on any breach activity. And you will be assigned a permanent partner for field operations."

Drevik's chest tightened. A permanent partner meant the Order was taking his development seriously. It meant they saw a future for him beyond mere survival.

"Lexi Thornwood." Elder Thornwood's voice carried no trace of favoritism. "You have requested this assignment since Drevik's initiation. Your request is granted."

"Requested?" Drevik turned before he could stop himself.

Lexi stepped forward from the shadows where she'd been standing, her athletic form emerging into the candlelight. She wore the practical combat gear of a field acolyte—dark pants, fitted jacket, boots designed for silent movement. Her auburn hair was pulled back in a tight braid, and her green eyes held a challenge that made something in his chest tight.

"Someone has to keep you alive," she said, her tone carefully neutral. "Your luck won't hold forever."

"My luck has been doing fine so far."

"Which is exactly what someone with bad luck would say." She moved to stand beside him, close enough that he caught the faint scent of something floral beneath the leather and weapon oil. "Face forward, Acolyte. The ceremony isn't over."

Elder Vane—the third member of the council—stepped forward holding a small wooden box. She was younger than the others, perhaps only in her fifties, with dark hair and sharp features that her daughter Seraphina had inherited. The resemblance was striking, though where Seraphina's beauty held an ethereal quality, her mother's was purely intimidating.

"Kneel," Vane commanded.

Drevik went to one knee on the cold stone floor. The position felt natural now, though it had seemed strange a year ago. The Order's rituals had a way of becoming familiar.

The box opened with a soft creak. Inside, on a bed of black velvet, lay a silver pin shaped like a crescent moon wrapped in shadow—the symbol of the Acolyte, but larger than his old one. More ornate. The metal seemed to absorb the candlelight rather than reflect it.

"This marks your authority within the Order," Vane said, lifting the pin. "It binds you to our purpose. It grants you access to chambers you have never seen. And it marks you as one who has proven worthy of trust." She fixed him with a cold stare. "Do not make us regret this."

"I won't." The words came out steady, though his heart hammered against his ribs.

The pin pressed into his chest, just above his heart. A flash of cold, then heat, then something that felt like magic sinking into his skin. He gasped before he could stop himself as the enchantment took hold.

"Rise, Acolyte Markinsoul. Your duties begin at sundown."

The afternoon sun felt strange after the perpetual twilight of the underground chamber. Drevik emerged through the hidden entrance behind the mathematics building, blinking against the brightness. Students crossed the quad in clusters, laughing, arguing, living their ordinary lives with no idea what existed beneath their feet.

Sometimes he envied them.

"You're brooding again."

He didn't jump. Barely. Lexi had come up behind him with the kind of silence that came from years of training. She fell into step beside him as he walked toward the student union, her presence both comforting and irritating in equal measure.

"I'm not brooding. I'm thinking."

"Same thing, where you're concerned." She matched his pace easily, her longer legs eating up the distance. "You've been an acolyte for twenty minutes and you're already retreating into your head."

"I'm processing."

"You're stalling." She grabbed his arm, pulling him to a stop near a cluster of oak trees. The touch sent electricity up his arm despite the layers of fabric between them. "Talk to me, Drevik. What's bothering you?"

He met her gaze, struck as always by the intensity there. Lexi didn't do anything halfway—not her training, not her duties, and definitely not whatever this thing was between them.

"The permanent assignment," he said finally. "You requested it."

"Obviously."

"When?"

"Does it matter?"

"It might." He studied her face, looking for cracks in her carefully controlled expression. "You didn't tell me you were going to ask for this."

"What would you have said if I had?"

He opened his mouth to answer, then closed it again. The truth was complicated. Being assigned to Lexi full-time meant spending most of his waking hours with her. It meant trusting her with his life on a regular basis. It meant the tension that had been building between them all year would have nowhere to go.

"I don't know," he admitted.

"Then I made the right choice not asking." Her hand was still on his arm. She seemed to realize it at the same moment he did, because she pulled back quickly. "Besides, someone has to keep you from walking into Shadowlands traps. Your future-sight might show you possibilities, but it doesn't make you invincible."

"I never thought it did."

"You act like it." She started walking again, forcing him to catch up. "Every patrol last month, you took point. Every potential breach, you stepped in front of me like I'm some fragile initiate who needs protecting."

"Maybe I just have good instincts."

"Maybe you have a death wish." She shot him a glare that would have terrified anyone else. "We're partners now, Drevik. Officially. That means you trust me to watch your back. You don't treat me like I'm going to break."

The frustration in her voice carried layers he was still learning to parse. Lexi had been raised in the Order—born to it, trained from childhood, expected to excel. Having an outsider like Drevik come in and show natural talent had to sting, even if she'd never admit it.

"I trust you," he said quietly. "I wouldn't be standing here if I didn't."

Her steps slowed. For a moment, the mask slipped, and he saw something vulnerable beneath the fierce exterior. It was gone before he could be sure it had ever existed.

"Good." Her voice was rough. "Then let's make sure you survive long enough for that trust to mean something. Patrol starts in two hours. I'll meet you at the eastern entrance."

"Lexi—"

"Don't." She cut him off with a look. "Whatever you're about to say, save it. We have a job to do. Personal feelings can't factor in."

She walked away before he could respond, her stride quick and purposeful. He watched her go, acutely aware that she hadn't actually denied anything. She'd just told him to stop talking about it.

Personal feelings. As if what was growing between them could be so easily categorized.

The eastern entrance to the Order's underground complex sat behind the old library, concealed within a maintenance shed that most students had learned to ignore. Drevik arrived twenty minutes early, his new acolyte pin a constant pressure against his chest.

He'd spent the last two hours trying to prepare for patrol. His dorm room had become a strange space since his initiation—ordinary textbooks and posters sharing space with grimoires and magical components he was still learning to use. He'd changed into the field gear all acolytes wore for patrol: dark pants with reinforced knees, a fitted shirt that allowed movement, boots designed for silent running. A belt of small tools hung at his waist, each one designed for dealing with Shadowlands breaches.

The shed's door opened before he could reach for it.

"You're early." Lexi stood in the doorway, backlit by the dim maintenance lights. "Eager or nervous?"

"Prepared."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive." She stepped aside to let him in, then closed the door behind them. In the darkness, her presence filled the small space. "First patrol as official partners. We should establish ground rules."

"Ground rules?"

"How we operate in the field." She moved to the back wall, pressing a sequence of stones that caused a hidden panel to slide open. "Communication is key. If you see something, you tell me. If you sense something, you tell me. If you have one of your visions—"

"I tell you. I've got it."

"You say that, but in the field, you tend to act first and report later." She descended the stairs, her voice echoing off the stone. "That works for solo operations. For partnered work, it gets people killed."

The familiar chill of the underground wrapped around them as they went deeper. But this time, instead of stopping at the acolyte chambers, Lexi kept going—past the training rooms, past the council chambers, into sections Drevik had never seen.

"Where are we going?"

"Perimeter access." She didn't slow. "The campus has specific points where the barrier between here and the Shadowlands grows thin. Patrols check each one, reinforce the seals, and report any anomalies. Simple enough in theory."

"And in practice?"

She glanced back at him, a grim smile touching her lips. "In practice, nothing is ever simple."

They emerged into a long corridor that stretched in both directions, its walls covered in symbols that seemed to shift when Drevik looked directly at them. His new pin grew warmer against his chest, responding to the magic saturating the space.

"These are the old paths," Lexi explained, stopping at a junction. "Built into the foundation of the university when it was founded. The Order has been here longer than the school."

"I know. They taught us history during initiate training."

"Knowing and seeing are different things." She turned left, leading him down a narrower passage. "Most students walk over these tunnels every day without realizing. The veil hides us from ordinary sight. But some can sense the shadows beneath their feet. They just don't understand what they're feeling."

They reached a door made of something that looked like black glass. Lexi pressed her palm against it, and the surface rippled like water.

"Beyond this point, we're technically in the Shadowlands. A thin slice of it, anyway, running parallel to the campus." She pulled her hand back. "Stay close. Don't touch anything you don't recognize. And if you feel something watching you, don't react."

"That's... unsettlingly specific."

"Welcome to patrol." She stepped through the rippling surface.

Drevik followed, pushing through the strange sensation of the barrier giving way around him. The world on the other side was darker—not black, but a deep purple twilight that seemed to glow from no visible source. The air tasted different here, thicker, carrying scents that didn't exist in the mortal realm.

"This way." Lexi's voice had dropped to barely above a whisper. "Stay on the path."

He could see it now—a faint line of lighter darkness running along the ground, marking safe passage. On either side, the shadows moved with purpose, shapes forming and dissolving in ways that defied normal physics.

"The barrier thins here because of the library," Lexi explained, moving with practiced ease. "Books carry knowledge. Knowledge carries power. Power attracts attention."

"From the Shadowlands?"

"From everything." She stopped at a junction where three paths converged. "This is the first checkpoint. Feel along the barrier. Tell me if anything seems wrong."

Drevik closed his eyes, reaching out with the sense that had developed over the past year. The Shadowlands pressed against his awareness, vast and cold and hungry. But there was something else—a rhythmic pulse of magic, like a heartbeat, marking the boundary between realms.

The seal was intact. He could feel it humming, strong and steady.

"Nothing unusual," he reported.

"Good. Let's keep moving."

They worked their way through five more checkpoints, each one requiring Drevik to test his senses against the barrier. The work was methodical, almost boring, but he forced himself to stay alert. Complacency got people killed. He'd learned that lesson the hard way.

At the seventh checkpoint, something felt different.

Drevik stopped, his hand raised to signal Lexi. The barrier here didn't pulse the same way—it stuttered, its rhythm uneven, like a heart struggling to beat.

"There's something wrong with this seal."

Lexi was at his side immediately, her shoulder pressing against his as she reached out to feel what he was feeling. The contact was practical, necessary for sharing magical senses, but it still sent warmth spreading through his chest.

"I feel it." Her voice was tight. "It's not broken, but it's strained. Something's pushing against it from the other side."

"Should we reinforce it?"

"That's not our job. We mark the location and report to the Wardens." She pulled a small crystal from her belt, pressing it against the nearest wall. The crystal glowed briefly, then faded. "Location marked. Let's move."

They continued through the shadow paths, checking three more checkpoints without incident. Drevik was beginning to relax when Lexi suddenly stopped, her body going rigid.

"Do you feel that?"

He reached out with his senses again, expecting another damaged seal. Instead, what he found made his blood run cold.

A heartbeat.

Not the magical pulse of a barrier seal, but an actual heartbeat—coming from inside the Shadowlands.

"That's impossible," he whispered. "Nothing living exists in the Shadowlands. Not really living."

"I know." Lexi's hand moved to the blade at her hip. "But it's there. I can feel it too."

They stood in the purple twilight, two acolytes staring into a darkness that had suddenly become much more dangerous. The heartbeat continued, steady and strange, coming from somewhere beyond the safe path.

"We need to report this." Drevik's voice came out steadier than he felt. "Now."

"Agreed." Lexi didn't argue, which told him how serious she found the situation. "Back the way we came. Quick, but quiet."

They moved through the shadow paths at double speed, checking corners before turning, watching the darkness for movement. The heartbeat followed them—not getting closer, but not fading either. It was simply there, a presence that shouldn't exist in a realm where nothing truly lived.

The black glass door appeared ahead, its surface the only bright thing in the purple darkness. Lexi reached it first, pressing her palm against the surface—

Nothing happened.

"Lexi?"

"It's not opening." She pressed harder, her face pale. "The door isn't responding."

Drevik tried his own palm against the glass. The barrier rippled but didn't give, as if something was holding it closed from the other side.

"Something's blocking us."

"I can see that." She drew her blade, the metal gleaming strangely in the purple light. "We need another way out."

"There isn't one. These paths were designed for access, not escape."

"Then we make our own." She turned to face the darkness behind them. "Shadow walking. Can you do it under pressure?"

"I've never tried."

"First time for everything." She stepped closer, close enough that he could see the flecks of gold in her green eyes. "I'll anchor you. Focus on the library. We came through near the library. If you can shadow walk us there—"

"The library is on the other side of campus from the main entrance."

"Doesn't matter. Shadow walking doesn't follow physical distance. It follows shadow connections." Her hand found his, her fingers interlacing with his own. "I've seen you do this. You're faster than anyone. Stronger. You just have to trust yourself."

The heartbeat pulsed behind them, closer now. Drevik could feel it in his bones, a rhythm that called to something inside him.

He closed his eyes. Pictured the library—the old stone building, the shadows that pooled in its corners, the darkness beneath its stacks. He felt Lexi's hand in his, her presence a warm anchor against the cold.

The shadows wrapped around them.

And Drevik pulled.

They emerged in the library's basement, collapsed against each other between two rows of forgotten archives. Drevik's head spun, his body aching from the effort of moving two people through the shadow paths at once.

Lexi was breathing hard beside him, her hand still gripping his. She hadn't let go.

"You did it," she gasped. "You actually did it."

"Was there doubt?"

"With that much power? No." She turned her head to look at him, her green eyes wide. "I've never felt anyone move that kind of distance with a passenger. The Wardens can't even do that."

The heartbeat echoed in his memory, that impossible rhythm coming from a realm where nothing lived. It had followed them—pressed against his awareness as they moved through the darkness—but it hadn't caught them.

"What was that?" he asked. "That heartbeat. It wasn't natural."

"No. It wasn't." Lexi finally released his hand, pushing herself to a sitting position. "I've read about things in the Shadowlands. Creatures that take forms we recognize. But a heartbeat implies..."

"Life."

"Which shouldn't exist there." She ran a hand over her face. "We need to report this. Now. The council needs to know."

They made their way out of the basement, through the library's main floor, and into the evening air. The sun had set while they were underground, leaving the campus lit by scattered lamps and the moon rising overhead.

The moon. Drevik felt its pull immediately, his enhanced senses responding to the lunar phase. The Order drew power from the moon—its cycles affected everything they did.

"Three days until the full moon," Lexi said, noticing his distraction. "Our power will peak then. If something is trying to break through the barriers..."

"It would choose the full moon as its best chance."

"Exactly." She started walking toward the hidden entrance. "We need to move. Council's going to want a full report."

Drevik followed, his mind racing. A heartbeat in the Shadowlands. Damaged seals. Being trapped in the shadow paths. None of it made sense, and all of it pointed to something larger.

His first day as a full acolyte, and already the world was getting more complicated.

But as he watched Lexi's determined stride ahead of him, as he felt the weight of his new pin against his chest, he knew one thing for certain.

Whatever was coming, he wouldn't face it alone.

They reached the maintenance shed just as another figure emerged from the shadows. Seraphina Vane stepped into the moonlight, her dark hair loose around her shoulders, her expression unreadable.

"Lexi. Drevik." Her voice carried that ethereal quality that made everything she said sound like part of a prophecy. "The council is assembled. They're waiting for your report."

"News travels fast," Drevik observed.

"News of blocked paths and damaged seals travels faster." Seraphina's eyes met his, a strange intensity in their depths. "You felt it, didn't you? The heartbeat?"

Lexi's head snapped toward the other woman. "You knew about it?"

"I felt something earlier. A stirring in the Shadowlands." Seraphina's gaze hadn't left Drevik. "But I couldn't identify it. You saw it more clearly. You always do."

There was something in her tone—interest, perhaps, or curiosity. Drevik couldn't quite read it. His brief interactions with Seraphina during his initiate year had left him with more questions than answers.

"The council," Lexi interrupted, her voice sharp. "We need to report."

"Of course." Seraphina stepped aside, gesturing toward the hidden entrance. "After you, Acolytes. I believe you have quite a story to tell."

Drevik moved past her, acutely aware of both women's presence. The night had barely begun, and already it felt like the world was shifting beneath his feet.

As he descended into the Order's depths, he couldn't shake the feeling that the heartbeat was still echoing in his bones—steady, patient, waiting.

Something was coming.

And for the first time since his initiation, he wasn't sure he was ready for it.
