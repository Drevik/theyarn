Chapter Four: The Final Quest

Mira cut the pin at dawn, when the void-glass was coldest and least likely to flatter her. Ten files. She kept the count out loud, under her breath, the way the Bureau had taught: number held against the work, work held against the number. The filings came off black and lay gray in the tray, like light that had been spent.

The pin was half the joint of her little finger, cut to a tolerance she could state and would not show. A tolerance shown is a tolerance beaten. That had been her whole religion for eleven years. Then the Fold had grown her a workshop with a bench and a lamp, and the religion had a church, and she distrusted how much she liked the church.

Down the hall, Wren hummed at her hem, one note held long. From the training hall came wood on wood, patient, patient: Vesper at drill. The kitchen was warm. The kitchen was always warm. In eleven days Mira had not found the fire that kept it so, and she had stopped looking, and she noted that she had stopped looking, which was not the same as being at ease.

On the bench lay her list. Fourteen doors. She had measured every one her first week and filed the survey under EXITS, because a woman new to any house owes herself an escape audit. The Fold's doors had no keyholes on the inside. She had written that down too. Since then she had oiled fourteen sets of hinges and re-seated two pins in the garden door, and the survey was still filed under EXITS, and no escape audit needs oiling.

Breakfast was laid and not eaten. Drevik stood at the head of the long table with his staff across his shoulders, and every hand at the table found its work. Sera set the censer where his hand would fall. Wren took up a hem and kept it taut. Vesper sat with her back not touching the chair. Mira drank her tea, because a hinge that rattles is a hinge replaced.

"Osen Toll," he said. "Nine days ago the eighth bell stopped mid-swing. Not cracked. Not dropped. The swing is still up there, waiting to finish. The town stands under the tower at the hour it should have finished, and then goes home."

Nobody asked what the bell was waiting for.

"Under the tower is shade with nothing to cast it. A cart-wheel across, and a hand wider every day. A drover off the high pasture tallied forty sheep into that shade on the market road and thirty-nine out the far side. No blood. No dog. No carcass. He has stopped driving that road. He has not stopped counting."

Mira set her cup down. Sheep into shade and fewer out was a ledger that would not close, and ledgers that would not close were the only thing that had ever frightened her at her work.

"The toll-house comes up short three nights running. The coins are all there. The total is not. And there is a child." The table was already quiet; everyone had read the rider's face last night, if not his report. "A girl, six. She comes when her mother calls supper. The town can't say her name. It went out of their mouths the way the swing went out of the bell: up, and not coming down. Her mother calls her daughter now."

He said the name. "Tansy." He said it twice, unhurried, so the table would hold it, the way you seat a pin so the mechanism can't forget it. "While I can say it, she still has it. That's the work. Give the town back its count and its name, and shut the mouth both fell into.

"Ansel asked me last night if this was the final quest. There's no such animal. There are debts that stop moving, and even those get audited. Now the cost, so no one can say I didn't." He had a saying — no hope taken, no promise given, without the cost said out loud — and he had never once written it down, because he had never once written anything down, and his enemies called that unguarded, and that was the trick.

"The Nought isn't like the others. The Choir wanted to be heard. The Tide wanted in. The Blazon wanted the law to be a weapon, and the Bureau wanted the world in a form with fields. The Nought is the form under all of them. It doesn't want. It files. It doesn't push at the door; it takes the door's memory of being shut. Every mouth is a threshold that lost its latch, and you can't re-hang a latch from the side the pressure comes on. So I go around, out past the seam, and I hang a new one. And a latch wants, at its heart, one thing that cannot be argued out of its size."

He did not look at her when he said it. He had said as much at supper, with less detail, and she had not slept, and the pin had come out of that, and this morning he did not look at her, and that was how she knew he knew.

"The cost is three. One: the bell. A latch should be a thing that was always meant to hold, and a bell was always meant to stop. It will finish that toll, nine days late, once, and be spent. Osen Toll gets the name back and loses its eighth hour forever. I'll say that to the town's wardens first, and they can stop me if they think it's a bad trade.

"Two: warmth. Standing where the file is finest costs warmth. Some grows back. Some Sera grows back. The rest we live with, and no one is to grieve me in advance. Grief in advance is hope badly spent.

"Three: while the mouth stands open I can't spare the door. The Fold shuts behind me. No one in, no one out, till the latch hangs. If I'm slow, be patient. If I'm quick, be patient anyway."

"It should have an escort," Vesper said.

"It notices edges. A blade is an edge."

"Your staff is an edge."

"It's a key," he said. "It can't count keys."

He turned. "And the road out will want your law, Ninth—" He stopped.

"Vesper," said Vesper.

Sera did not look up from the censer. "Vesper. You gave her the name. Keep it in use."

The smirk came late and sat crooked. "Vesper. Forgive me. The door was loud today."

Mira wrote that in her head, where she kept what did not fit the forms. A tumbler hanging in a mechanism she had been told held every word ever said to it and lost none. In a lock, that is wear. In a lock, you ream the chamber, seat a new pin, and the lock forgets it ever stuck. She did not know what you did with a man, and she did not like not knowing.

"While you're out," Sera said, "we're the hinges."

"You always were," he said.

After the table she went down to the vestibule to check the strike of the great door, which needed nothing, which was why she checked it.

Steward Ansel was at the threshold with a knotted cord, pacing it, logging the width.

"Sister," he said. He was tidy, and devoted, and a man who had appointed himself indispensable, which are three different things that can share a coat. "For the registers, Sister. The Ascendant keeps no clerks. Someone must."

The Bureau's term for a house measured without its owner's hand on the cord was target file. She said none of it.

The courier came while he was still measuring: a Bureau rider, road dust on perfect stationery, the seal pressed a little too well. The form requested leave to attend upon the Communion's registers and effects, during the Ascendant's absence. It was signed Haldane, Matron of Records, and below the signature, in the small field:

P.S. The Bureau keeps its children's files open. Yours was never closed.

She read it twice. Absence had been said at the hearth-table, to the household, not twelve hours past. The nearest Bureau house was a week by road. The form had come in one night.

Someone had written it before the words finished.

She put it in her pocket, where a keeper keeps what she means to answer on her own schedule. She told Ansel nothing, because the form asked for registers, and there were no registers, and there was exactly one man in the Communion making some, and he was standing at that moment, logging the width of the door the household was about to be shut behind.

Before the door, there was the order of giving. Nothing in that house went from a woman's hand to his that had not first lain in Sera's. A mended key went through Sera. A hem went through Sera. Bread went through Sera. Mira had thought about going around that once, her first week, and had found she did not want to, and had filed the not-wanting under EXITS, where she filed everything she could not yet say.

So she took the pin to the shrine-room. The altar held nothing, and the nothing was swept. Sera turned the pin once in the warm ash of the censer and handed it back.

"Hearth ash," Sera said. "Let it go out warm and come home warm."

"It's a tool," Mira said, and took it with both hands.

"Tools come home too," Sera said, and watched her, and whatever the watching was, it was a gate, and the gate opened.

He was in the doorway of the shrine-room when she turned, mask on, the crystals of the mitre making their slow crossings above him, and the small room went to him the way filings go to a magnet. Under the gold seams of the mask his eyes were night with the coins still in it, and she had stopped pretending, eleven days ago, not to have noticed.

"Void-glass," she said, before he could ask. "It keeps its size in any cold. Out there, nothing can argue it thinner."

"Why a quarter past finger-tight?"

"Because the housing will be iron, and iron lies about its size in the cold. Set to today's weather, and tomorrow the pin rattles in its chamber. You cheat the setting toward tomorrow's weather, not today's."

"Good," he said. "Now say the rest."

"The pin wants its cutter out there. It wants a hand that knows the cheat."

"Mine is the hand," she said.

"The mouth files what it notices, and what it notices first is whatever is most certain of its own size. A hand that knows the cheat would be the brightest thing on that road." He held out his glove, palm up, and did not hurry her. "I'll set it myself. A quarter past. And I'll keep my hands honest by remembering whose tolerance I'm holding them to."

"Don't make a ceremony of it."

"I never make ceremonies," he said. "I make costs."

The smirk arrived then, right on time. She turned away and put out the lamp before it could be witnessed. In the dark that followed, she set the pin in Sera's palm. Sera set it in his glove.

They walked him down to the door at dusk. The wardens had come at noon with the escort carts and been sent home at duskfall; the road past the door did not take carts.

Sera worked the sending. She did not sing to anything. She named the household into the censer-smoke in the order of their joining: Sera, who keeps the ledger of you. Wren, who keeps your note. Vesper, who keeps the law of your road. Mira, who keeps your pins. Her name had a verb in it now. Keeper of pins: the best title the Bureau never printed, and she stood straighter for it, and was annoyed at standing straighter.

Wren's thread went into the hem of his cloak at the last. The thread held the door's note, so that on the road home he could follow the hum of his own hem. "It knows the way home," Wren said, "even if you forget it." Nobody at the door made anything of that, and everyone at the door made something of it.

Vesper gave him the road's law in three lines and made him say them back. Nothing out there is owed. Take nothing offered. Come home by your own name.

At the threshold his voice went plain.

"The Bureau knew," Mira said. She said it the way she set a pin: straight in, no ornament. "The absence. The form came in one night from a week's road. Someone wrote it before you finished saying it."

"I know," he said.

That was all. Vast, kind, and never once fully known: he knew, and he said no more, and she had not expected more, and she hated that she had not expected more.

"Keep the list current," he said. "Keeper of pins. Every door in this house is yours while I'm gone. Anything that opens, count it. Anything that opens without me, tell Sera, and hold it shut until I knock."

"Until you knock," she said.

"Until I knock," he said.

The door opened on the vaulted dark, and the stars over the road were coins nobody had spent. The orb at his staff's head held its light the way a lamp holds oil. He stepped through, and did not look back, and the door shut from the outside, and from within it was a wall with a seam, and the seam went cold.

The kettle found its boil. The bread came up on the second proof. The house went on being a house, which is what houses are for, and for an hour nobody said his name aloud, not because it was lost, but because it was being kept.

In the morning her workshop had grown a lamp-room off the north wall: one bench, one lamp, a rack for files. Rooms grew in the Fold when a woman put down root, and she had told no one she had put down root, and the room told them for her.

The Bureau's form lay on the bench with a field for everything. She filled in none of them. Somewhere east of the seam a bell nine days late waited to finish. A quarter past finger-tight, her work kept its size and held, and down that road a man walked toward a mouth that filed names, with her pin in his glove. She was not afraid. She was counting.

Fourteen doors. Thirteen of them hers to keep. The fourteenth was a wall with a cold seam in it, and a week to hold, and in her pocket a form from a Matron who had known the road before he took it.

He had said his costs out loud, all of them, the way he always did. The world had not said one of its.

She kept a list of those too.
