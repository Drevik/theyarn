The training hall in the Fold smelled of cold iron and shaved cedar. It was a room built for the weight of things, with thick stone walls that did not echo, and floorboards heavy enough to hold a mountain. The light came from iron cups set into niches along those walls, burning something resinous and slow, and it did not fall the way light fell in the world outside the Fold. In here, light pooled. It gathered in the corners like something drinking, and it left the center of the hall in a low amber dusk that made every movement seem older than it was.

The mats were woven rush laid over the heavy boards, gone grey with use, chalked with old dueling circles worn nearly smooth. Vesper watched him from the edge of them.

She had told herself, all the way down the cold corridors, that this was only a spar. Blazon drill. Doctrine made flesh — the way the Ninth Blazon had always settled its disagreements, not with words but with weight. She had told it to herself so many times that the lie had worn smooth against her skin, the way a coin wears smooth in a pocket.

For years they had circled each other. Every duel-of-law worth fighting, she had fought against him. Every argument worth having, she had had with him — across a council table, in a corridor, once in the rain outside the adjudication hall until both their voices went ragged. She knew his doctrine better than she knew her own. She knew his tricks, his silences, the sly tilt of a smirk he had never once turned on anybody else.

She had never, until tonight, let herself ask what that was.

Drevik did not move like a man who expected to be watched. He moved like a man who was already part of the room's geometry. He was stripping the heavy, gold-embroidered outer vestments from his shoulders, leaving him in the dark silk of his undershirt. His movements were efficient, the way a blade-smith cleans a whetstone. No ceremony to it. The vestments were the Blazon's, and the mask was the Blazon's, and the debt was the Blazon's, and she had watched him vest and unvest a hundred times before she understood that she was waiting — had been waiting a long while — to find out whether anything beneath it all was his.

He did not look up when she approached. He only reached for the hilt of his practice staff, a length of weighted ash that caught the low, amber light of the hall.

"You are late, Vesper," he said. He did not turn. "The Ninth Blazon does not wait for the sun to set."

"The Blazon is a ghost in a suit of armor," Vesper said. She wiped the sweat from her neck and stepped onto the mat. "I am here. That is the only truth that matters in this house."

The sweat, at least, was honest. She had spent the hour before this one drilling recruits in the outer yard, working her shoulders past the point of trembling — so that if her hands shook when she finally stood in front of him, she would have something to blame it on. She was a master of the duels-of-law. She knew exactly what a witness was worth. She had arranged her own.

He turned then. The white-ceramic kitsune mask sat high on his brow, the gold accents gleaming like trapped sunlight. His eyes—those vast, star-flecked voids—settled on her. There was no warmth in them, not yet, but there was a recognition. It was the look a hunter gives a familiar hound.

Once, that look had made her want to break something. Tonight it only made her chest ache, which was far worse.

"Then prove the truth," he said.

He offered her a short sword, the blade blunt but the weight real. One of the house's old drill blades — edgeless, nicked by a hundred years of exactly this, its grip wrapped in waxed cord gone dark where ten thousand palms had held it. Vesper took it. The steel was cold, a familiar bite against her palm. She settled into her stance, her weight distributed low, her center finding the floor.

"Terms?" she said.

"There are no terms in the Fold," he said. "Past the Threshold there is no law. There is only what happens, and what is true."

"How convenient for you."

"I didn't build the house." He rolled the staff once across his knuckles, a slow ratchet of sound. "I only hold it down."

And there it was, worn as openly as the vestments he had just taken off. I only hold it down. As if he were a tent-peg. As if he were furniture. As if the debt had already spent the man, and what remained — the mask, the mitre, the anchor's tonnage — was all the Choir had ever wanted and all the world would ever get.

He did not come at her with a flourish. He came with a sudden, terrifying economy. The staff swung in a low arc, aimed at her lead knee. Vesper stepped, a sharp pivot that sent the air whistling past her ear, and brought her blade down in a biting descent.

He caught the strike. The wood of the staff met the flat of her blade with a crack that sounded like a bone breaking. The vibration traveled up her arm, settling in her shoulder.

The walls drank the sound at once. That was the worst of this hall — nothing echoed here. Every impact died the instant it was born, so that a fight happened inside a held breath, private as a confession.

He was smiling. It was the sly, tilted smirk that drove her to the edge of her own discipline.

"Too much anger," he said. "You are fighting the steel, not the man."

"I am fighting the lie you tell yourself," Vesper said. She lunged.

He gave ground the way a rich man gives alms — one measured coin at a time, watching her spend herself to take it. The staff blurred through his hands. He shifted his grips without shifting his feet, and the weighted length of ash moved like something alive that had merely agreed, for the moment, to be a weapon. She cut for his ribs and met nothing; he was already turning inside the arc, his shoulder sliding past her point so close she felt the heat of him through the silk. The butt of the staff cracked against her blade and knocked it a hand's width off line. She recovered on the count of two, breathed on the count of four, and struck again, and again, while the salt ran into her eyes and she blinked it away without breaking rhythm, because the Blazon taught you to bleed on schedule or not at all.

The spar became a conversation of friction. Every time she thought she found a seam in his defense, he closed it. He moved with a relaxed confidence that made the room feel smaller, as if his very presence was consuming the available air. He was a wall of moving shadows and polished white ceramic.

And the whole time, in the quiet place the duels-of-law had trained into her, she was reading him — and what she read made her angry, and the anger made her faster, and the speed made her reckless, and she knew it, and she chose it anyway.

Because here was the thing about the lie: he believed it. He truly believed the man had already been paid out; that the debt had drawn him down to his last coin; that what was left — the mask, the mitre, the weight — was all that anyone, including himself, had any right to hold. He fought like a man defending a house he did not think he lived in. She had crossed blades with masters on three sides of the law, and she had never once wanted to beat anyone so badly, and never once for her own sake.

She pushed harder. She used the footwork of the Blazon, the aggressive, geometric strikes that had made her a master of the duels-of-law. The rush of the mats grabbed at her feet; the pooled light slid down her blade; the sharp smell of cedar rose wherever their heels ground the shavings into the boards. First posture. Then the fourth. Then the ninth — the closing sequence, the one the Blazon used to settle a matter of law, each strike a clause, each angle a signature. She forced him back a single step, her breath coming in short, sharp rasps. For a second, the mask was tilted, and she saw the star-pupils of his eyes widen.

One step. In all the years she had watched him fight, no one had taken one step off him. She wanted to laugh — wanted it with a wildness that frightened her — and she rode the wanting straight through the middle of her discipline, and did not laugh.

She paid for it.

Then the weight hit her. He didn't use the staff to strike; he used it to leverage. He caught her blade, twisted the ash, and stepped into her guard.

The world tilted. The floor vanished.

Vesper hit the mats hard, the air driven from her lungs, her teeth meeting the inside of her cheek so that copper bloomed across her tongue. Before she could roll, he was over her. The heavy weight of his body pinned her, his chest pressing against hers, the dark silk of his shirt rough against her skin. The staff lay forgotten at the edge of the mat.

The hall grew silent. The only sound was the rhythmic, heavy pull of their shared breath.

He was breathing hard, she noticed. He was breathing exactly as hard as she was. The discovery rang in her like a struck bell. He had not been giving her alms at all. He had been paying.

He did not move away. He stayed there, a vast and heavy shadow, pinning her to the earth. The mask was inches from her face. She could see the tiny, drifting void crystals in his mitre, orbiting his head like silent, trapped moons.

"You found the seam," he said. His voice was low, stripped of the trickster's edge. It was the voice of the anchor.

The seam. In his guard, in the geometry, in the myth. She heard all three meanings, and chose to answer all three.

Vesper reached up. Her fingers, calloused from the hilt, brushed the edge of the ceramic mask. She did not pull it away; she only held the threshold. "I found you," she whispered.

For one breath he did not move at all. Then he was the one who crossed it. He lifted the mask from his own brow and set it down on the boards beside them, face toward the ceiling, the gold accents guttering in the pooled light — a thing built for war and ceremony, laid aside like a paid debt.

He leaned down. The movement was slow, a deliberate descent into the space where the law ended and the person began. When his mouth met hers, it was not a soft thing. It was a collision. It tasted of salt and the cold iron of the hall.

She wrapped her arms around his neck, pulling him deeper into her, trying to find the man beneath the myth, the soul beneath the debt. He groaned—a low, guttured sound that vibrated against her ribs—and his hands, large and steady, slid beneath the hem of her tunic.

His skin was warm, a stark contrast to the chill of the room. He moved with a desperate precision, his touch mapping her as if he were recording a history that could never be written down. There was no grace in the struggle, only the raw, unadorned need to be held, to be anchored, to be known.

She had beaten masters of three schools. She had stood in the adjudication hall with the whole weight of the law on her shoulders and felt nothing but her own pulse, steady as a metronome. She had never once in her life been held by something that would not, under any circumstance at all, let the Sundered take her. Her hands found the warm plane of his back and learned it — the old scars like braille, the muscle and heat of him — and above them the void crystals kept their orbits, tiny patient moons attending whatever happened beneath.

In the heat of it, the boundaries of the Fold seemed to pulse. The darkness behind her eyelids was not empty; it was full of him. He was the weight that kept her from drifting into the Sundered; he was the hearth that kept the void at bay.

Afterward, the hall felt larger, and much colder.

They lay on the mats, limbs tangled, the sweat cooling on their skin. Drevik stared up at the ceiling, his breathing leveling into the slow, rhythmic pattern of a man in prayer. The mask sat on the floor beside them, looking like a discarded face.

Vesper watched the rise and fall of his chest. She felt the ache in her muscles, a dull, honest pain. The tension of the day had not vanished, but it had changed shape. It was no longer a blade; it was a weight. At the edge of the mats lay the small ruins of what they had been to each other an hour ago — his vestments folded, by habit, into a neat square, and the staff rolled away to the boards where it had waited out the whole of it.

For a while, neither of them spent anything on words.

"In the duels-of-law," she said at last, "there are terms. Winner takes the case. Loser pays costs." Her voice sounded strange to her — used. "What does the winner take here?"
