The beans wanted another hour. The band wanted to eat now, so Halla lied to them about the beans and paid them the truth in bread.

The ninth band of the Veiled Communion came down from Dunmarl at dusk with ash on their boots and the look of men who had slept three nights under a floor. Eleven bowls went out of her kitchen and eleven came back scoured. She had sent their suppers down to them in a bucket on a rope, noon and dusk, all three days. Nobody had asked her to. The soup was hot and they were under the floor. That was the whole of the arithmetic.

Tolm, who was the band's point and the band's appetite both, reported to the pot between spoonfuls.

"Bins at Dunmarl," he said. "Full wheat, gone to grey ash overnight. No smell, no heat, no rats. The Ascendant went down under the floor and sang the seam shut. Came up grey about the eyes and made jokes about it. Now it's just a floor."

"Third granary since Greenmonth," said the woman at the end of the bench.

Third since Greenmonth. The Sundered had never cared for bread before this year. Something under the world had learned what a winter eats.

Tolm had brought a fistful of the ash wrapped in a rag, because grain men pay for proof. It was fine as mill dust, weighed nothing, smelled of nothing at all. She put two fingers in it. The fingers went cold and stayed cold until the soup was done.

That morning, Renn's clerk had stood in her passage with an offer: silver for her route books. Where the Communion bought. Which roads the stores took. Who carried, who signed. She had asked what a grain factor wanted with another man's bread. The clerk had smiled and said his master collected accounts. She had kept her books and shown him the street. And when Dunmarl's bins went to ash, Renn bought the town's standing grain at grief prices before the bells stopped ringing. Somebody always did. She put the fact away in the back room where she kept facts she could not yet afford.

Nineteen years she had kept the Long Table at Saltcross, where three roads crossed and everything that walked them got weighed. Wakes, weddings, caravans, garrisons: she cooked for any table that was not hers. The trade had taught her one sentence, and the sentence was the whole of it. Everyone eats. Everyone counts. The counting comes after. Her rule was as old as her trade: feed the table, and be elsewhere when the bowls come back to be counted. She had kept the rule so long it had gone from habit to doctrine without ever passing through thought.

He came alone at full dark. The talk stopped. The spoons did not, because the ninth band ate like soldiers, and soldiers eat through anything short of the last trumpet. He stood inside the door with the yard draft behind him and Dunmarl's white ash on his boots, and the whole hall's attention crossed the room to him, and he did nothing with it. He leaned the staff against the wall the way a man leans a hoe.

Half his face was white porcelain, gold at the seams, cunning in the cut. Above the mask his eyes were night-colored, a star pinned small in each pupil. Over those, a mitre of glass with three dark stones going round inside it, unhurried, like bees that had chosen a hive. Black vestments buttoned to the chin, gold thread at the collar, no ornament a workman's eye could not price. She set down the ladle to look at him and picked it up again, because there was soup to stir.

His hands were what she trusted. Big, scarred across the knuckles, kept clean. A man who had carried sacks before he carried whatever he carried now.

"You'll want supper," she said.

"I'll want to settle," he said. "After."

He waited while she served. Nobody waited while Halla served. It unsettled her, small, like help arriving at a full tray unasked.

When the bowls were back she reached for her ledger, and he said, "Fourteen months," and then he said the rest of it out of his own head.

"Eleven sacks of bolted meal the first month after Choir-winter. Nine the next. Eight, and eight again. Dark oil twice, the good jars, unbilled. Forty loaves every fourth day come Thaw, invoiced at nine, and the market paid eleven, and after Dunmarl, twelve. In the ash month your invoice said forty, and fifty-one went out your door, twice, and once fifty-two, and the odd ones never found the invoice. They went into a basket. The beggar with the limp works your street on Thursdays." He said, "And three days of rope suppers, noon and dusk, under Dunmarl. Also unbilled."

Halla kept accounts. It was not pride; it was the floor she stood on. In nineteen years, no one had counted her back. She had been appraised, dunned, and complimented in her time. Nobody had ever remembered her.

"You keep my books," she said.

"I keep no books," he said. "I remember you."

He paid at her scale by the door, with the brass weights her mother had left her, because he had watched long enough to know she trusts a scale and not a palm. Market for every loaf. Grief-price for the ones the invoice missed. He said the margin out loud before she could argue it, which took the argument's legs.

"Forty loaves every fourth day for fourteen months," he said. "And never once a chair."

"I don't cross doors," she said.

"Once," he said. "The cost is an evening, and I'll say the rest out loud. My kitchen has four keepers and not one of them a cook. Your bread holds four days in Saltcross and one day in my house. Come look at the pantry your loaves die in. One supper. You cook it. Sit or stand as you like. The door puts you back in this yard tonight, the evening spent, and no one owing anything."

"Good bread does not stale in a day."

"That's why I'm asking you."

It was not the invitation that moved her. It was the loaf. Bread is honest; it keeps or it doesn't. If his house could make her bread lie, she needed to see the lie happen.

"One supper," she said. "I cook. I don't sit. Back in my yard by midnight."

"Done," he said.

She packed the way she packed for catering: three knives, her salt, the crock of live starter under one arm like a firstborn. She left the keys with the sous girl and banked her own fire. In the yard he set the staff's foot on the cobbles where no door was, and turned it once, and there was a door. Plain wood, black iron latch, standing alone on its hinges in the open air, colder a foot away than the Greenmonth night around it. She went through apron on, as staff, not as guest.

The far side smelled of stone and woodsmoke and proofing dough. The kitchen was large enough to cook for a street, and the hearth in it was burning, and under the fire there was no ash bed at all. Nineteen years of fires, and she had never met one with no ash. A fire with no ash is a fire with no history. She kept her hands off it, the way you keep your hands off another cook's stove. The window over the sink gave on a walled garden holding late-afternoon light. The light did not move. She looked twice. It did not move the second time either.

The stores were racked wrong, and a wrong rack is a slow fire in a pantry, so she had the onions half moved before anyone came. They sat shelved at the flour's height, sweating at it. A woman with a key ring on her hip stopped in the doorway at the sight.

"I filed those," she said. "By form. Signed, shelved, dated."

"You filed them where the flour keeps dry. Onions sweat. In a week your flour will taste of them."

"It took me two hours."

"Your bread will take the taste in two days. Choose."

The woman looked at the onions, then at the flour, and set her jaw. "I'll re-file tonight."

"Move the flour, not the onions. Onions like it high."

Later, at the table, the woman took seconds of the pot without a word to anyone. Mira, someone called her. That was the treaty.

While the pot worked, a woman in grey came to the kitchen doorway smelling faintly of good incense, the kind a church buys when it means it. She watched Halla rack the dried plums and said, "I have prayed for this."

Halla looked up.

"The table," the woman said. "He keeps the door. Nobody keeps the table. We have prayed over the stores for a year as if they were a wound. I'm Sera."

"Halla."

Sera took down a sixth bowl from the shelf before anyone had asked her to. "While you're here," she said, "the little one's proofing falls flat. Wren's. Would you show her your hand?"

No one had asked Halla for her hand in years.

"For an evening," she said.

The table ran the length of the room, scrubbed, unscarred, with more places at it than the household needed. He served them himself, pot to bowl down the row, and took the smallest portion for his own, and ate it standing at the hearth with a heel of her plain loaf and nothing on it. Fourteen months of her loaves had gone through his door sight unseen. This was the first she had watched him eat. He ate the way the poor eat bread: no hurry, no crumb left, full attention on the food.

The sixth bowl went down within her reach at the counter, the one Sera had taken from the shelf, and he had carried it there without a word about it. She was not sitting; she had said she would not. The bowl stood there anyway. It was the first meal in years that anyone had set for her, and she ate it standing and working, angry in a small way, to the last drop.

"Good bread," he said.

"I know," she said.

The porcelain did not move. The visible half of him smiled, once, and it was the least mysterious thing in the house.

She could read a table in one pass. Cardinals, constables, bridegrooms, liars. She could not read him, and it was not the mask doing the hiding.

Wren hummed while she ate, a thread of sound, and put a roll in her sleeve with the ease of old habit, the kind you survive into. Halla set two more rolls at her elbow and said nothing about it, and the humming went up a note. The tall one at the far end ate in small exact cuts and thanked her with a bow from the neck, formal as findings read to a court. Vesper, Sera said, and that was the whole of the introduction.

When the second loaves went out, Halla cut the one her cart had carried through the door two days back, and stopped with the knife in it. The crumb had gone dull and friable, an old loaf's crumb.

"This bread is one day old in the ledger," she said, "and eight days old in the crumb."

Nobody spoke.

"Something in this house eats the fresh out of things."

"I know," he said. "That's why the door asked for you."

Sera said the short grace, the one for bad news. It was four words long. Halla had not known a house could need one.

After the bowls were out she stepped into the garden to breathe. Late afternoon stood in it like a held note. The last table she had kept was small, four stools and a bench. She had fed it to its end, every soul on it, and the end had not been starving. There are worse ways to lose a table, and she had found one, and she had made her rule that year and kept it since, and the rule had kept her.

All evening this table had been trying to keep her. Good tables do that. This was the best she had ever worked. That was why she had to be gone before the counting.

She went in for her apron and he was in the passage holding it, folded. Wrong: eighths, the way soldiers fold. She took it and folded it in thirds, and their hands did not touch, and the not touching lasted longer than a touch would have, and neither of them made anything of it.

"The door, please," she said.

"Yes," he said.

He walked her out himself. The passage out ran longer than the passage in, and she counted doors against habit. Four coming in. Five going out. The fifth stood open with lamplight on its sill.

It was a kitchen. Nobody had cooked in it. The hearth was laid with kindling and cold. The lamp on the sill held no oil. The table was scrubbed birch, standing the height of her hip, and the hearth stood where her hand reached for a hearth, and by the door there was a peg with nothing on it. Nothing smelled of anything.

"It grew during supper," he said.

"The Fold grows when someone joins." She had heard that much of the house's working.

"Yes," he said.

"Nobody joined."

"No," he said.

He said the asking once, cost first, the way he did everything.

"The work is the stores and the long table. The pay: you would never again feed a table that cannot feed you back. The cost: you would be counted. What keeps a house becomes the house. A house can be lost, and everything counted in it lost with it. That's the whole cost. It's the only kind I ask anyone to pay."

"No," she said.

He took the no the way her scale took a weight, at face value, without argument.

"Then it will wait," he said. "Rooms keep."

He turned the staff, and the door stood open on her own dark yard, and the market bell had not yet struck the half. The door had spent none of her night. She went through with the apron under her arm and the crock sleeping against her hip, and the door behind her shut, and the yard was the yard.

She walked home by the market road under the ordinary stars, lit and spent, no account kept of them. Her kitchen was cold and smelled of her own banked fire, and it knew her. She set the sponge for the morning loaves and made herself slow it: cold water, the bowl covered, the long overnight proof, the way you prove bread when you want it to keep. Nineteen years of cooking for tables that were not hers, and she had never once cooked anything to keep.

She hung the apron on its peg and the peg held it, and that was the whole of the peg's work, and she stood a minute being a woman with one apron. Then she banked the fire low and left the lamp lit, and did not ask herself for whom.

In the morning the sponge had risen anyway.

It does that. It does not wait for your no.
