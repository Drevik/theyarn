Core idea

Chapter 10 focuses on the realization that a relationship has fundamentally failed and the necessity of leaving it "well." It addresses the psychological trap of staying in a dynamic where you have transitioned from a partner to an adversary. Using the case study of Marsha and Craig, the chapter illustrates how avoidant deactivating strategies—such as belittling, sexual avoidance, and status ambiguity—can drive a partner into a cycle of protest and eventual indifference. The goal is not to fix the unfixable, but to recognize when the attachment bond has become toxic and to execute a structured departure.

Key concepts and terms

  • The Inner Circle Paradox: The phenomenon where the person physically and emotionally closest to an avoidant partner is often the person treated most poorly. Because the avoidant partner views closeness as a threat to autonomy, they direct their deactivating strategies toward their primary attachment figure.
  • Becoming "The Enemy": A state of relationship decay where you are no longer a teammate but a target. This is characterized by a specific set of red flags involving shame, surveillance, and instability.
  • The Royal Inner Circle: The benchmark of a secure attachment. In this state, the partner is the person whose well-being is prioritized, whose opinion carries the most weight, and who is protected from outside threats.
  • Deactivating Strategies: Subconscious behaviors used by avoidant individuals to create distance, such as withdrawing affection, focusing on flaws, or avoiding commitment.
  • The Rebound Effect: The tendency to return to a dysfunctional relationship ("returning to the scene of the crime") because the familiarity of the pain feels safer than the uncertainty of being alone.
  • Planned Departure: A strategic exit that involves logistical preparation and the mobilization of a support network to prevent the cycle of returning to a toxic partner.

Techniques worth drilling

  1. The "Enemy" Audit

To determine if your relationship has moved from "difficult" to "failed," run a diagnostic on your current level of intimacy.

  • Step 1: Review the following symptoms: Are you ashamed to tell friends how your partner treats you? Are you surprised when others call your partner "sweet"? Do you find yourself eavesdropping on your partner's conversations to gauge their mood or activities? Are you the person most likely to be insulted by them? Do you feel uncertain if they would drop everything in an emergency?
  • Step 2: Tally the "hits." Do not rationalize the behavior as "just a bad week."
  • Step 3: If the tally is high, categorize the relationship as "failed" rather than "fixable."
  1. The Secure Baseline Comparison

Use the "Royal Inner Circle" as a template to measure what you are currently sacrificing.

  • Step 1: List the hallmarks of a secure partner: Your well-being is second to none; you are the first person confided in; your opinion matters most; you are admired and protected.
  • Step 2: Compare these to your current reality. If the gap between your current state and this baseline is wide and persistent, the relationship is no longer providing attachment security.
  1. The Exit Architecture (Planned Departure)

If the audit confirms you are "the enemy," do not wait for a crisis to act. Build the infrastructure for leaving.

  • Step 1: Logistics. Determine where you will physically go. Identify a secondary location (a friend's house, a new apartment) that is ready for immediate use.
  • Step 2: Inventory. Create a list of essential items to take (documents, sentimental items, necessities) so you can move quickly without being forced to negotiate with the partner.
  • Step 3: The Support Network. Identify your "Reminders." This is a list of specific people (like Marsha’s sister and friends) who know the truth about the relationship.
  • Step 4: The Truth Guard. Explicitly task these people with the role of reminding you why you left when you feel the urge to return to the "scene of the crime."

Practice drill

The Seven-Day Red Flag Log For the next seven days, carry a small notebook or use a dedicated note app. Every time you experience one of the "Becoming the Enemy" symptoms (shame regarding their behavior, feeling insulted, or feeling the need to monitor them), record the date, the specific event, and how it made you feel. Do not attempt to "fix" the behavior during this week; simply collect raw data. At the end of the week, review the log to see if the behavior is an isolated incident or a documented pattern of deactivation.

Critiques and caveats

  • Safety Warning: The "Planned Departure" and "Audit" techniques assume a situation of psychological or emotional dysfunction. If your relationship involves physical violence, the "Planned Departure" must be handled with extreme care through professional domestic violence resources, as notifying an abusive partner of your intent to leave can trigger high-risk retaliation.
  • The Complexity of Ambivalence: The book acknowledges that leaving is rarely a single event but a process of moving from protest to indifference. Do not mistake a temporary period of "protest" (fighting for the relationship) for a finalized decision to leave; the decision is only actionable when you reach the stage of indifference.
  • Subjectivity of the Audit: It is easy to over-report "hits" during a period of high conflict. The drill requires an honest assessment of whether these behaviors are chronic or merely symptomatic of a temporary external stressor (e.g., work stress).

Reading notes

This chapter serves as the concluding realization of the book's journey: that attachment theory is not just about finding love, but about knowing when to stop pursuing a dead end. It connects the mechanics of avoidant deactivation discussed in earlier chapters to the practical, life-altering necessity of self-preservation.
