The kitchen in the Fold did not know the concept of a shortage. The pantry held grain that never went rancid, and the larder held salt that never lost its bite. It was a pocket of impossible abundance, a sanctuary carved from the entropy of the outside world. But the kitchen knew the weight of a heavy heart, and today, the heart of the house was straining, pulled taut like a drumhead.

Halla stood at the long butcher block, her hands deep in a mound of rye dough. The texture was gritty and stubborn, resisting her movements. She worked the grain with a steady, rhythmic pressure, pushing and folding, building the strength the bread would need for the oven. The heat of the kitchen, fueled by the low-burning hearth, made the fine dust of flour cling to her skin, coating her forearms in a pale, ghostly shroud. She did not look up when the heavy oak door creaked on its iron hinges. She did not look up when the boots of the others entered the room, damp with the mist of the threshold and smelling of the damp, ozone-heavy air that preceded a storm.

The others were a chaos of disparate needs, a collection of jagged edges brought together by necessity. Wren was a flurry of silver needles and humming, her eyes fixed on a seam in a heavy tapestry that refused to hold; her fingers moved with a frantic, nervous energy. Vesper moved with the sharp, clipped precision of a blade being honed, her thoughts clearly on the boundary-wards, her hand resting habitually on the hilt of her dagger. Mira was a quiet shadow in the corner, her fingers tracing the edge of a brass key-ring as if counting the tolls of a way station, her eyes distant and unfocused.

And then there was Drevik.

He did not enter a room so much as the room reoriented itself around him. He was the center of gravity, the silent sun around which their small, fractured lives orbited. The light from the high, narrow windows seemed to pull toward his silhouette, caught in the gold embroidery of his deep-purple vestments. He moved with a relaxed, terrifying confidence that made the air feel thicker, more settled, as if his very presence compressed the atmosphere into something more manageable. The white-carved kitsune mask he wore caught the dim afternoon light, the gold accents gleaming like trapped suns, hiding the expression of a god or a monster.

He did not speak. He simply stood by the hearth, the void staff resting against his shoulder, and the room felt the sudden, sharp presence of the Threshold held shut. The silence he brought was not an absence of sound, but a presence of weight.

Halla kept kneading. She focused on the friction of the dough against the wood, the heat of it, the way the gluten strands fought back against her palms. Push, fold, turn. Push, fold, turn. She felt the eyes of the room drifting toward her, sensing the shift in her rhythm, sensing that she was no longer just the woman who fed them, but something else.

"The stores are not what they were," Halla said. Her voice was plain, stripped of ornament, the voice of a woman who knew the arithmetic of winter and the cold math of survival. She did not look up. She could not afford to break the rhythm of the dough. "The Unmaking Choir is pressing the seam. The wards are thinning, and the energy required to sustain them is draining the very marrow of this house. The rations must be tightened. We cannot feast on memory alone."

The room went still. It was a small thing to an outsider—a simple fact of accounting—but in the Fold, where resources were tied to the strength of the magic, it was a command. The others, who usually moved in their own orbits of thread, blade, and lock, paused mid-motion. The sewing stopped; the sharpening ceased; the counting of keys froze.

"The rations are the foundation of the Fold," Halla continued. She wiped her floury hands on her apron, the linen stained with the work of a thousand meals, but scrubbed clean of anything that smelled of decay. "If the belly is uncertain, the mind cannot hold the ward. If the pulse of the house falters from hunger, the perimeter will follow. From this night, we weigh the grain. We mark the meat. We do not take more than the hearth provides. No exceptions. No waste."

She looked up then.

She did not look at Drevik. She did not seek his approval, nor did she fear his judgment. She looked at the others. She saw Wren’s needle pause mid-air, a single silver thread dangling like a trapped spider. She saw Vesper’s hand tighten on the hilt of her small dagger, her eyes narrowing as she assessed the new reality.

"Is there any objection?" Halla asked.

No one spoke. The silence was not the heavy, predatory silence of the Sundered, the emptiness that sought to devour; it was the silence of a well-ordered room hearing its own law. It was the heavy, expectant quiet of a congregation receiving a new liturgy.

Drevik moved then. He stepped toward the table, his boots making a dull, authoritative thud on the stone floor. The movement was slow, deliberate. He reached out and picked up a small, dried apple from a ceramic bowl, the fruit shriveled and dark. He bit into it, the sound sharp and sudden in the quiet. He did not offer an opinion. He did not dismiss her. He simply watched her with those galaxy-void eyes, the star-pupils fixed on the steady, unyielding movement of her hands. He was a vastness, a presence that could have overturned her command with a single, whispered word, yet he remained a witness.

"The weight is noted," Drevik said. His voice was low, a chime in a dark cathedral, vibrating through the floorboards.

He turned and walked toward the far corner, toward the shadows where the library began. He left the room as he had entered it—leaving the gravity of him behind, a lingering pressure on the skin that took several minutes to dissipate.

Halla turned back to the dough. The work was harder now. The tension in the room had shifted; it had moved from the external threat of the Unmaking to the internal weight of the command. She felt the eyes of the others—the scrutiny of the women who had built this home with him, women who were used to her being the provider, the nurturer, the one who smoothed over the edges of their fear with a warm bowl of stew. Now, she was the one setting the boundaries.

Wren approached the table. She did not speak, but she reached out and took a small portion of the dough, testing the elasticity with a thumb. She looked at Halla, a long, searching look, and then nodded once—a silent, solemn acknowledgement of the new law.

But as the afternoon waned, the shadows in the corners of the kitchen seemed to grow longer than they should. The light was late, retreating from the windows as if afraid of what the dusk might bring. The warmth of the hearth felt thinner, more brittle.

Halla moved to the hearth to tend the embers, her muscles aching from the kneading. As she reached for the iron poker to stir the dying coals, she saw a figure standing in the doorway.

Sera.

The first. The litany-keeper. The woman whose bond with Drevik was the very gate through which all others passed.

Sera did not enter. She stood in the threshold, her white vestments stark and clinical against the deepening gloom of the hallway. Her face was unreadable, carved from something harder than flesh, but her eyes were fixed on Halla with a piercing, holy intensity. There was no warmth in the look, no recognition of the shared labor of the household. There was only the cold, bright scrutiny of a priestess checking a cracked altar for signs of rot.

"You speak of rationing, Halla," Sera said. The words were not a question; they were an indictment. "You speak of the grain and the meat. You speak as if the Fold is merely a pantry to be managed."

Halla gripped the poker. The iron was cold despite the dying fire. "The stores are the life of the Fold, Sera. Without them, there is no hearth to protect. Without a foundation, the spire falls."

"You are counting the cost of the bread, but you are failing to see the cost of the hunger," Sera replied. She stepped into the room, her movement fluid, silent, and predatory. She did not look at the others; her gaze remained locked on Halla, a challenge wrapped in a prayer. "A house cannot live on starch and salt alone. A house lives on the fervor of its inhabitants. On the strength of their devotion."

"Devotion does not fill a void, Sera," Halla countered, her voice rising just enough to catch the attention of the room. "And it certainly does not hold a line when the belly is empty and the spirit is weakened by starvation. We are facing an era of scarcity. We must adapt."

Sera stepped closer, the scent of incense and old, dusty parchment clinging to her like a shroud. She leaned in, her presence a sharp, liturgical edge that threatened to cut through Hallan's resolve. "Do not mistake the management of a pantry for the stewardship of a soul, Halla. If you tighten the belt too far, if you strip the joy from the feast and the warmth from the hearth, you will find there is nothing left to hold the weight of him. You will leave us hollow. And a hollow house is easily broken."

Halla felt the weight of the kitchen shift again. The command she had issued was being tested, not by the hunger of the Sundered, or the emptiness of the larder, but by the very foundation of the house's identity. She felt the eyes of Wren and Vesper on her back, the silent judgment of the others.

Halla did not flinch. She stood her ground behind the long butcher block, the heavy wood a barrier between her and the doubt. She forced herself to look Sera directly in those piercing, sanctimonious eyes.

"I keep the hearth fed so that he may keep the world shut," Halla said, her voice dropping to a low, dangerous register. "That is my task. I am the keeper of the physical reality. If you wish to count the stars and pray to the shadows, Sera, do so. I will continue to count the grain. I will ensure there is enough to keep us breathing, even if the breath is heavy with your 'devotion'."

Sera leaned in even further, her voice a lethal whisper that seemed to chill the very air around the hearth. "Then pray, Halla. Pray that when the grain finally runs low, you still have enough left to keep the door closed. Because if the hunger takes us, it won't be the lack of bread that kills us. It will be the lack of will."

Sera turned on her heel and vanished back into the gloom of the hallway, her white robes swallowed by the dark.

Halla stood alone by the fire. The embers were dying, retreating into grey ash. She looked down at her hands—the knuckles were red, the skin white with flour, and they were trembling with a sudden, cold uncertainty that no amount of iron or command could suppress.

The dough sat on the block, heavy, unyielding, and silent.

Outside, the wind from the void brushed against the Threshold, a low, mourning sound that no amount of rationing or discipline could ever hope to quiet. It was a sound of infinite hunger, and for the first time, Halla wondered if she had been counting the wrong things all along.
