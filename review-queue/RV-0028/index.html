Core idea

Safer sex is a negotiated protocol, not an improvised reaction to heat. You must treat sexual health as an active, ongoing agreement between partners rather than a set of assumptions. The goal is to move away from "Finger Crossing"—the unethical practice of hoping for the best and shifting risk onto others—toward a system of transparent, proactive management. By establishing clear boundaries regarding barriers and testing, you protect your partners and preserve the intimacy of the encounter.

Key concepts and terms

  • Fluid Bonding (or Fluid Monogamy): A specific agreement within a closed subset of a larger network (such as a primary couple or a small polycule) to engage in unprotected sex with one another, provided everyone adheres to strict testing and barrier protocols with all outside partners.
  • Finger Crossing: The act of engaging in sexual behavior based on hope, luck, or the assumption that "everything is fine" without verifying status or using protection. The authors categorize this as unethical because it lacks accountability.
  • Preexposure Prophylaxis (PrEP): A medical preventative measure (medication) used to reduce the risk of contracting HIV. While highly effective, it requires medical oversight and advance logistical planning.
  • Negotiated Practice: The process of discussing boundaries, barriers, and health status before sexual arousal makes logical negotiation difficult.
  • High-Risk Behaviors: Specific sexual acts or lifestyle choices (such as certain types of anal sex or drug use) that increase the statistical probability of transmission, requiring specific, agreed-upon protections.
  • Testing Cadence: The agreed-upon frequency and type of medical screenings (e.g., every three months, full STI panel) required to maintain "fluid bonding" status.

Techniques worth drilling

  1. The Pre-Sex Negotiation Script

Do not wait until clothes are off to discuss barriers. You must practice a "sober-state" negotiation.

  • Step 1: Identify the specific barrier (condom, dental dam, etc.) you require for the specific act.
  • Step 2: State your requirement as a non-negotiable boundary, not a suggestion.
  • Step 3: Confirm the partner's understanding by asking them to repeat the plan.
  • Example: "I want to use a condom for this. Are you okay with that?"
  1. The Disclosure Protocol

You must have a rehearsed method for communicating changes in your sexual health or partner list.

  • Step 1: Define "The Trigger"—what event requires a conversation? (e.g., a new sexual partner, a positive test, a lapse in condom use).
  • Step 2: Draft a one-sentence notification.
  • Step 3: Deliver the information via a medium that allows for follow-up (text or in-person), ensuring the information is received and acknowledged.
  1. The "Sober Check" Calibration

Since drugs and alcohol destroy the ability to negotiate, you must drill the ability to pause a sexual encounter.

  • Step 1: Establish a personal "hard stop" rule: If you or your partner are too impaired to discuss consent or barriers, the encounter stops.
  • Step 2: Practice the "Pause Phrase" in a non-sexual setting so it becomes a reflex.
  • Example: "I'm having a great time, but I'm feeling a bit too tipsy to make good decisions right now. Let's take a break."
  1. Logistical Barrier Management

Minimize friction by making barrier use a seamless part of your sexual toolkit.

  • Step 1: Audit your supplies (condoms, lube, dental dams) weekly.
  • Step 2: Practice the physical motion of opening and applying a condom while distracted or watching TV.
  • Step 3: Ensure high-quality lubricant is always within reach to prevent condom breakage.

Practice drill

The Weekly Agreement & Rehearsal

This week, you will draft a formal "Safer Sex Agreement" and perform a vocal rehearsal.

Part 1: The Document Write out a one-page document (for your eyes and your partners' eyes) that contains the following sections:

  • The Inner Circle: List the specific individuals with whom you have agreed to fluid bonding.
  • The Barrier Standard: Explicitly state which barriers are required for which acts (e.g., "Condoms for all penetrative sex; dental dams for oral-vaginal/anal").
  • Testing Cadence: State exactly when you will test (e.g., "Every 90 days") and what the results must include (e.g., "Full STI panel including HIV, Hep C, and Syphilis").
  • The Lapse Protocol: Define what happens if a barrier is missed or a new partner is added without prior discussion (e.g., "Immediate cessation of unprotected contact until new testing is confirmed").
  • The Change Notification: State how you will notify partners of changes (e.g., "I will text you if my status or partner list changes").

Part 2: The Vocal Rehearsal Find a private space. Stand in front of a mirror. You must say the following two sentences aloud, ten times each, until you can say them without hesitation, without blushing, and with a tone of "boring" matter-of-factness. Do not "perform" them; deliver them as if you are reading a grocery list.

  • Sentence 1 (The Boundary): "I'm not comfortable doing this without a condom; let's use one."
  • Sentence 2 (The Disclosure): "I have started seeing someone new, so we need to revisit our testing agreement."

Critiques and caveats

  • The False Sense of Security: A "clean" test result is a snapshot in time, not a permanent status. You must respect the "window period" (the time it takes for antibodies to show up on a test), which can be up to six months for certain infections. Never assume a recent negative test confers permanent safety.
  • The Burden of Enforcement: While the book emphasizes negotiation, the responsibility for safety does not fall solely on the person requesting the barrier. If you are the person "performing" the risk, you are equally responsible for upholding the agreement.
  • Complexity Overload: Over-complicating agreements with too many "if/then" clauses can lead to "negotiation fatigue," where partners stop communicating because the rules feel too heavy. Keep the core protocol simple and actionable.

Reading notes

This section serves as the practical "safety manual" for the more abstract concepts of autonomy and freedom discussed earlier in the book. While other chapters focus on the emotional architecture of polyamory, "Keeping Sex Safe" provides the concrete, physical boundaries required to prevent emotional trauma from physical health complications.
