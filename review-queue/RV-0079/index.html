The Revision Engine — Part 1: Why This System Exists

The Revision Problem

You finished your novel. Congratulations — that puts you ahead of 90% of people who start one.

Now comes the part that separates finished novels from published ones: revision.

If you're like most writers, revision goes something like this:

  1. You read through your manuscript
  2. You notice some things that feel off
  3. You start fixing sentences
  4. You realize a scene doesn't work
  5. You try to fix the scene
  6. You notice a plot hole connected to that scene
  7. You go back to fix the plot hole
  8. That fix breaks something in chapter 3
  9. You give up and line-edit instead because at least that feels productive
  10. You "finish" revising but you know it's not actually done

Sound familiar? You're not doing it wrong — you're doing it without a system.

Why Free-For-All Revision Fails

When you try to fix everything at once, three things happen:

1. You solve problems out of order. Line-editing a scene you'll later cut is wasted work. Polishing prose before fixing structure is painting a house before the foundation sets.

2. You miss problems you can't see. When you're looking for typos, you miss pacing. When you're checking pacing, you miss character inconsistencies. Your brain can only hold one type of problem in working memory at a time.

3. You burn out. Revision fatigue is real. The more types of problems you try to solve simultaneously, the faster you exhaust your attention.

The 4-Pass System

Professional editors don't fix everything at once. They work in stages, each with a specific focus. The Revision Engine adapts this professional workflow for self-editing:

| Pass | Name | Focus | What You're Looking For | |---|---|---|---| | 1 | Structural | Story architecture | Does this work as a story? | | 2 | Line Edit | Sentence craft | Does each sentence earn its place? | | 3 | Copy Edit | Consistency & correctness | Is everything internally consistent? | | 4 | Proofread | Clean copy | Are there any remaining errors? |

Each pass has one job. You complete that job, then move on. You never go back to a previous pass while working on a later one. (If you discover a structural problem during line editing, you flag it and fix it during the next structural pass — you don't stop and rewrite.)

Why This Order Matters

Structural before Line Edit: There's no point polishing sentences in scenes you'll cut. Fix the story's bones first.

Line Edit before Copy Edit: Style and rhythm changes can introduce new errors. Get the prose right, then check for consistency.

Copy Edit before Proofread: Consistency edits (changing a character's name, fixing timeline errors) can introduce typos. Proofread last, when the text is stable.

Proofread last: The final read is the only one where you're reading for errors, not making changes. You need a stable, complete text before this pass works.

How to Use This System

  1. Complete each pass fully before starting the next. Don't skip ahead.
  2. Use the worksheets. They're designed to keep you focused on one type of problem at a time.
  3. Take breaks between passes. Minimum 3 days, ideally a week. You need fresh eyes.
  4. Don't edit and create simultaneously. Each pass is analytical. If you get a creative idea, note it and address it during the appropriate pass.
  5. Expect to spend real time. A 80,000-word novel needs roughly:
  • Pass 1 (Structural): 20-30 hours
  • Pass 2 (Line Edit): 15-25 hours
  • Pass 3 (Copy Edit): 8-12 hours
  • Pass 4 (Proofread): 5-8 hours

Total: 48-75 hours across 4-8 weeks. This is normal. Revision is supposed to take longer than drafting.

A Note on Perfectionism

This system will help you find every problem in your manuscript. That doesn't mean you have to fix all of them.

Every published novel has flaws. The goal of revision isn't perfection — it's good enough to publish. The 4-pass system gets you to "good enough" efficiently. After that, diminishing returns kick in hard.

Know when to stop. A finished, imperfect novel beats a perfect manuscript that never leaves your desk.

The Revision Engine — Part 2: Pass 1 (Structural Edit)

What Structural Editing Fixes

This pass fixes story-level problems. If the structure is broken, no amount of sentence polish will save it.

You're answering one question: Does this work as a story?

The Scene Audit

Go through your manuscript scene by scene. For each scene, answer these questions:

The Scene Survival Test

Every scene must earn its place. For each scene, check:

  • [ ] Does this scene advance the plot? If you removed it, would the story still make sense?
  • [ ] Does this scene reveal character? Do we learn something new about a character?
  • [ ] Does this scene raise or answer a question? Every good scene creates curiosity or delivers on a promise.
  • [ ] Does this scene have conflict? Even quiet scenes need tension — internal or external.
  • [ ] Does this scene change the situation? The end of the scene should be different from the beginning.

Scoring: A scene needs at least 3 out of 5 to survive. Below that, cut it or merge it with another scene.

What to look for:

  • Flat line: Pacing doesn't vary. The story feels same-y throughout.
  • Sustained high: Too many intense scenes in a row. Reader fatigue.
  • Sustained low: Too many quiet scenes. The story drags.
  • Missing escalation: Stakes should generally rise. Each peak should be higher than the last.
  • Sagging middle: Common pattern — strong opening, strong ending, flat middle. Cut or tighten.

The Escalation Test

  • [ ] Each major conflict is harder than the last
  • [ ] The stakes increase over the course of the story
  • [ ] The protagonist's choices get harder, not easier
  • [ ] The consequences of failure grow
  • [ ] The pace generally accelerates toward the climax

The Sagging Middle Checklist

The middle of a novel is where most drafts fall apart. Check these specific middle-act problems:

  • [ ] Does the middle introduce new complications? Or is the protagonist just reacting to the same problem?
  • [ ] Does the midpoint shift the story's direction? Something should change the game at the 50% mark.
  • [ ] Are there subplots that deepen the main conflict? Not distract from it — deepen it.
  • [ ] Do secondary characters have their own arcs? They should affect the story, not just serve the protagonist.
  • [ ] Is there a "dark moment" or "all is lost" beat? The lowest point should come before the climax.
  • [ ] Does every scene in the middle move the story closer to the climax? If not, cut it.

Structural Pass Completion

Before moving to Pass 2, verify:

  • [ ] Every scene scores 3/5 or higher on the Scene Survival Test
  • [ ] All plot holes logged and prioritized (🔴/🟡/🟢)
  • [ ] All major character arcs mapped with clear progression
  • [ ] Pacing graph shows variation with escalation toward climax
  • [ ] Sagging middle addressed
  • [ ] Any scenes that need cutting are marked (don't cut yet — just mark)
  • [ ] Any scenes that need adding are noted (add after structural pass is complete)

Now take a break. Minimum 3 days before starting Pass 2.

03-pass2-line-edit

The Revision Engine — Part 3: Pass 2 (Line Edit)

What Line Editing Fixes

This pass fixes sentence-level craft. The story's structure is solid (you fixed that in Pass 1). Now you make every sentence earn its place.

You're answering one question: Does each sentence work?

The Sentence-Level Audit

For each paragraph in your manuscript, check these items:

Weak Verb Audit

The #1 line-level problem in fiction: weak verbs propped up by adverbs.

| Problem | Example | Fix | |---|---|---| | "To be" + adverb | She was really angry | She slammed the door | | "To be" + adjective | He was tall | His head nearly touched the doorframe | | Have + past participle | She had gone to the store | She went to the store | | Generic action verb + adverb | He walked quickly | He strode / hurried / sprinted | | Sensing verb + description | She felt the cold wind | The wind bit her cheeks |

Action: Search for "was," "were," "had," "felt," "seemed," "appeared." Replace with strong specific verbs.

The Redundancy Audit

| # | Page/Line | Redundant Phrase | Lean Version | |---|---|---|---| | 1 | | | | | 2 | | | | | 3 | | | | | 4 | | | | | 5 | | | |

Common redundancies to hunt:

  • "nodded his head" → "nodded"
  • "shrugged her shoulders" → "shrugged"
  • "thought to herself" → "thought" (who else would she think to?)
  • "past history" → "history"
  • "completely destroyed" → "destroyed"
  • "free gift" → "gift"
  • "fell down" → "fell"
  • "rose up" → "rose"
  • "joined together" → "joined"

Telling vs. Showing Audit

Find every "telling" sentence and determine if it should be shown instead.

When telling is fine: transitions, summaries, backstory, emotional context that's not the focus of the scene.

When showing is required: the emotional core of a scene, character-defining moments, any moment you want the reader to feel.

| # | Page/Line | Telling Version | Showing Version | |---|---|---|---| | 1 | | | | | 2 | | | | | 3 | | | | | 4 | | | | | 5 | | | |

The Dialogue Audit

Dialogue Mechanics Checklist

For each line of dialogue:

  • [ ] Can you tell who's speaking without the tag? If not, the voice isn't distinct enough.
  • [ ] Does the dialogue advance the scene? If you can remove it without losing anything, cut it.
  • [ ] Is the dialogue saying what the character means? People rarely say exactly what they mean. Add subtext.
  • [ ] Is there conflict in the dialogue? Characters agreeing is boring. Find the disagreement.
  • [ ] Are you over-explaining in dialogue tags? "She said" is invisible. "She exclaimed vehemently" draws attention to the tag, not the dialogue.

Dialogue Tag Audit

Search for dialogue tags. Rate each one:

| Tag Type | Keep? | Example | |---|---|---| | "said" / "asked" | ✅ Always | "I don't know," she said. | | Action beat | ✅ Usually | She picked up her coffee. "I don't know." | | Adverb tag | ❌ Rarely | "I don't know," she said quietly. → She kept her voice low. "I don't know." | | Emotion tag | ❌ Rarely | "I don't know," she said nervously. → She wouldn't meet his eyes. "I don't know." | | Explaining tag | ❌ Never | "I don't know," she said, shrugging to indicate her uncertainty. → Cut the explanation. |

The Dialogue Compression Test

Read every conversation aloud. Cut:

  • Greetings and small talk (unless they reveal character)
  • Repeated information the reader already knows
  • Any line where a character says exactly what they're thinking
  • Any conversation that's just "filler" between action beats

Target: Cut 20-30% of dialogue length. Most drafts have too much.

The Paragraph Flow Audit

Transition Quality

For every scene break or paragraph transition, check:

  • [ ] Does the transition make temporal sense? The reader should know where they are in time.
  • [ ] Does the transition make spatial sense? The reader should know where they are in space.
  • [ ] Is the transition smooth or jarring? Intentional jumps are fine. Unintentional ones confuse readers.

Paragraph Length Variety

Just like sentences, paragraphs need variety. Check:

  • [ ] Are all paragraphs the same length? (Bad — creates visual monotony)
  • [ ] Do you have one-sentence paragraphs for emphasis? (Good — creates punch)
  • [ ] Do you have longer paragraphs for complex ideas or action sequences? (Good — creates flow)
  • [ ] Does the paragraph length match the pacing? Short = fast. Long = slow.

The Sensory Detail Audit

The Five-Senses Check

For each major scene, count sensory details:

| Scene | Sight | Sound | Smell | Touch | Taste | |---|---|---|---|---|---| | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | |

Common pattern: 90% sight, 10% sound, almost nothing else. Target a more balanced distribution. You don't need all 5 senses in every scene, but most scenes should have at least 2-3.

The Specific Detail Test

For every description, ask: Is this the specific detail a real person would notice, or a generic description any writer would write?

  • Generic: "The room was messy." → Specific: "Dishes stacked in the sink, a tower of mail on the counter, and a cat hair sweater draped over the couch arm."
  • Generic: "She was beautiful." → Specific: What kind of beautiful? The kind that stops traffic, or the kind that sneaks up on you?

Line Edit Pass Completion

Before moving to Pass 3, verify:

  • [ ] Weak verbs replaced (search for was/were/had/felt/seemed/appeared)
  • [ ] Redundancies cut
  • [ ] Telling converted to showing where it matters
  • [ ] Dialogue compressed (20-30% cut)
  • [ ] Dialogue tags cleaned (mostly "said" and action beats)
  • [ ] Paragraph length varied
  • [ ] Transitions smooth and intentional
  • [ ] Sensory details balanced (at least 2-3 senses per scene)
  • [ ] Specific details replace generic descriptions

Take a break. Minimum 3 days before starting Pass 3.

The Revision Engine — Part 4: Pass 3 (Copy Edit) & Pass 4 (Proofread)

PASS 3: COPY EDIT

Fixing Consistency and Correctness

What Copy Editing Fixes

This pass fixes consistency and correctness. The story works (Pass 1), the prose works (Pass 2), now you make sure everything is internally consistent and mechanically correct.

You're answering one question: Is everything in this manuscript consistent?

The Style Sheet

Before you start copy editing, create a style sheet. This is your manuscript's rulebook.

Character Style Sheet

| Character | Full Name | Nickname(s) | Age | Physical Description | Speech Patterns | Key Details | |---|---|---|---|---|---|---| | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | |

Timeline Style Sheet

| Event | Chapter/Scene | Date/Time | Time Elapsed Since Last Event | Consistency Issue? | |---|---|---|---|---| | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | |

World-Building Style Sheet

| Element | Rule Established | Chapter/Scene | Used Consistently? | |---|---|---|---| | | | | | | | | | | | | | | |

Style Decisions

| Decision | Your Choice | Notes | |---|---|---| | Oxford comma? | Yes / No | | | Numbers spelled out? | Under 10 / Under 100 / Never | | | Time format? | 3pm / 3:00 PM / three o'clock | | | Dialogue punctuation? | "Hi," she said. / "Hi". She said. | | | Chapter titles? | Numbers / Names / None | |

The Consistency Audit

Character Consistency

  • [ ] Names spelled the same way throughout (check nicknames too)
  • [ ] Physical descriptions match (eye color, height, hair — no changing mid-book)
  • [ ] Character knowledge is consistent (they can't know something they haven't learned yet)
  • [ ] Speech patterns are consistent (a character who uses short sentences in chapter 1 shouldn't give speeches in chapter 15)
  • [ ] Relationships are consistent (if they're strangers in chapter 3, they can't be old friends in chapter 7)

Timeline Consistency

  • [ ] Seasons match the timeline (no sudden shifts from winter to summer without explanation)
  • [ ] Day/night sequences are correct
  • [ ] Travel times make sense (a character can't be in two places 500 miles apart in the same day without explanation)
  • [ ] Ages and dates are consistent
  • [ ] References to past events have correct timing

World-Building Consistency

  • [ ] Rules established early are followed later (magic systems, technology, social norms)
  • [ ] Place names are spelled consistently
  • [ ] Cultural details don't contradict each other
  • [ ] Physical geography is consistent (if the river is to the east in chapter 2, it's still to the east in chapter 20)

Grammar and Mechanics

Common Fiction Errors to Check

  • [ ] Comma splices — two independent clauses joined only by a comma
  • [ ] Dangling modifiers — "Walking down the street, the trees were beautiful" (the trees weren't walking)
  • [ ] Subject-verb agreement — especially with collective nouns
  • [ ] Pronoun reference — every "he/she/it/they" clearly refers to one antecedent
  • [ ] Dialogue punctuation — consistent throughout
  • [ ] Possessives vs. contractions — its/it's, their/they're/there, your/you're
  • [ ] Who vs. whom — if you can replace it with "him," use "whom"
  • [ ] Lie vs. lay — you lie down, you lay something down

Formatting Consistency

  • [ ] Chapter headings formatted the same way
  • [ ] Scene breaks consistent (asterisks, blank lines, or hash marks — pick one)
  • [ ] Dialogue formatting consistent
  • [ ] Italics used consistently (thoughts, foreign words, emphasis — pick rules and stick to them)
  • [ ] Numbers formatted consistently
  • [ ] Time formatted consistently

PASS 4: PROOFREAD

The Final Clean

What Proofreading Fixes

This pass fixes typos and mechanical errors. The story, prose, and consistency are all solid. Now you catch what slipped through.

You're answering one question: Is this manuscript clean?

The Proofreading Protocol

Step 1: Change the Format

Print it out, or change the font/size/color. Your brain stops seeing errors in familiar layouts. A new format forces fresh eyes.

Step 2: Read Backwards

Read the last sentence first, then the second-to-last, etc. This prevents your brain from filling in what should be there and forces you to see what's actually there.

Step 3: Read Aloud

Read the entire manuscript aloud. Your ears catch what your eyes skip:

  • Missing words ("the" is the most commonly missed)
  • Awkward phrasing that looked fine on the page
  • Repetitive sentence rhythms
  • Dialogue that doesn't sound like speech

Step 4: Use the Spell Checker (But Don't Trust It)

Run a spell check, but review every suggestion manually. Spell checkers miss:

  • Homophones (their/there/they're, its/it's, your/you're)
  • Wrong-but-real words (form/from, though/through, defiantly/definitely)
  • Missing words that create grammatical sentences

The Common Error Checklist

  • [ ] Homophones: its/it's, their/there/they're, your/you're, to/too/two, affect/effect, than/then, weather/whether, complement/compliment, stationery/stationary, peak/peek/pique, bare/bear, board/bored, sight/site/cite, piece/peace, coarse/course
  • [ ] Missing small words: a, an, the, in, on, at, to, of, is, are
  • [ ] Double words: "the the," "a a," "that that" — your brain skips these
  • [ ] Wrong-but-real words: form/from, though/through, defiantly/definitely, casual/causal, weary/wary, discrete/discreet, complement/compliment
  • [ ] Punctuation pairs: Missing closing quotes, parentheses, brackets
  • [ ] Number consistency: "seven" in one place, "7" in another
  • [ ] Capitalization: Same term capitalized in some places, lowercase in others
  • [ ] Spacing: Double spaces, inconsistent paragraph spacing, extra line breaks

The Final Verification

Before you publish or submit:

  • [ ] All Pass 1 (Structural) items addressed
  • [ ] All Pass 2 (Line Edit) items addressed
  • [ ] All Pass 3 (Copy Edit) items addressed
  • [ ] Style sheet completed and consistent
  • [ ] Read aloud completed
  • [ ] Spell check completed
  • [ ] Homophone check completed
  • [ ] Formatting consistent throughout
  • [ ] Beta reader feedback incorporated
  • [ ] Manuscript formatted per submission guidelines (if submitting) or publication platform (if self-publishing)

The Final Read-Through Protocol

One last pass. This time, you're not editing. You're reading as a reader.

  1. Set aside the manuscript for at least a week. Longer if possible.
  2. Read it in a different format (print, e-reader, different device).
  3. Read it in one or two sittings if possible. This reveals pacing problems.
  4. Mark only two things: where you got bored, and where you got confused.
  5. Don't edit during this read. Just mark and keep going.

After this read-through, address the boredom and confusion marks. Then you're done.

Not "done enough to keep editing." Done. Ship it.
