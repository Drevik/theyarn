The Bureau answered at dusk, and Mira smelled the answer before she saw it. Hot iron. Cold mortar. Under both, a whine pitched just below her teeth. She knew that whine. A hinge taken past its tolerance sings before it lets go, and the whole hill was singing.

The stronghouse doors stood open on their tracks, and a stronghouse never opens for courtesy.

"Considerate," Drevik said. "They've left everything open for us."

"Nothing of theirs is open for you." Mira set her case on the gravel and knelt to its latches. Files, gauges, the collaring bar. "It's open because the thing inside is the larger tenant now."

"Second-largest," he said, "while I stand on the steps. I've held worse rankings."

She had walked the entry corridor on her measuring rounds, eleven years ago. Forty-one paces, sill to turning. Tonight she counted sixty and stopped when the left-hand wall began arriving before she reached it. The night watch had kept their posts. The posts were still there. The watch had gone into the walls, or the walls into the watch; the geometry had not settled which, and she did not stay to arbitrate.

The warden's office had kept its shape, or something was wearing it. The requisition book lay squared to the desk's edge, because Assessor Tobin had been a neat man, and neatness outlives the hand that keeps it.

Mira read the tolerance sheet twice, because the first time through she refused what it said. The containment order for Cage One had been recut into a delivery order: eight gates re-sequenced to open inward, toward the seam, so the asset could be walked out like freight. Her countersignature had been scraped off with a blade. A new name sat over the seam, in a hand she knew the way she knew her own.

He had taught her the forms. He had always been neat.

"Tobin," she said. Just the name. It was all the sheet needed.

"There's more." Drevik turned the manifest page with one finger, the only part of him that ever touched paper. Eleven assets, shipped out over the last month. The destination line read Station Twelve.

"There's no Station Twelve on any charter I ever copied," she said.

"There's no Station Twelve on any charter anyone copied. Which means a station that doesn't exist is receiving goods, and the goods are arriving." He stood over the desk a while, touching nothing. "On the road to Graymarker, Tobin gave me his word on our bargain. 'A man who keeps books keeps faith.' I told him I'd hold him to the wording." He turned from the desk. "I keep everything. That's my filing."

The whine stopped. She had heard a thousand hinges go quiet and never once been glad of it. A hinge stops singing when it decides.

"Down," Drevik said. His jokes had run out at the cage door. She noticed when they went.

She had cut the tolerances for Cage One herself, eleven years ago, and been shown the road for refusing to loosen them. The work had been honest, which was the whole of her defense, and the Bureau had kept her sheets and scraped her name whenever the sheets said no.

The gates of Cage One stood open on their broken order. The thing behind them was mostly doorway. It had taken the corners first, because corners are where a room keeps its arithmetic. What was left was a hole squared off where the chains had held it, and patient. Where the deep wall used to be, the seam stood open, and behind it the pressure of everything that wants in came through like weather under a door.

"Collar gates," Drevik said. "In the order you wrote. Not the order on the desk."

"There's a difference?"

"There will be, inside forty seconds."

She set her bar and worked. Eight gates, bolts cold as a winter ledger, one frame already thinking about warping; each bolt seated, the frame gave up its bad idea. While she worked he held the seam. He set the staff against it and leaned, the way you lean a prop under a lintel that has begun to go, and the leaning did not look like effort, and that was the effort.

He said one word. The word had overtones. She caught the note meant for the thing, and the note meant for the door, and under both a third note that she did not like, because it did not sound like him anymore. The star-points of his eyes had gone small and far, like coins spent down to their rims.

Tobin came up the inner corridor at a walk, gray to the lips, the requisition book held to his chest with both arms.

"The way out is behind me," Drevik said. "It stays open as long as I do. Come."

"The forms," Tobin said. "The audit copies. I only need the forms."

He weighed the two doors. She watched him weigh them. He had taught her to weigh fast and cut clean, and he did both, and chose his own door.

The corridor behind him did not end in a wall anymore. It ended in before. He walked into it at a measured pace, and it closed after him the way accounts close: balanced, nothing left carrying.

"Receipts," Drevik said, to the space where a man had been. It was not a joke. It was the wording, held to.

The asset went back through its gates in her order, gate by gate, each taking its share of the load the way her sheets had said it should, eleven years before anyone thought to scrape her name off them. He found the true line of the thing and held the frame to it until the frame remembered which side of itself was wall. The seam sealed. The hall became a hall: four corners, forty paces, ordinary arithmetic.

On their way out he took the tolerance sheet from her case, which he never did, and gave it to the candle on the warden's desk.

"I have it," he said. "Now the paper archive agrees with mine."

They stood on the gravel while the stronghouse settled behind them, roof folding into roof until the hill had its own shape back.

"Eleven years they asked that seam a question," she said. "It answered before you did."

"I'm a slow answer," he said, "but I keep better books."

Sera was waiting at the Fold door with the censer and the short office she said over travelers. The smoke went where it went, and his shoulders came down a finger's width. The crystals in his mitre, frantic since the cage hall, slowed to a sulk.

He said yes to Wren's welcome, and thank you to Vesper's road, and the words were right and the throw was missing. Mira had spent her life around keys. She knew a key that turned without throwing the bolt.

Wren took her torn cuff without asking, ran a thumb along the fray, and hummed the count of it. Vesper came in from the garden gate having counted the dark behind them twice. "The road's paid," she said. "Nothing followed. There was nothing left to follow."

Halla had been baking since the news came, and grief in that kitchen rose as bread: eleven loaves, and she had salted none of them.

When he came through the kitchen door the room gave him its whole attention. Spoons stopped. The fire settled. Halla's hands went still on the dough.

"I set his place twelve winters," Halla said. "He carried my market-lists four of them. A woman knows her own table. I thought I was teaching him the house. He was weighing it. I gave a scale a seat and called it kin."

Drevik took bread from the board. "You set a place for a hungry man," he said. "The cost of setting places is that some men are hungry in the wrong way. I pay that cost at the door every night of the year. Tonight the turn was yours. There will be other turns."

He ate. He asked for the salt. That was the whole rite, and Sera's censer gave it its smoke, and Halla untied her apron, folded it once, and tied it back on. That was her way of taking an oath.

Mira and Halla had been short with each other since the first thaw, over keys, over salts, over the right depth to bury the turnips. Watching him hand the salt across, Mira found she had no grievance left standing. Someone had swept the shelf where she kept it.

His fingers were cold on the salt cellar. She filed that. He sat with his cup and did not drink it, and answered Halla in the right number of words, and none of the words had warmth in them until Sera sat down, and then there was some.

Later, with the table cleared, Sera took the tool case from the bench, set it by the stair, and put the lamp into Mira's hands.

"Take the light up."

"He has his own lamp," Mira said.

"He won't ask for anyone tonight," Sera said. "Go anyway."

Mira had been four months learning the doors of that house. That was the first time one of them had been held open for her from the inside.

The mitre was off already, set on the desk, its crystals drifting near the ceiling. The vestments were still fastened, every hook of them, and the mask was on, and the lamp found the silver of his hair where he stood at the window with the staff.

"Your door's not locked," she said.

"No," he said. "It isn't."

Nothing of his was ever unlocked by accident. She stood in the seam of it a moment and took the measurement.

"You should eat more than bread," she said.

"I had bread. I had salt."

"That's not a supper. That's a receipt."

"Then reconcile me," he said, and crossed the room, and that was all the asking he did.

The mask came off under her hands. She had cut hooks for eleven years; his collar was small gold hooks, honest ones, and her fingers had no trouble and did not hurry. She had a joke ready about hooks and eyes. She did not spend it.

The lamp stayed lit. Neither of them reached to put it out.

He said her name. Not Assessor, not mistress of anything. Mira, once, level, the way you set a bolt home. The vestments came off him in folds; he paid them out like a man paying out rope, careful of the gold. Her own clothes she managed herself, faster than he had, and his breath roughened at her mouth, and hers answered it, and the rope frame of the bed kept a count under them both. His weight came down careful, then not careful. He had been looked at all her life at the Bureau; they looked at her hands. He looked at her hands too. Then the rest of her, unhurried, and she held still for it, because being looked at like that had never once been in her trade. It went the way good work goes. Slow at the fastenings. Honest in the middle. Finished without hurry.

Afterward his hands were warm. She checked the way she checked work, two fingers at his wrist, and the warmth was back, and his pulse ran slow again. The crystals over the desk had gone back to their lazy drifting. She had no theory that fit a door that mends what it shuts. She built one anyway and put it away for cutting later.

She woke to the smell of new wood. Her key ring hung on the bedpost where she'd left it, and on it was a key she had not cut. New brass. Bright teeth.

The corridor held a door that had not been there. The key turned; the bolt threw, clean. Inside was a bench at the right height, her vice bolted down, her files in order by cut, the spare blanks she had carried eleven years in a case she never opened, laid out in a row. The room smelled of new wood and old oil. The Fold had grown it overnight the way bread grows in a warm place: quick, and with the grain of what was already in it.

She threaded the new key on beside the old Bureau key she kept as a scar. The ring held both without complaint. Rings do.

At breakfast the smirk had come back. She watched it decide to.

Wren hummed over a mend. Vesper counted passes on the whetstone. Sera poured, and Halla stood at the end of the long table with her apron on and her hands still.

"I've kept the stores of this Communion since the first winter," Halla said. "The counts. The salting-down. The grain against the bad years. I kept them the way a hired woman keeps them. I want to keep them in the house. I want to be one of the anchored."

Drevik set down his cup.

"That isn't mine alone to give," he said. "Ask the first wife."

Every head at the table came up. Halla turned to Sera, and her chin did not drop, and her hands stayed still at her apron's edge.

"Sera," Halla said. "I'm asking."

Sera set down the pot.

"Sit, then," she said. "I'll say the cost out loud."

Mira turned the new key over in her pocket and did not warn her. The cost of that house was never the asking. It was hearing it said out loud, and staying at the table after.
