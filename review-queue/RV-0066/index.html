The leak started singing around midnight, half-voice, the way seams sing when they think nobody's auditing.

Drevik had the altar boards up and his thumb on the joint before the second verse finished. The Sundered press the underside of the world the way weather presses a roof — patient, and paying in nothing — and every so often a seam gives up a little hymn, gratis, to see who answers. He set the staff's orb into the joint and gave it three notes. The cheap chord, he called it, the one you pay leaks. The hymn went back down through the stone owing him change.

"Rent's paid," he said, to the floor in general.

"Or the toll came due," Sera said, from the aisle. "With you it's never clear which."

She came up the nave carrying the last candle in the town — tallow, cheap, honest, the size of a thumb — and no ceremony about it. Gray censer-cloth over patched wool. Ink still on her fingers from copying psalters nobody had ordered. Nine years she'd kept censer-ward at this altar and the town had let her, because somebody has to keep a fire company, and the god had never sent so much as a bill.

Drevik put the boards back, sat on the altar step, and made room without making it obvious. She took the other half of the step anyway.

The chapel of Candledown kept its roof, half its floor, and one bell of nine. The other eight had cracked the night the Choir came up the well, and the town had taken that personally. Doors had gone threshold-sick all along Weeper's Row: barred against nothing, salt on sills that kept nothing out. Forty-one people still slept in their houses, in the dark, on the grounds that it was their dark. Far beneath the hill, the Choir kept its half-voice going — a hymn with a hole in it where a voice should have been, going around and around the hole. You stop hearing it after a while.

That's what's wrong with it.

"Say it," he said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been carrying up the aisle every night this week. You hold things like a man holds a full cup in a crowd. I keep waiting for you to spill."

She set the candle on the step between them and let it do its small work.

"I want to bind myself to the quest," she said. "To the work. To the door. I've priced the words for a week and none of them fit. Bind is the honest one."

"There's nothing to bind to. I'm not a church. I'm a man with a bad ledger and a door. You can't swear at that; there's nowhere to hang the oath."

"The wood," she said. "One swears at a door."

He laughed once. "That's liturgist thinking. Hang everything where the congregation can reach it." Then, because she'd come that far: "But since you're here to spend, let's read the bill. I take no one's hope and give no one's promise without saying the cost out loud. Nobody's ever stayed past the reading. You've had the usual warning, which is a paragraph in advance.

"First cost. The work doesn't finish. There is no last Sundered; there's the next one, and the arithmetic never carries a zero. You'll die on shift and the shift goes on. Most callings at least promise you a grave with a view.

"Second cost. I promise nothing. Not your life, not the town, not your soul's comfort. I'll tell you your odds honestly, which most gods won't, and which is worth less than people think — hope doesn't invoice. It just accrues.

"Third," he said, and stopped, and did the thing instead of describing it.

He took her candle off the step and put two fingers of his free hand straight into the flame, and held them there. The wick leaned. Wax ran down and found his knuckles and set, small white beads. Sera stood. "You're burning yourself."

"No," he said. "That's the third cost. The door takes its keeper by degrees. The small warmth goes first — fingers, tastes, the way a coat matters. It works inward. I'm told." He took his fingers out and handed back her candle. The skin hadn't even reddened. "That's the part that worries me."

Sera sat back down. She looked at his fingers the way a woman looks at a cracked bell she's been told is fine.

"Nine years," she said. "Wax, wick, oil — my buying. The god never came. Never billed me, never thanked me, never sent so much as a wrong psalm. And the town kept lighting candles anyway, and I kept arranging them, and do you know what an empty altar costs? Everything, annually, and no one says the price out loud. Then the Choir came up the well, and I found what a real hymn runs. Two streets. Eight bells. A boy named—"

She stopped there. He let the sentence lie where she dropped it. Even a man who collects words knows the ones you leave on the ground.

"The point," she said, "is that you're the first thing through that door that ever told me a price up front. So say the vow, Ascendant. I've been in funds for years with nothing to spend on."

You'll want her fierce here. Everyone does. She was calm, and calm was worse, and it was the first thing about her he never did learn to out-think.

"Hear the rest of my terms," he said. "I'll out-scheme you. That isn't a promise; it's a confession. When somebody comes to buy you, I'll have already bought the buyer. When somebody lies to you, I'll have already charged them for it. Price that in now. Men like me are expensive to know."

"I tithe time, not trust," Sera said. "Trust is for men who owe you less."

He filed that. There's a shelf in him for sentences that will matter; it's load-bearing.

"There's no vow," he said. "There's a door and there's me. If you must swear at something, swear at the wood."

He laid the staff flat across the step. She put her hand on the orb. So did he.

The orb took them in.

A kitchen that never cooled. A long table, more chairs than one look could count. Doors shut and standing on their own word. A garden folded away behind the house, light at late afternoon, in no hurry. A hearth, banked red, the kind that keeps.

The orb let them go. The candle came back. The boards. The cold.

"What did you see?" he said.

"A house."

"What time was the garden?"

"Late afternoon." She looked at him. "It was holding."

"It holds," he said.

Her hand was still on the orb. His had come off first. He'd wanted, for the length of one verse down below, to lay his palm over her knuckles — and had priced the wanting instead, and not bought. Some purchases are made by not making them. He was good at those. He'd had practice. I audit that hour still. The return on it compounds.

"For how long does this bind?" she said.

"Until the debt clears."

"Whose debt?"

"Yes," he said — and she laughed, the first since the well, and it was the first coin the church ever took in, though it didn't have a name yet.

The founding took nine days to gather and four minutes to perform. Most forever fits that shape.

They came up the hill in ones, past the sick doors of Weeper's Row. Sera went first, and her tithe was the rite itself: every cost in order, kept in the mouth, carried home. Marlow, who had rung the bells for four priests and outlived the fourth, tithed the tower's upkeep and his knees. Tolla the cooper tithed her Sundays and the use of a cart. Ilma — two husbands buried, one parish outlasted — named her dead when she tithed, dates and all, like a bank draft, and Drevik repeated them back word for word and said, "They're on the ledger now." He meant it kindly. It arrived as arithmetic. Sera looked at the candles after that, not at him.

A net-mender and her sister came up from downriver and tithed between them one voice — "the loud one; you'll want it eventually." Piet, a boy of sixty with a soldier's back, tithed his mornings, which he no longer slept through. Odran, a tithe-clerk out of a diocese that had downsized its miracles, had soft hands and a smile that went half the way up; he tithed bookkeeping.

And the ninth came last.

The ninth came late, tithed nights, and asked no questions at all. His candle was beeswax where everyone else's was tallow, and it smelled like a parish that hadn't been robbed yet. Drevik took the candle, said thank you, and priced, privately, the questions men don't ask. They're the expensive ones.

Nine candles on the step. Nine tithes said aloud and repeated back exact — that was the receipt; he'd never needed paper for one and wasn't starting a habit for nine people's comfort. He read them the rule — I take no one's hope and give no one's promise without saying the cost out loud — and then the bill, all three costs, same as he'd read Sera. A man who writes nothing down can at least be accused of consistency. Then he asked who stayed.

Nobody left. It was a poor beginning for a church: nine candles, one hoarse bell, a debt read aloud. Every church worth burning starts smaller than legend remembers.

A draft came down the hill and nine people hunched. He didn't. He'd stopped noticing cold two winters back and hadn't noticed that either, until Sera began leaving her cloak folded on the step beside him, the way you leave a coat for a man who won't admit the season. Before they went down, he paid the bell a visit — thumb to the rust, three notes lent — and the bell found its old job under all the crack. One note. Nine people flinched at it like a town waking. It wasn't a hymn yet. It was a down payment.

Odran waited until the others started down. "Someone should write the founding down, Ascendant. Words unwritten are words lost."

"Words unwritten are words kept where I can bill for them," Drevik said.

"A man's memory flatters the man." Odran's smile did its half.

"The ledger's mine either way," Drevik said. "I don't keep my memory for flattery. I keep it for the arithmetic."

He let Odran go down with the rest. During the rite the clerk had written anyway, small, in ledger-hand, on paper of his own. Drevik saw it and let it lie. Men write what they expect to need. Drevik remembered what Odran expected to need.

On the ninth night of the Communion, Sera sat at the altar with paper. Odran had left a ream of it on the vestry bench, "for the records." Everything the Communion has ever written began on Odran's paper. I didn't care for that then. I care for it less now.

The mitre stood on the altar rail like a guest with opinions. The mask stayed where masks stay; she'd stopped asking about it on the second day, and he had noticed, and filed that too. On the altar, the dead censer sat with its ash still in it — arranged, because she arranged ash.

"Founding liturgy," she said, not looking up. "The Devoted need something to carry home in their mouths. Costs ride badly loose."

"You'd know," he said. "You've carried one nine years."

"Ten," she said, and kept writing.

He read over her shoulder. She numbered her verses. The first was the rule dressed for walking:

We keep the door, and we say the cost. We tithe time, not blood; hope only with the bill attached; promise only with the price read out. The Sundered press. The Devoted hold. The hearth is banked, not cold. Say the cost aloud. Say it again.

"Verse eleven has a blank," he said. "Two blanks."

"For voices not sworn yet."

"It's a long walk to verse eleven."

"The table was long," she said.

He gave that a moment. Then, because a cost should be said aloud even when it's a compliment: "The garden's light was late afternoon. Write that square."

She wrote it. Her pen didn't slow, which told him more than a question would have. At the top of the page, under the numeral, in her best psalter-hand: The Veiled Communion, Being the Order and Rite of the Devoted.

"You named it," he said.

"It was going to get named. Better it owes its name to someone who has to live inside it."

"Read me verse one," he said. "The meter matters. Bills ride on meter."

She stood and read it to the empty nave, full voice. We keep the door, and we say the cost—

Under the hill, the Choir stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, the way a man stops mid-sentence when his own name crosses a market square. The candle went on with its work.

"They heard me," Sera said.

"They heard a bill read out loud," Drevik said. "Nobody's ever done that to them. Creditors hate an audit." He looked at the page, then at her ink-stained fingers, then nowhere. "A singing Choir is a Choir at work. A quiet one is a Choir deciding. Keep the verse. Write the next."

"Is that praise, Ascendant?"

"It's a purchase order," he said.

She bent back to the page. He memorized her verses as she wrote them and set none of them down; that rule was the oldest thing he owned. Under the hill the quiet held, and he had been on the wrong end of enough collections to read it right. Quiet means the bill is already in the post. This one had the church's name on it. Nine days old, one hoarse bell, nine candles' worth of assets. You'd think nobody would bother billing a thing that small.

Collectors know better.
