The law has no pulse, but it has an even sharper edge.

I stood in the center of the ritual circle, the stone floor leaching the heat from my boots. The air in the Fold was heavy, a suffocating shroud thick with the scent of old, cloying incense and the sharp, metallic tang of unsheathed steel. This was not a trial of words alone. Under the Blazon’s Code, a duel of law requires a duel of presence. To claim a breach of the Threshold is to invite the blade to prove the lie; it is to step into the arena and accept that if your spirit falters, the law will strike you down.

Vane stood opposite me, perhaps ten paces of cold, empty space separating us. He did not move, yet the distance between us felt crowded, as if his resentment occupied more volume than his physical body. He held his claim like a blunt instrument—heavy, graceless, and designed to crush rather than to dissect. He had come to accuse the household of a breach, a fundamental instability in the binding of the new wards. He called it a rot. He called it a weakness that Drevik could no longer contain.

I did not look at the crowd. I could feel them—the Devoted, their faces pale and indistinct in the low, flickering light of the Fold’s afternoon. I did not look at the edges of the room. I looked only at the line of tension that stretched between Vane and myself, a taut wire ready to snap.

Drevik sat in the high chair of the hearth, the throne of the house. He did not wear the ornate mask of a judge, but he possessed the terrifying gravity of a mountain. The crystal mitre upon his brow caught the dim light, the void crystals within it drifting in slow, rhythmic, agonizing orbits, like dying stars caught in a localized event horizon. He was silent. He was the anchor. He did not need to speak to validate the law; his presence was the law. He sat with his hands resting motionless on the arms of the chair, his dark vestments unmoving, his galaxy-void eyes fixed on the center of the circle. He did not blink. He did not lean forward. He simply existed, and because he existed, the room held its breath.

"The evidence is in the breach, Vesper," Vane said. The sound of his voice was a rasp, scraping against the silence. He stepped forward, his boots grinding against the grit of the stone. "The wards are fraying. The stitch is failing. You claim stability, yet the seams of the Fold are pulling apart. Look at the perimeter. The energy bleeds into the gray. We are losing the grip on the threshold."

"A seam does not pull unless it is forced," I said. My voice was plain. It did me no favors, but it did not tremble, and in a duel of law, a tremor is a confession. "A breach is a puncture, Vane. And a puncture leaves a mark."

Vane let out a short, derisive huff. "The marks are everywhere. The tension is rising. The Iron Choir presses harder against our borders every night, and your protection weakens. We are being hollowed out by our own inadequacy."

I felt the weight of the Blaton’s Code pressing against my ribs, a physical constriction. The Code demanded precision. It demanded the truth of the strike. I could feel the sweat cooling on my neck, a thin, slick sensation. I needed a pivot. I needed the truth to stop being an abstract concept and start being a physical reality.

I turned my gaze away from him and toward the edge of the circle, where Wren sat. She was tucked into the shadows of a pillar, her small frame looking even more fragile than usual. Her fingers were working a length of silver thread, a nervous, repetitive motion that drew the eye. She was the memory of the Bazaar, the keeper of the seams, and today, she was the evidence.

Vane had intended to use the instability of the wards to claim a lack of stewardship. He intended to use the fear of the Sundered to force a restructuring of the household, to strip Drevik of his autonomy and place the reins in the hands of the more "pragmatic" Council. He had brought a lie wrapped in the language of necessity, a beautifully crafted deception that relied on the ambiguity of the unseen.

"You speak of fraying," I said. I began to walk. The distance I kept between myself and Vane was measured, a tactical gap designed to show I was not retreating, but advancing. "But you doer not speak of the puncture itself. You speak of the effect, Vane. You speak of the wound, but you ignore the blade."

Vane narrowed his eyes, his hand twitching toward the hilt of his ceremonial dagger. "The wound is the reality. The cause is secondary to the collapse."

"Not in a court of law," I countered. I reached the edge of the circle, stopping just short of Wren. I did not touch her. To touch a witness in a duel of law is to taint the testimony, to suggest a conspiracy of flesh. "To claim a breach, one must identify the point of entry. If the cloth is torn, we must know if it was worn by age or cut by steel."

Wren lifted her head. Her eyes were wide, the light of the room reflecting in them like shattered glass. She looked terrified, her breath coming in shallow, jagged sips. She did not speak; she had been warned that her silence was her only protection.

"The tension is not in the wards," I said, turning back to Vane. I pointed a steady finger toward the collar of Wren's tunic, where the fabric met the pale, bruised skin of her throat. "The tension is in the application. Look at the stitch-marks."

Vane paused. His face tightened, a momentary, microscopic fracture in his carefully maintained composure. "The stitch-marks are irrelevant to the stability of the Threshold. We are discussing the cosmic binding, not a seamstress's error."

"The stitch-marks are the record of the force applied," I said, my voice gaining a sharp, crystalline edge. I stepped closer to him, forcing him to meet my eyes. "Look at the way the thread meets the skin. It is not the wear of time. It is not the natural settling of the weave. Look at the uneven pulling, the way the silver has been dragged through the dermis. It is the mark of a deliberate, localized tension. Someone has been pulling at the seams, Vane. Someone has been trying to unmake the binding from the inside, to create the very breach you now protest."

"You are grasping at shadows, Vesper," Vane spat, though his voice lacked its earlier thunder. "A single girl's collar cannot prove a systemic failure."

"It isn't just the collar," I pressed, the adrenaline finally beginning to surge, warming my limbs. "It is the pattern. The theft of energy from the wards requires a tether—a way to draw the power out without triggering the alarms. And where do we find the most delicate, most frequent contact with the weave? In the hands of the keepers. In the hands of those who mend what is broken. The instability you call 'rot' is actually a drain. A deliberate, calculated extraction. You didn't come here to fix a breach, Vane. You came here to announce that you had finally succeeded in making one."

The room fell into a sudden, vacuum-like silence. The weight of the accusation hung in the air, heavy and suffocating. I could feel the eyes of the Devoted burning into me, the shift in the atmosphere as the tide of suspicion turned.

I looked toward the high chair.

Drevik had not moved, but the air around him had changed. The void crystals in his mitre had slowed their drift, settling into a heavy, somber rotation. He looked at the collar of Wren’t tunic. He looked at the way the dim light hit the disturbed, angry embroidery. He looked at Vane.

He did not offer a verdict. He did not need to. A small, sly smirk—a terrifying, ghostly expression—floated across the edge of his white-ceramic mask. It was the look of a predator that had watched a trap being set, only to see the trapper stumble into the teeth of it.

Vane’s hand dropped from his side, his fingers curling uselessly against his thigh. The silence in the Fold became absolute. It was a heavy, suffocating thing, a silence that held the weight of a closing door.

"The verdict is recorded," I said. My heart did not race; it beat with a cold, rhythmic certainty. "The breach is not an external pressure. It is an internal sabotage. The law is clear. The claim is voided by the discovery of intent."

Vane opened his mouth, perhaps to protest, perhaps to scream, but the words died in his throat. The moment the evidence was named, his authority dissolved. The law had found its edge, and it had cut him clean. He was no longer a claimant; he was a suspect.

The tension in the room did not break; it merely changed shape. It moved from the fear of the Sundered to the sharp, jagged suspicion of the hearth. The Devoted began to whisper, a low, frantic hum like a swarm of disturbed hornets.

I turned away from him. I walked back to the center of the circle, my boots feeling heavy and leaden on the stone. I had won, but the victory felt like a puncture in my own soul. The triumph was hollow, leaving me feeling exposed and raw.

I looked toward the high chair. Drevik was watching me. His gaze was vast, kind, and utterly unreadable. He was the master of the threshold, and even now, with the traitor exposed, I could not tell if he was mourning the breach or simply calculating the cost of the repair.

I reached down and placed my blade back into its sheath with a definitive click. I walked toward the shadows of the hall, seeking the relief of the dark. The shadows behind me stayed dark, and for a moment, I thought I was safe.

But as I passed through the heavy threshold of the room, a flicker of movement caught the corner of my eye. Near the edge of the darkened corridor, a shadow moved—a subtle, deliberate shift that did not belong to the breathing crowd or the settling stone. It was a movement of intent.

Vane was not the only one who had been pulling at the threads. I realized then, with a jolt of cold terror, that the tapestry was already unraveling, and I had only just begun to see the pattern.
