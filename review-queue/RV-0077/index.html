The Humanization Toolkit

A 4-Pass Editing System for Fiction Writers Who Use AI

PART ONE: WHY THIS EXISTS

You're Not Cheating. You're Editing.

You used AI to help draft your fiction. Maybe ChatGPT gave you a first chapter. Maybe Claude helped you brainstorm dialogue. Maybe you wrote the whole thing yourself and used AI to polish a scene.

Whatever your process — that's fine. AI is a tool, like a thesaurus or a writing group.

But here's the problem: AI leaves fingerprints on your prose.

Even when the ideas are yours, the sentences can carry patterns that experienced readers and detection tools recognize. Not because you did anything wrong, but because large language models produce text using statistical probability — and probability creates repetition.

This toolkit is not about "tricking detectors." It's about making your writing genuinely better. The same changes that strip AI patterns from your prose are the same changes that make your fiction more vivid, more human, and more yours.

Think of this as a specialized revision system. You wouldn't publish a first draft without editing it. If your first draft had AI assistance, it needs a specialized kind of editing. That's what this provides.

How We Got Here: The AI Writing Problem

In 2023, "delve" became the poster child for AI writing. The word appeared in ChatGPT output with absurd frequency. Writers who'd never used the word in their lives suddenly found it in their drafts.

But "delve" was just the tip. By 2024, researchers had documented dozens of AI-tell patterns:

  • Vocabulary fingerprints: Words like "tapestry," "pivotal," "underscore," "fostering," "intricate," and "showcase" appear far more often in AI text than human writing
  • Structural patterns: Em dashes used as universal punctuation, "Not X, but Y" constructions, sentences of uniform length
  • Content habits: Hedging language ("it's important to note"), forced significance ("stands as a testament"), and the rule of three (always three examples, never two or four)
  • Tone problems: Emotional flatness, corporate neutrality, and the inability to take a strong position

By 2025-2026, AI models had evolved. The "delve" problem faded. But new patterns emerged: "emphasizing," "showcasing," "enhance," "highlighting." Different words, same statistical fingerprint.

The research is clear: heavy LLM users can detect AI text about 90% of the time. Casual readers might not consciously notice, but they feel something is off. The prose sounds "smooth but hollow." It reads like it was generated by a committee.

This toolkit exists because no one has built a systematic editing process for this specific problem.

Why "Humanization Tools" Don't Work

You've probably seen them: BypassGPT, NaturalWrite, Humbot, Undetectable AI. These software tools claim to "humanize" your text by paraphrasing it past detection.

Here's the problem: they don't make your writing better. They make it different.

Running AI text through a humanizer is like running it through a second AI. You get a paraphrased version of the same content with the same structural problems — different words, same rhythm, same flat emotional register. Sometimes worse, because the paraphrasing introduces new awkwardness.

A Reddit user who tested GPTZero extensively put it plainly: "The only way I've ever managed to get a 100% human rating was to write from scratch. Rewriting LLM text in my own words still came back 80-90% human."

80-90% is not 100%. That 10-20% gap? It's not about detection scores. It's about craft. It's the difference between prose that sounds like you and prose that sounds like you-but-slightly-off.

This toolkit teaches you how to close that gap systematically, not by paraphrasing, but by revising with intention.

PART TWO: HOW THIS SYSTEM WORKS

The 4-Pass Method

This toolkit uses a structured editing approach. You make four separate passes through your manuscript, each targeting a different category of AI patterns:

| Pass | Name | Target | Time per 1K words | |------|------|--------|-------------------| | 1 | Pattern Purge | AI vocabulary, punctuation habits, formulaic constructions | 10-15 min | | 2 | Rhythm Restore | Sentence structure, paragraph flow, burstiness | 15-20 min | | 3 | Voice Injection | Character voice, emotional specificity, human imperfection | 20-30 min | | 4 | Final Polish | Read-aloud verification, detection awareness, quality check | 10-15 min |

Total time: ~60-80 minutes per 1,000 words. For a 5,000-word short story, that's about 5-7 hours of focused editing. For an 80,000-word novel, plan 40-55 hours across multiple sessions.

Yes, that's real work. That's the point. The craft of writing has always required revision. AI drafts just need a different kind of revision — one that addresses the specific patterns AI introduces.

Important Principles

  1. Passes are sequential. Don't try to do all four at once. Pattern Purge first, then Rhythm, then Voice, then Polish. Each pass builds on the last.
  1. Be ruthless in Pass 1. The AI Vocabulary Hit List exists because these words and phrases are statistically overrepresented in AI output. If you find one, replace it — don't negotiate with yourself about whether you "really mean it." You can always change the replacement later.
  1. Trust your ear in Pass 2. The rhythm tests are objective (sentence length variation, paragraph structure), but the final arbiter is whether it sounds right when you read it aloud. If it sounds robotic, it probably reads robotic.
  1. Get weird in Pass 3. This is where you inject the things AI can't: your specific memories, your character's unique speech patterns, the unexpected metaphor that doesn't follow the obvious path. AI writes the statistical average of human expression. You need to write the outlier.
  1. Pass 4 is non-negotiable. Reading your work aloud catches things no checklist can. Do it.

What This Toolkit Is Not

  • Not a detection beater. If your goal is solely to pass GPTZero, this is the wrong product. The goal is better prose.
  • Not a replacement for craft. If you can't write a compelling scene, this won't help. Learn scene craft first.
  • Not a one-click solution. You have to do the work. Each pass requires you to actively engage with your text.
  • Not software. These are worksheets and guides. You'll need a pen, a highlighter, and your manuscript.

The Humanization Toolkit — Part Two: The Four Passes

PASS 1: PATTERN PURGE

Removing AI Fingerprints from Your Prose

What Pattern Purge Does

This pass targets the identifiable, searchable patterns that AI leaves in text. These are words, phrases, punctuation habits, and formulaic constructions that appear disproportionately in AI output compared to human writing.

Think of this as weeding a garden. You're pulling out the obvious invasives before you start landscaping.

Section 1.1: The AI Vocabulary Hit List

These words and phrases appear far more often in AI-generated text than in human writing. Academic studies (Juzek 2024, Kobak 2024, Merrill 2025) have confirmed statistically significant increases in these terms post-2022, when ChatGPT became widely available.

When you find one of these in your draft, replace it. Don't argue with yourself about whether you "naturally use this word." You might — but the cumulative density of these words is what signals AI. Replacing one doesn't hurt. Leaving all of them does.

The 2023-2024 Era (GPT-4 Patterns)

These words peaked in the first wave of AI writing and remain common:

| AI Pattern Word | Replace With | |---|---| | additionally | [also, or just start the sentence differently] | | boasts (meaning "has") | [has, features, includes — or just state the fact] | | bolstered | [supported, strengthened, backed] | | crucial | [important, key, vital — or rephrase to show why] | | delve | [explore, examine, dig into — or just remove and state the point] | | emphasizing | [showing, highlighting — or cut and demonstrate instead] | | enduring | [lasting, continuing — or be specific about duration] | | foster/fostering | [encourage, support, build — or rephrase] | | garner | [earn, gain, collect — or be specific about how] | | intricate | [complex, detailed, complicated — or describe the complexity] | | interplay | [relationship, interaction — or name the specific connection] | | key (as adjective) | [important, main, central — or show why it matters] | | landscape (abstract) | [field, area, scene — or be specific about what] | | meticulous/meticulously | [careful, thorough, precise — or show the care through detail] | | pivotal | [important, critical, turning-point — or show the turning] | | robust | [strong, solid, reliable — or demonstrate the strength] | | showcase | [display, show, present — or just present it] | | tapestry (abstract) | [mix, blend, collection — or name the specific elements] | | testament | [proof, evidence, sign — or show what it proves] | | underscore (verb) | [show, prove, highlight — or just state the point directly] | | valuable | [useful, important, helpful — or show the value] | | vibrant | [colorful, lively, active — or describe what makes it vibrant] |

The 2024-2025 Era (GPT-4o Patterns)

These replaced the earlier patterns as AI models evolved:

| AI Pattern Word | Replace With | |---|---| | align with | [match, fit, follow — or just state the connection] | | enhanced | [improved, better, stronger — or show how] | | highlighting | [showing, pointing out — or just show it] | | showcasing | [showing, presenting — or just present it] | | serves as | [is, acts as — usually just "is" works fine] | | stands as | [is, remains — usually "is" works fine] | | functions as | [is, works as — usually "is" works fine] |

The 2025-2026 Era (GPT-5 / Current Patterns)

The newest generation of AI-tell words:

| AI Pattern Word | Replace With | |---|---| | emphasizing (still dominant) | [showing, demonstrating — or cut entirely] | | enhance/enhancing | [improve, make better — or show the improvement] | | highlighting (still dominant) | [showing, pointing to — or demonstrate it] | | showcasing (still dominant) | [presenting, showing — or just show it] | | "it's important to note" | [delete entirely, then make the note directly] | | "serves as a reminder" | [cut — if it matters, the reader will remember] | | "represents a significant" | [is important — or show the significance] |

Critical rule: Don't just swap one AI word for another. "Delve" → "explore" is fine. "Delve" → "fostering" is replacing one AI fingerprint with another.

Section 1.2: Em Dash Audit

AI overuses em dashes (—) as a universal connector. Research confirms they appear at 2-5x the rate in AI text compared to human writing.

How to Audit

  1. Search your manuscript for "—" (em dash)
  2. For each em dash, ask: Could this be a comma, period, colon, or semicolon instead?
  3. If yes, replace it. Em dashes should feel like a deliberate dramatic pause, not a default connector.

AI pattern:

The system analyzes the data, revealing key insights—and this fundamentally changes how we approach the problem—and ensures better outcomes.

Human revision:

The system analyzes the data. The insights it reveals change how we approach the problem. It also ensures better outcomes.

AI pattern:

She walked into the room—exhausted, frustrated, and ready to leave—and immediately noticed the envelope on the table.

Human revision:

She walked into the room, exhausted and frustrated, and immediately noticed the envelope on the table.

The Rule of Thumb: If you have more than 1-2 em dashes per 1,000 words, you probably have too many. Cut at least half.

Section 1.3: Hedging Language Removal

AI models are trained to be "helpful and harmless," which manifests as constant hedging — qualifying every statement so nothing is absolute.

Common AI Hedges to Find and Remove

| AI Hedge | What To Do | |---|---| | "It's important to note that..." | Delete the hedge. Just state the point. | | "Generally speaking..." | Delete. If you can't state it confidently, reconsider including it. | | "To some extent..." | Either commit to the statement or cut it. | | "From a broader perspective..." | Delete. Or rewrite to show the specific perspective. | | "In many ways..." | Delete. Name the specific way or cut. | | "It could be argued that..." | Either make the argument or don't. Hedging weakens fiction. | | "Some might say..." | Who? Name the character who believes this. | | "Arguably..." | Same as above. Fiction needs conviction. | | "While it's true that X, Y is also worth considering..." | Pick a side. Your characters have opinions. | | "This raises important questions about..." | Name the question. Fiction explores, it doesn't just raise. |

The fiction writer's principle: Characters have opinions. Narrators take positions. Even an omniscient narrator has a perspective. Hedging makes fiction feel like a committee wrote it — because one did, statistically.

Section 1.4: Formulaic Construction Buster

AI relies on predictable sentence templates. Here are the most common ones and how to break them.

"Not X, but Y" / "Not just X, but also Y"

AI pattern: "She wasn't just a good detective, but also a compassionate friend." Fix: "She was a good detective. She was also a compassionate friend, though she'd never admit it." Why it works: Two sentences. Character voice. A detail that only a human writer would add.

"From X to Y" Range Construction

AI pattern: "From bustling cities to serene landscapes, the country offered everything." Fix: "The country had cities that never slept and valleys where you could hear yourself think." Why it works: Specific instead of generic. "Bustling" and "serene" are AI defaults.

Present Participial Phrase (-ing construction)

AI pattern: "The system analyzed the data, revealing key insights about the market." Fix: "The system analyzed the data. The results showed three key insights about the market." Why it works: AI uses "-ing" participial phrases at 2-5x the rate of human writers. Breaking them into two sentences immediately sounds more human.

Rule of Three

AI pattern: "She was smart, determined, and resourceful." Fix: "She was smart. Determined, too — but mostly she was the kind of person who'd rather show you than tell you." Why it works: AI defaults to three items. Two or four breaks the pattern. Adding character-specific detail replaces generic adjectives.

"X is a testament to Y" / "X stands as a reminder of Y"

AI pattern: "The monument stands as a testament to the resilience of the community." Fix: "The monument's still there. Cracked, weathered, but standing. Like the community that built it." Why it works: Concrete detail replaces abstract significance. The reader feels the testament rather than being told about it.

Section 1.5: The Superficial Significance Detector

AI inflates the importance of everything. A train station isn't just a station — it's "a pivotal hub reflecting the enduring spirit of connectivity." A local restaurant isn't just a restaurant — it "serves as a testament to the vibrant culinary landscape."

In fiction, this is deadly. Every paragraph reads like a tourism brochure.

The Test

For every statement about significance, importance, or legacy in your manuscript, ask:

  1. Would a real person say this? Would anyone actually call a train station "a testament to enduring connectivity"?
  2. Does this serve the story? Does the reader need to know the historical significance, or do they need to know what the character sees when they walk through the door?
  3. Can I show instead of tell? Instead of "the ancient cathedral stood as a symbol of faith," can you describe the way light falls through cracked stained glass onto a character's face?

Examples

AI version: "The old bookstore nestled in the heart of the historic district, serving as a vibrant reminder of the community's rich cultural tapestry."

Human version: "The old bookstore on Maple Street still smelled like dust and last century. Mrs. Chen kept it open Tuesdays through Saturdays, same as always, even after the coffee shop next door closed."

Pattern Purge Checklist

Before moving to Pass 2, verify:

  • [ ] Searched for and replaced all AI Vocabulary Hit List words
  • [ ] Audited em dashes (max 1-2 per 1,000 words)
  • [ ] Removed hedging language ("it's important to note," "generally speaking")
  • [ ] Broke "Not X, but Y" constructions
  • [ ] Eliminated "From X to Y" ranges where they feel generic
  • [ ] Broke participial -ing phrases into separate sentences where possible
  • [ ] Broke rule-of-three patterns (replace with 2 or 4 items, or character-specific detail)
  • [ ] Cut "testament/reminder/symbol" significance statements
  • [ ] Removed promotional language ("boasts," "vibrant," "nestled in the heart of")
  • [ ] Checked for "serves as" / "stands as" / "functions as" and replaced with "is" where possible

The Humanization Toolkit — Part Three: Passes 2, 3, and 4

PASS 2: RHYTHM RESTORE

Fixing Mechanical Sentence Structure

What Rhythm Restore Does

AI text has a heartbeat, and it's metronomic. Every sentence lands at roughly the same length. Every paragraph has the same shape. The result reads like elevator music — technically fine, emotionally flat.

Human writing has burstiness: short punchy sentences mixed with longer flowing ones. Fragments alongside complete thoughts. Paragraphs that breathe.

This pass fixes the rhythm.

Section 2.1: The Burstiness Audit

Burstiness is the technical term for variation in sentence complexity. Humans write with high burstiness — a three-word sentence followed by a twenty-word one. AI writes with low burstiness — every sentence hovers around 15-20 words.

How to Audit

  1. Pick a 500-word section of your manuscript
  2. Count the words in each sentence
  3. Calculate: what's your shortest sentence? Your longest? Your median?
  4. Look for the variation ratio: longest sentence ÷ shortest sentence

Healthy fiction: Variation ratio of 5:1 or higher (e.g., shortest sentence 3 words, longest 18+) AI pattern: Variation ratio of 3:1 or lower (e.g., shortest 8 words, longest 22)

The Quick Test

Read a page aloud. Does every sentence hit the same rhythm? If you can tap a steady beat while reading, the rhythm is too uniform.

Fixes

| Problem | Fix | |---|---| | All sentences 12-18 words | Break some in half. Combine others. Aim for 3-word and 25-word sentences. | | No sentence fragments | Add 2-3 intentional fragments per page. "Right." "Of course." "Again." | | No one-sentence paragraphs | Create them for emphasis, scene transitions, or emotional beats. | | Paragraphs all similar length | Vary: some 1 sentence, some 6-8. | | Every sentence is declarative | Mix in questions, exclamations, and imperative sentences. |

Section 2.2: Paragraph Rhythm Worksheet

For each paragraph in a scene, categorize its shape:

| Shape | Description | Example | |---|---|---| | Tower | One long sentence building | "The rain started slow, just a few drops pinging off the dumpster lid, but within seconds it was a wall of water..." | | Staccato | Several short punchy sentences | "She ran. Door. Lock. Breathe." | | Wave | Medium sentences with rhythm | "He opened the letter. Read it twice. Put it down on the table and stared at the wall for a long time." | | Flow | Long, connected sentences | "Margaret had been waiting for this call for three years, ever since the night David left his keys on the counter and walked out without saying goodbye, and now that it was finally here she couldn't bring herself to pick up the phone." |

The rule: No more than two consecutive paragraphs of the same shape. Mix them up.

Section 2.3: Structural Pattern Breakers

AI relies on predictable sentence structures. Here are the most common and how to break them.

"X, doing Y" (Participial Phrase Opener)

AI: "Walking through the market, she noticed the strange vendor." Human: "She walked through the market. The strange vendor caught her eye." or "Through the market and past the vendors until one of them — the strange one — made her stop."

"With X, Y" (Instrumental Phrase)

AI: "With a determined expression, she entered the boardroom." Human: "She entered the boardroom. Her expression said she wasn't leaving without what she came for."

"As X, Y" (Temporal Clause)

AI: "As the sun set, the city transformed." Human: "The sun set. The city changed." or "By the time the sun hit the horizon, the city had already become something else."

"X is Y" (Copula Dominance)

AI: "The building was tall. The man was angry. The situation was complicated." Human: "The building scraped the clouds. The man's jaw set hard. Everything tangled into a mess nobody wanted to untangle."

The pattern: AI defaults to "to be" verbs (is, was, were, had been). Replace with active verbs wherever possible. Every "was" is a chance for a stronger verb.

Section 2.4: The Monotony Meter

Rate each scene on a 1-5 scale:

| Factor | 1 (Robotic) | 3 (Mixed) | 5 (Vivid) | |---|---|---|---| | Sentence length variety | All similar | Some variation | Wide range | | Paragraph variety | All same length | Some short/long | Deliberate variety | | Sentence openers | Most start same way | Mixed starters | Every sentence starts differently | | Verb strength | Mostly "to be" | Mixed | Strong active verbs | | Sensory detail | Abstract/summary | Some specifics | Grounded in senses |

If any row scores 1-2, that's your priority for this scene.

PASS 3: VOICE INJECTION

Adding Authentic Human Elements

What Voice Injection Does

Pass 1 removed AI patterns. Pass 2 fixed the rhythm. Pass 3 puts something back: your voice.

AI can't replicate:

  • Your specific memories and experiences
  • Your characters' unique speech patterns
  • The weird metaphors only you would make
  • The imperfect, human way real people talk and think
  • The specific sensory details that make a scene feel lived-in

This pass is about replacing the statistical average with the individual.

Section 3.1: Character Voice Differentiation

AI tends to give all characters the same "smart, articulate, measured" voice. Real people don't talk like that.

The Voice Matrix

For each major character, define:

| Element | Questions to Answer | |---|---| | Vocabulary level | College-educated? Street-smart? Uses slang? Avoids big words? | | Sentence length | Short and punchy? Long and rambling? Mixed? | | Emotional expression | Volatile? Controlled? Sarcastic? Understated? | | Hedge patterns | Do they say "maybe," "sort of," "I guess"? Or are they direct? | | Pet phrases | What 2-3 words/phrases does this character repeat? | | What they DON'T say | What topics do they avoid? What emotions do they suppress? | | Contradictions | What does this character say that contradicts what they do? |

The Dialogue Test

Read a page of dialogue with all speaker tags removed. Can you tell who's speaking? If not, the voices aren't differentiated enough.

AI pattern: All characters sound like the narrator — articulate, measured, emotionally even.

Fix: Give each character speech imperfections. Real people:

  • Start sentences they don't finish
  • Change direction mid-thought
  • Use filler words differently (some say "um," others "like," others "you know")
  • Have topics that make them talk faster or slower
  • Reveal personality through what they avoid saying

Section 3.2: Idiom and Slang Injection

AI text is linguistically "clean" — no regional dialects, no era-specific slang, no character-specific expressions.

The Specificity Exercise

For each character, list:

  1. Three idioms or expressions they'd use that no other character would
  2. One phrase they overuse (their verbal tic)
  3. A topic they get emotional about (and how their speech changes when discussing it)
  4. Their education level and how it shows in vocabulary
  5. Their generation and the slang that comes with it

Then go through your manuscript and find places where generic dialogue can be replaced with character-specific language.

Section 3.3: The Imperfection Audit

AI text is too clean. Too polished. Every sentence is a complete thought. Every transition is smooth. Real writing — and real thought — is messier.

Planned Imperfections to Add

| Imperfection | How to Use It | Example | |---|---|---| | Sentence fragments | For emphasis, emotion, or stream-of-consciousness | "Not this time." "Never again." "Right." | | Interrupted thoughts | Dashes for mid-sentence redirects | "I was going to say—never mind, it doesn't matter." | | Run-on sentences | For breathless, anxious, or excited narration | "He ran and ran and the streetlights blurred and the cold air burned his lungs and he couldn't stop because stopping meant dying." | | Repetition for emphasis | Deliberately repeating a word or phrase | "She kept walking. Walking and walking. The road didn't end." | | Colloquial grammar | Starting sentences with "And" or "But" or "So" | "So she left. And she didn't look back." | | Incomplete comparisons | Metaphors that trail off | "It was like that time in—no, it wasn't like anything." | | Self-correction | Characters catching themselves | "He was the smartest—I mean, he thought he was the smartest person in the room." |

The rule: Add 3-5 imperfections per page in dialogue. Add 1-2 per page in narration. Not sloppy — deliberately human.

Section 3.4: Emotional Specificity

AI describes emotions in vague, universal terms: "She felt a mix of emotions," "He was overwhelmed by the situation," "A sense of dread washed over her."

Human writers name the specific emotion and ground it in the body.

The Emotion Replacement Table

| AI Vague Emotion | Human Specific Replacements | |---|---| | "A mix of emotions" | Anger + relief. Grief + hunger. Jealousy + gratitude. Name the two. | | "Overwhelmed" | "Her chest tightened like someone was sitting on it." / "He couldn't keep track of everything happening, so he stopped trying." | | "A sense of dread" | "The hair on her arms stood up." / "His stomach dropped." / "She knew before she opened the door. Just knew." | | "Felt conflicted" | "Part of her wanted to stay. A bigger part wanted to run." / "He could argue both sides, which meant he'd probably do nothing." | | "Emotional" | Name it: angry, scared, jealous, grieving, ashamed, relieved, confused. "Emotional" is never a specific enough word. |

The Sensory Grounding Exercise

For every emotional beat in your manuscript:

  1. Name the specific emotion (not "emotional" or "overwhelmed")
  2. Ground it in a physical sensation (chest, stomach, hands, throat, jaw)
  3. Add one sensory detail from the environment (what the character sees, hears, smells, touches)

PASS 4: FINAL POLISH

Verification and Quality Check

What Final Polish Does

Pass 4 is your safety net. It catches what the first three passes missed and verifies the overall quality.

Section 4.1: The Read-Aloud Protocol

This is the single most important step in the entire toolkit. If you skip everything else, do this.

How to Read Aloud

  1. Read the entire manuscript aloud. Not skimmed. Not mumbled. Actually read every word.
  2. Mark where you stumble. If you can't read it smoothly, it needs rewriting.
  3. Mark where you lose interest. If you zone out while reading your own work, your reader will too.
  4. Mark where the rhythm feels wrong. You'll hear it before you can articulate what's wrong.
  5. Mark sentences that sound like an essay. Fiction shouldn't sound like a Wikipedia article.

What to Listen For

  • Smooth vs. stuttering: If you stumble, the sentence is too complex or awkward
  • Monotone vs. musical: If every sentence has the same rhythm, it needs more variation
  • Corporate vs. human: If it sounds like it could appear in a business memo, it needs more personality
  • Vague vs. specific: If emotions or descriptions are fuzzy, name the specific thing

Section 4.2: The Detection Awareness Check

This is NOT about "beating detectors." It's about understanding what they look for so you can make informed decisions.

What Detectors Actually Check

Modern AI detectors (GPTZero, Originality, etc.) analyze:

  1. Perplexity: How predictable is each word given the previous words? AI text is less perplexing (more predictable).
  2. Burstiness: How much does sentence complexity vary? AI text has low burstiness.
  3. Vocabulary distribution: Does the word choice match AI statistical patterns?

The Honest Assessment

After completing Passes 1-3, your text should:

  • Have higher perplexity (unexpected word choices, specific details)
  • Have higher burstiness (varied sentence lengths and structures)
  • Have a different vocabulary distribution (AI-tell words removed, character-specific words added)

What NOT to Do

  • Don't add deliberate typos. Detectors don't check for spelling, and it makes your work look unprofessional.
  • Don't add random "human" patterns. Inserting "um" or "like" randomly reads as forced.
  • Don't use "humanizer" software. It paraphrases your text through another AI, adding different patterns on top of existing ones.

Section 4.3: The Beta Reader Guide

Give your beta readers these specific questions:

  1. Did any dialogue sound like it could come from any character? If yes, which lines?
  2. Were there moments where you lost interest? Mark them.
  3. Did any sentences sound like a textbook or business email? Which ones?
  4. Which character's voice was most distinct? Least distinct?
  5. Were there any moments that felt "too clean" or "too perfect"?

Section 4.4: The Final Checklist

Before you publish/submit:

  • [ ] All AI Vocabulary Hit List words replaced (Pass 1)
  • [ ] Em dashes audited (max 1-2 per 1,000 words) (Pass 1)
  • [ ] Hedging language removed (Pass 1)
  • [ ] Formulaic constructions broken (Pass 1)
  • [ ] Sentence length variation confirmed (burstiness ratio 5:1+) (Pass 2)
  • [ ] Paragraph shapes varied throughout (Pass 2)
  • [ ] "To be" verbs replaced with active verbs where possible (Pass 2)
  • [ ] Character voices differentiated (dialogue test passed) (Pass 3)
  • [ ] Idioms and slang added per character (Pass 3)
  • [ ] 3-5 imperfections per page in dialogue, 1-2 in narration (Pass 3)
  • [ ] Emotions specified and grounded in body/senses (Pass 3)
  • [ ] Full read-aloud completed (Pass 4)
  • [ ] Beta reader feedback incorporated (Pass 4)

A Final Note

This toolkit gives you a system. It doesn't give you shortcuts.

Writing good fiction has always required revision. The difference now is that AI-assisted drafts need a different kind of revision — one that addresses the specific patterns AI introduces while adding back the human elements AI can't provide.

Your voice. Your experiences. Your characters' messy, contradictory, specific humanity.

That's what makes writing worth reading. This toolkit just helps you get there systematically.

HOW TO USE THIS LIST
