Chapter 11: The Elder's Test

Chapter 11: The Elder's Test

The walk to Elder Thornwood's private chambers felt like a march toward judgment.

Drevik moved through the eastern corridor of the compound, where the shadows pooled deeper than physics should allow. The torches here burned with that distinctive violet hue the Order favored, casting everything in shades of amethyst and charcoal. His footsteps echoed against stone that had been old when the university above was nothing but pasture and ambition.

Lexi's father. The words kept circling in his mind like vultures over carrion. He was about to meet Lexi's father, a man who might have ordered his parents' murders. A man who had been testing him, pushing him into increasingly dangerous situations.

A man who, by all accounts, had every reason to want him dead—or worse.

The door at the corridor's end was ancient oak, blackened with age and carved with symbols that seemed to shift when Drevik looked at them directly. He paused before it, forcing his breathing to slow. His heart hammered against his ribs like something caged and desperate.

Get it together. You've faced Shadowlands creatures. You've walked through nightmares made flesh. This is just a conversation.

But his instincts screamed otherwise. Every shadow in this corridor felt heavy with intent, as if the darkness itself were watching, waiting to report his failures to some higher power.

He knocked. Three times, as protocol demanded.

"Enter."

The voice was deeper than he'd expected, roughened by years or sorrow or both. Drevik pushed the door open and stepped into a room that defied his expectations.

Elder Thornwood's chambers were sparse. A desk cluttered with papers and artifacts. Bookshelves lined with journals and texts in languages Drevik didn't recognize. A single window that shouldn't have existed—the compound was underground—looking out onto a twilight landscape that definitely wasn't the mortal realm.

And standing at that window, his back to Drevik, was a man who looked nothing like what he'd imagined.

Elder Marcus Thornwood was not the imposing, menacing figure Drevik had constructed in his mind. He was of average height, with the lean build of someone who had once been athletic but had let time erode his edges. His hair was steel-gray, thin at the crown, and his posture carried the particular weight of a man who had buried too many people he loved.

He turned as Drevik entered, and the resemblance to Lexi struck like a physical blow. Those eyes—the same pale green, the same assessing quality, the same guarded depth that Drevik had spent hours trying to read.

"Acolyte Markinsoul." Thornwood's voice was carefully neutral. "Please, sit."

Drevik remained standing. "With respect, Elder, I'd prefer to see your face clearly."

Something flickered in those green eyes—surprise, perhaps, or approval. "Wise. Your mother would have done the same."

The words landed like a punch. Drevik's breath caught, and he watched Thornwood's expression for any sign of deception, any hint that this was manipulation.

"Sit," Thornwood said again, and this time Drevik obeyied, lowering himself into the chair across from the Elder's desk. "We have much to discuss, and little time before the full moon ceremony. I imagine you have questions."

"Hundreds."

"Then let's begin with the one that brought you here." Thornwood settled into his own chair, the leather creaking beneath him. "You want to know if I killed your parents."

Direct. Brutally so. Drevik hadn't expected that.

"Did you?"

"No."

The word hung between them, simple and stark. Drevik searched the Elder's face for the tells he'd learned to read—micro-expressions, shifts in the shadows around a person's form that indicated deception. In the Order, lying was an art form, but Elder Thornwood's shadows remained still.

"I believe you," Drevik said slowly. "But that doesn't mean you're innocent."

"No, it doesn't." Thornwood leaned forward, folding his hands on the desk. "I've done many things I'm not proud of, Acolyte. Things that would horrify you. But I did not kill Elena and Marcus Markinsoul. I loved them both, in different ways, and their deaths have haunted me for twenty-one years."

Elena and Marcus. His parents' names, spoken aloud by someone who had known them. The sensation was disorienting, like hearing a song from a language you'd forgotten you once spoke.

"You knew them well."

"Better than most." Thornwood's gaze drifted to the impossible window, that twilight landscape beyond. "Your mother and I were initiates together. We trained side by side, advanced through the ranks together. I thought..." He paused, jaw tightening. "I thought we would build a life together. She had other plans."

The implication settled slowly. "You loved her."

"I did. Desperately." No shame in the admission, only an old wound scarred over but never fully healed. "She chose your father instead. A man I initially resented and eventually came to respect. Marcus Markinsoul was stubborn, brilliant, and absolutely devoted to her. He made her happy in ways I never could have."

Drevik's hands clenched beneath the desk. "And when they died, you were here. Already an Elder."

"I was. And yes, I know how that looks." Thornwood met his gaze without flinching. "I was one of five Elders then, as I am now. Any one of us could have given the order. I've spent two decades trying to discover which one, and I have failed."

"Why should I believe you're not simply protecting yourself?"

"Because you have your mother's eyes, and your father's infuriating need to question everything, and I am tired." The words carried exhaustion bone-deep. "I am tired of secrets. Tired of watching promising acolytes die because someone in this Order feeds information to our enemies. Tired of burying people I care about."

Drevik absorbed this, weighing each word. "You said you've been testing me. The attacks, the assignments—they've all been deliberate."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Thornwood rose from his chair, moving to a shelf and pulling a leather-bound journal from between larger tomes. "Your parents were investigating something before they died. They believed there was a traitor among the Elders—someone working with the Shadowborn, entities that should have been our enemies. They were close to a discovery when they were killed."

"And you think this traitor is still active."

"I know they are." He turned back to Drevik, journal in hand. "The attacks on you haven't been random. Someone has been testing your abilities, probing your weaknesses, trying to determine if you inherited your parents' talents."

The attacks that nearly killed me. The ambush in the Shadowlands. The creature that shouldn't have been able to find me.

"You've been putting me in danger intentionally," Drevik said, the realization bitter in his mouth. "To see if I could survive."

"To see if you were worth investing in." Thornwood's voice hardened. "To see if you had the potential to finish what your parents started. The Order is rotting from within, Acolyte. Someone in a position of power is feeding our people to the Shadowborn, and I need to know if you're the one who can help me root them out."

"And if I wasn't?"

"Then you'd be dead already." Blunt, matter-of-fact. "The traitor would have seen to that. Your survival tells me you have your parents' gifts—and possibly their fate."

Drevik stood abruptly, the chair scraping against stone. "You're using me as bait."

"I'm using you as a weapon." Thornwood didn't flinch from the accusation. "The difference is, I'm trying to ensure you survive long enough to strike. Others want you dead before you can become a threat."

The room felt too small suddenly, the shadows pressing close. Drevik's pulse thundered in his ears, but he forced himself to think, to parse the implications.

"If you're telling the truth, why reveal this now? Why not continue the tests?"

"Because tomorrow night's ceremony changes everything." Thornwood moved closer, his voice dropping. "The full moon empowers our magic, but it also thins the barriers between realms. If the traitor acts, it will be then. And you need to be prepared."

"Prepared for what?"

"Prepared to choose a side." The Elder's green eyes bore into his. "There are those in the Order who believe your parents were traitors themselves, that they died because they got caught conspiring with the Shadowborn. I've spent years trying to clear their names, but the evidence was sealed away—hidden by someone who didn't want it found."

Evidence. Hidden. Like the journal in his hands.

"You have proof."

"I have questions. Answers are in here." Thornwood extended the leather-bound journal. "Your father's record of the investigation. He entrusted it to me the night before they died, with instructions to give it to you when you were ready."

Drevik took the journal with hands that weren't quite steady. The leather was worn, the pages yellowed with age. His father's handwriting, cramped and precise, filled the first visible page.

If you're reading this, I didn't survive long enough to tell you myself. Trust Marcus Thornwood. He loved your mother enough to let her go, and he loves you enough to tell you the truth.

His father had written that. Had known, somehow, that Drevik would one day hold this book, would need these words.

"How long have you had this?"

"Twenty-one years. Hidden in the one place the traitor wouldn't think to look—my personal collection, disguised as a mundane diary." Thornwood's lips curved in something that wasn't quite a smile. "Your father was paranoid, but brilliantly so. He encoded the sensitive information in a cipher only family would recognize. Your grandmother taught it to him, apparently."

Grandmother. The woman who had raised him after his parents died, who had never spoken of the Order, who had died when he was sixteen, leaving him alone.

"She knew?"

"She was a Veiled One. Retired from active service when she took you in." Thornwood's expression softened fractionally. "She asked me not to contact you until you found your own way to us. She believed you needed to choose this path freely, not be guided to it."

Drevik's throat tightened. So many secrets. So many people making decisions about his life without his knowledge.

"There's something else," Thornwood said, and his tone shifted. The hardness remained, but something else crept in—something that sounded almost like paternal concern. "Something personal."

"I'm listening."

"Lexi."

The name landed like a stone in still water. Drevik's posture shifted, alertness sharpening.

"What about her?"

"She's my daughter." Thornwood's voice carried the particular weight of a father speaking of his child. "Her mother died in service to the Order when Lexi was nine. She watched me bury the woman I loved, and she swore she'd never let herself care about someone who could be taken from her."

And then she met me.

"She's been different with you," Thornwood continued. "More invested than I've seen her in years. She argues your case in council meetings. She volunteers for your training sessions. She nearly killed three Shadowborn single-handedly when you were trapped in the eastern passage last month."

Drevik remembered that night. The creature that had cornered him, the way Lexi had appeared from nowhere, her blade singing through shadows.

"I didn't know she—"

"She doesn't know I'm telling you this." Thornwood stepped closer, his presence suddenly imposing despite his unremarkable frame. "But you need to understand something, Acolyte Markinsoul. My daughter has a habit of loving people who get themselves killed. Don't make her bury another."

The words hung between them, weighted with history and warning and something that might have been plea.

Loving people who get themselves killed.

Lexi. Fierce, guarded Lexi, who had pushed him away as often as she'd pulled him close. Lexi, who looked at him sometimes with terror in her eyes that had nothing to do with external threats.

"I won't let anything happen to her," Drevik said, and meant it with every fiber of his being.

"You can't promise that." Thornwood's voice was rough. "No one can. But you can promise not to throw your life away recklessly. You can promise to survive, to fight smart instead of just hard. You can promise to come back to her."

The journal was heavy in Drevik's hands. His father's words. His parents' investigation. A traitor among the Elders. Lexi, who might actually—

"I need to know something," Drevik said. "If the traitor is an Elder, why haven't they killed you? You've been investigating for twenty-one years."

Thornwood's expression flickered, the first crack in his controlled facade. "I've wondered the same thing. The only explanation that makes sense is that my survival serves their purpose somehow. That leaving me alive is more valuable than eliminating me."

"Or they're confident you'll never find them."

"That too." He moved back to his desk, retrieving a small crystal vial from a drawer. "This is for tomorrow night. A protective talisman, blessed by the last Veiled One before your grandmother. It will shield you from direct magical assault for approximately thirty seconds."

"Thirty seconds doesn't seem like much."

"It's enough to strike or escape. Use it wisely." He pressed the vial into Drevik's palm. "And read your father's journal before the ceremony. There are names in there, names of people your parents trusted. Trust no one who wasn't investigated by them."

Drevik pocketed the vial, the journal tucked under his arm. "Why give me all this now? Why trust me?"

Thornwood studied him for a long moment, those green eyes—Lexi's eyes—unreadable.

"Because you're still here. Despite every test, every attack, every attempt to break you, you're still standing. You've earned the right to know the truth." He paused. "And because you look at my daughter the way your father looked at your mother. Like you'd burn the world down to keep her safe."

Drevik couldn't deny it. Didn't try to.

"If I find the traitor," he said quietly, "what do you want me to do?"

"Survive. Bring me proof. And let me handle the rest." The Elder's shadows flickered, dark and dangerous. "I've waited twenty-one years for justice. I can wait a little longer to deliver it myself."

The dismissal was implicit in his tone. Drevik turned toward the door, mind racing with revelations, but Thornwood's voice stopped him.

"Acolyte."

He looked back.

"Lexi will be at the ceremony tonight. She's been assigned as your escort for the lunar rites." The words were carefully casual. "Whatever happens between now and dawn, make sure she doesn't have to mourn you."

Drevik nodded once and stepped into the corridor.

The walk back to his quarters felt endless, his father's journal pressing against his side like a living thing. The shadows whispered around him, carrying secrets he was only beginning to understand.

His parents had been murdered. A traitor sat among the Elders. Elder Thornwood, Lexi's father, had been testing him, pushing him, measuring his worth against some invisible standard.

And Lexi—

She argues your case in council meetings. She volunteers for your training sessions.

She nearly killed three Shadowborn single-handedly when you were trapped.

My daughter has a habit of loving people who get themselves killed.

The implication settled into his bones, warm and terrifying. Lexi cared about him. Genuinely, deeply, in ways she probably hadn't meant to show.

And tomorrow night, at the full moon ceremony, he would have to face her knowing that her father had just given him permission—no, a command—to survive.

Don't make her bury another.

Drevik reached his quarters and closed the door behind him, leaning against the cool wood. The journal felt heavy in his hands, filled with his father's secrets and his parents' last desperate attempt to protect him from beyond the grave.

He opened to the first page and began to read.

If you're reading this, I didn't survive long enough to tell you myself. But know this: the Order is not what it seems. We are not the only ones who serve the shadows, and the true enemy has been among us all along.

These are the names of those I trust. Everyone else is suspect.

Drevik read into the night, his father's words painting a picture of conspiracy and betrayal that reached higher than he'd ever imagined.

And somewhere in the compound, Lexi Thornwood was preparing for a ceremony that might change everything.

Tomorrow night, he thought, we'll see who survives.

The shadows in his room had deepened to near-black by the time he finished reading. His father's journal contained names, dates, locations—evidence of a conspiracy that spanned decades. The traitor wasn't just working with the Shadowborn; they were feeding the Order's own people to them, sacrificing acolytes and seekers in a twisted bargain for power.

And three names appeared repeatedly, circled in red ink:

Elder Vance. Elder Seirin. Elder Thornwood.

His father hadn't known which one to trust either.

Drevik closed the journal carefully, his mind racing. Elder Thornwood had given him this book, had claimed to be his ally, but his own father had suspected him.

Trust no one who wasn't investigated by your parents.

And yet Thornwood had been investigated. His name appeared in the journal dozens of times, always with question marks, always with notes like motives unclear and actions don't align with suspected goals.

If Thornwood was the traitor, this was an elaborate manipulation. If he wasn't, then the real enemy remained hidden, watching, waiting.

Drevik's door opened without a knock.

He looked up to find Lexi standing in the threshold, her pale green eyes sharp with barely contained emotion. She wore training clothes, her auburn hair pulled back, and she looked like she'd been running.

"We need to talk." Her voice was clipped. "My father just sent word. You're assigned to me for tomorrow's ceremony."

"I know. He told me."

She crossed the room in three quick strides, stopping inches from him. Her shadow flickered with agitation, the darkness around her form restless and unsettled.

"What did he say to you?"

"That he's been testing me. That my parents were murdered. That there's a traitor among the Elders." Drevik met her gaze steadily. "That you've been arguing my case in council meetings."

Lexi's jaw tightened. "He had no right—"

"He had every right. He's your father." Drevik set the journal aside. "He told me not to make you bury anyone else."

Something shifted in her expression. The anger remained, but beneath it—fear. Genuine, raw fear.

"Drevik, tomorrow night is dangerous. The full moon ceremony isn't just ritual. It's when the barriers between realms thin enough for things to cross over. If someone is planning to betray the Order—"

"Then we'll face it together." He stood, closing the distance between them. "I won't let anything happen to you."

"That's not—" She stopped, breath catching. "You can't promise that."

"Your father said the same thing."

"Then listen to him." Her voice cracked. "I've buried too many people, Drevik. My mother. Friends. People I thought would always be there. I can't—" She turned away, shoulders rigid. "I can't do it again."

Drevik reached for her, his hand settling on her arm. "Lexi."

She didn't pull away.

"I'm not going anywhere," he said quietly. "Whatever happens tomorrow, whatever your father's tests revealed, whatever my parents discovered—I'm fighting to stay. For the Order. For the truth." He paused. "For you."

Lexi turned back to him, and her eyes glistened with tears she refused to let fall.

"You don't know what you're promising."

"Then tell me."

She stared at him for a long moment, the air between them thick with tension. Then she moved, closing the remaining distance until they were nearly touching.

"Tomorrow night," she whispered, "after the ceremony, if we both survive... I'm not holding back anymore."

Drevik's pulse spiked. "Lexi—"

"I've been afraid. Afraid of losing you like I've lost everyone else." Her hands came up to rest against his chest. "But your meeting with my father made something clear. If we're walking into danger, if tomorrow might be the end—I don't want any more regrets."

He covered her hands with his own. "We're going to survive."

"Promise me."

"I promise."

Lexi rose on her toes, brushing her lips against his in a kiss that was barely a whisper—soft, tentative, a vow waiting to be sealed. Then she pulled back, her eyes fierce despite the moisture clinging to her lashes.

"Read your father's journal. Prepare for tomorrow. And when the moon rises, stay close to me."

She was gone before he could respond, the door closing softly behind her.

Drevik stood in the darkness, his lips still burning from her touch, his father's secrets heavy in his hands.

Tomorrow night, everything would change.

He could only hope they'd both survive to see the dawn.
