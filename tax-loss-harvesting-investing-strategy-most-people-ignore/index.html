Tax-Loss Harvesting in 2026: The Investing Strategy Most People Ignore

Tax-Loss Harvesting in 2026: The Investing Strategy Most People Ignore

You're probably paying more taxes on your investments than you need to. Not because you're doing something wrong, but because you're not doing something that almost nobody talks about at dinner parties.

Tax-loss harvesting sounds like accounting jargon, and it kind of is. The idea is simple though: sell investments that are down, lock in the loss for tax purposes, then buy something similar (but not identical) to stay invested. The IRS lets you use those losses to offset gains elsewhere in your portfolio. You haven't really changed your investment strategy. You've just given yourself a discount on your tax bill.

How it actually works

Say you bought $10,000 of an S&P 500 index fund in January, and by July it's worth $8,500. You also own some Apple stock that's up 30%. If you sell the index fund at a $1,500 loss and buy a total market fund instead, two things happen:

  • You realize a $1,500 capital loss, which offsets $1,500 of gains from the Apple stock (or any other gains you've taken this year).
  • You're still invested in the broad market, just through a different fund that tracks a slightly different index.

    • If your losses exceed your gains in a given year, you can deduct up to $3,000 against ordinary income. Anything left over carries forward indefinitely.

    One catch: the wash-sale rule. You can't sell a stock at a loss and buy the same stock (or something "substantially identical") within 30 days before or after the sale. Buy an S&P fund, sell it at a loss, then buy the same S&P fund two weeks later? The IRS disallows the loss.

    This is why you swap into a similar-but-not-identical fund. An S&P 500 fund and a total stock market fund track different indexes, so they pass the wash-sale test. Fidelity offers commission-free trading on both, which makes the swap cheap. Open a Fidelity account here if you don't have one.

    When it's worth the effort

    Not every dip is worth harvesting. Transaction costs used to kill this strategy for small portfolios, but most major brokerages eliminated commissions years ago. The real question is whether the tax savings justify the paperwork and the risk of being out of the market for even a day.

    For portfolios under $25,000, the math is usually too small to bother. A $2,000 loss harvested at a 22% marginal rate saves you $440 in taxes. Not nothing, but also not worth obsessive monitoring.

    For portfolios over $100,000, the savings become real. A $10,000 loss harvested in a year where you also realized $10,000 in gains eliminates the capital gains tax entirely. That's $1,500 to $2,000 saved, depending on your bracket.

    The best time to harvest is during market corrections, which happen more often than people think. Between 1950 and 2025, the S&P 500 experienced a decline of 5% or more in roughly 60% of calendar years. You get opportunities.

    Where people screw it up

    The biggest mistake is ignoring the wash-sale rule. If you have automatic investing turned on (and you probably should), selling at a loss and then having your recurring buy purchase the same fund within 30 days triggers a wash sale. Turn off automatic investments in the fund you're selling before you harvest.

    Another one: trying to harvest losses inside IRAs and 401(k)s. The IRS doesn't let you claim losses on investments inside tax-advantaged accounts. Only taxable brokerage accounts qualify. This catches people off guard every year.

    Then there's allocation drift. If you harvest a small-cap fund and swap into a large-cap fund, you've changed your risk profile. Swap into something that plays the same role in your portfolio, different product, same asset class.

    And don't forget short-term vs. long-term rates. Short-term capital gains (investments held less than a year) are taxed at your ordinary income rate, which can be 32% or more. Long-term gains max out at 20%. Harvesting losses against short-term gains saves you more money per dollar of loss, so prioritize those first.

    Automation

    Robo-advisors like Wealthfront do this automatically. They monitor your portfolio daily and harvest losses whenever the math works. Wealthfront's automated tax-loss harvesting is included in their 0.25% annual fee, and they claim it can add roughly 1-2% to after-tax returns annually during volatile years.

    Whether that's worth the management fee depends on your portfolio size and whether you'd do this yourself. For a $200,000 portfolio, 0.25% is $500 per year. If tax-loss harvesting saves you $2,000 in taxes, you're ahead. For a $30,000 portfolio, the fee is $75 and the tax savings might be $100. Less compelling.

    Tax-loss harvesting isn't exciting. It won't make you a better investor or help you pick winning stocks. What it does is reduce the tax drag on returns you're already earning, and over 20 or 30 years that drag is enormous. A 1% annual tax savings compounded over 30 years can mean tens of thousands of dollars more in your account. Not because you earned higher returns, but because you kept more of what you earned.

    Check your portfolio for losses. Swap into similar funds. Turn off auto-invest on the position you're selling. Do it before December 31.
