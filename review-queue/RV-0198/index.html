The stone of the Fold does not move, but it breathes. It is a slow, heavy respiration of granite and void-glass, a rhythm set by the weight of the dimensions pressing against the outside. I sat by the edge of the foundation seam, where the masonry of the Great Hall meets the raw, unformed edge of the pocket-dimension.

To most, the seam is invisible. They see a floor of polished obsidian and a wall of seamless basalt, and they believe the room is closed. They walk over it with the careless grace of those who trust in the permanence of things. But I am Corin, and I know the math of the architecture. I know that a room is only as whole as its weakest stitch. I know that silence is often just the sound of a structural failure waiting for its moment to scream.

I ran a finger along the hairline fracture in the floor. It was a cold line, a thin strip of nothingness that tasted of ozone and old ash. Here, the Fold was thin. The Nought were pressing. I could feel the vibration in my marrow—a low, dissonant hum that did not belong to our music. It was the sound of a void that had forgotten how to be empty, a predatory hunger that lived in the spaces between atoms.

I reached for my chisel. The steel was heavy, a familiar weight in my palm, the cold metal grounding me against the vertigo of the edge. To mend a seam, one must first understand the decay. I began to scrape at the edge of the crack, clearing away the microscopic flakes of void-glass that had drifted from the Threshold. Each flake was a tiny, spent star, a piece of light that had been used up and discarded. They glittered like dying embers in the dim light of my brazier, beautiful and utterly hollow.

The work was precise. It was the work of counting the grains of sand in an hourglass, of measuring the pulse of a dying god. If the seam widened by even a fraction of a millimeter, the pressure of the Unmaking Choir would find a foothold. They would not break the door; they would simply unmake the hinge. They would dissolve the very concept of a barrier until the Hall was no longer a sanctuary, but merely another mouth for the void to swallow.

A shadow fell across my workspace. It did not drift in like a cloud; it arrived with the sudden, heavy presence of a mountain.

I did not look up. I knew the cadence of his step. Drevik did not walk so much as he displaced the air around him. The room grew smaller when he entered, not because the walls moved, and not because he was a giant, but because his attention demanded the space. His very existence was an imposition.

"The seam is deepening, Corin."

His voice was low, a strike of flint against steel. He stood at the edge of my light, his tall crystal mitress catching the dim, flickering glow of the braziers. The void crystals within the mitre drifted in slow, hypnotic orbits, casting fractured, violet light across the silver-white of his hair. He wore the deep-purple vestments of the Ascendant, fully fastened, the gold embroidery gleaming like captured lightning against the dark silk.

I kept my eyes on the stone, my chisel pausing mid-stroke. "It is the Nought. They are singing in the dark again. The vibration is pulling at the mortar."

"The Nought do not sing," Drevik said. He moved closer, his presence pressing against my back like a physical heat. I could hear the soft, rhythmic rustle of his heavy silk, the authoritative weight of his staff tapping the obsidian floor. "They only erase. If there is a song, it is a mimicry. A lure to draw the ear toward the edge."

I looked up then, wiping a smudge of grey dust from my forehead with the back of a trembling hand. He was looking down at the crack, his kitsune mask reflecting nothing but the emptiness of the floor. The white ceramic was stark, an accusing brightness against the dark of his robes. Behind the gold-accented slits of the mask, his eyes—those vast, star-pupiled voids—were fixed on the fracture. He looked at the seam not as a mason looks at a wall, but as a judge looks at a crime.

"I can bridge it," I said, trying to find the steadiness of the stone in my own voice. "With enough silver-solder and a steady hand. But I need more time. The masonry needs to set, and the vibrations are too high to allow for a clean pour."

Drevik leaned heavily on his staff. The glowing orb at the tip pulsed once, a slow, rhythmic heartbeat of light that illuminated the dust motes dancing in the air. "Time is the one thing we cannot borrow from the hunger of the abyss, Corin. Not without a cost that we are unprepared to pay."

"The Bureau says the containment protocols are holding," I countered, though the words felt hollow even as they left my lips.

"The Bureau sees only what is permitted to be seen," Drevik replied, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial murmur. He stepped into the pool of light, his shadow stretching long and distorted across the floor. "They watch the edges of the maps, not the fraying of the cloth. They see the tremor in the Threshold and call it a seasonal shift. They see the loss of the outer outposts and call it an evacuation. They are blinded by the bureaucracy of preservation."

"And you?" I asked, setting the chisel down. "What do you see, Drevik?"

He did not answer immediately. He stood motionless, a monument of silk and crystal. The silence stretched between us, thick with the scent of ozone and the metallic tang of the silver-solder.

"I see a foundation that is being undermined by more than just the Nought," he said softly. He moved his hand toward the crack, not touching it, but hovering his palm just inches above the void. The heat from his skin seemed to react with the cold of the seam, creating a tiny, swirling mist. "I see a structure that is being hollowed out from within. The pressure from the outside is immense, yes. But the weight from the inside... that is what will cause the collapse."

"Are you saying there is a breach in our loyalty?" I whispered, the fear finally finding its way into my throat.

Drevik turned his head slightly, the mask tilting toward me. I could not see his eyes, only the dark, empty hollows where they should be. "I am saying that when the mountain falls, it is rarely because the wind was too strong. It is because the earth beneath it lost its resolve."

He straightened, his stature reclaiming the room. "The work must be finished by dawn, Corin. Do not let your focus drift to the shadows. Focus on the stone. Focus on the seam."

"I am focused on the truth," I said, my voice hardening.

"The truth is a luxury for those who do not have to hold the ceiling up," he snapped. He turned, the heavy hem of his robes sweeping the dust into a miniature storm. He walked toward the Great Hall, toward the hearth where the light of the others would be gathering. He left behind a trail of silence, a vacuum where his presence had been, leaving me alone with the cold and the creeping dark.

I turned back to the fracture, my heart hammering a frantic rhythm against my ribs. I had to work faster. I had to mend the wound before it became a mouth.

I picked up my tweezers, intending to clear a stubborn cluster of void-glass shards, but as the fine metal tips entered the crevice, I felt a resistance that was not stone. It was not the brittle snap of glass or the gritty texture of mortar. It was something smooth, something organic.

I dug deeper, my breath hitching in my chest. I reached into the very marrow of the seam, into the space where the masonry met the nothingness. My tweezers snagged on something.

I pulled.

A small, heavy object emerged from the darkness of the crack. I held it up to the light of my brazier, my hands shaking so violently that the light danced errly across the floor.

It was a fragment of a seal—a thick, rectangular piece of official red wax, stamped with the unmistakable, embossed crest of the Aegis Bureau. The eye of the watcher, surrounded by the laurel of the Fold.

I stared at it, the world around me suddenly tilting. My breath did not hitch; I could not afford to lose my rhythm. I simply held the red wax in my hand, feeling its heat. It was not cold, as the stone was. It was warm. It was fresh. The wax had not yet been subjected to the freezing temperatures of the void-drift. It had been placed here recently.

The realization hit me with the force of a physical blow. The seam had not just been widening; it had been opened. Someone had been here, in the dead of night, working not to mend the breach, but to widen it. Someone had used the Bureau's own authority to peel back the edge of our reality.

The betrayal was not a sudden strike to the walls; it was a slow, systemic rot in the foundation. It was a finger placed directly into the wound, ensuring the infection would spread.

I looked toward the Great Hall, where the distant, flickering light of the communal hearth suggested the others were gathered, talking, laughing, oblivious to the fact that the floor beneath their feet was being unstitched. I thought of Drevik, standing in the dark just moments ago, speaking of negotiations and the failure of the Bureau. I thought of the way he had looked at the crack—not as a mason, but as a judge.

I did not know who had placed the seal. I did not know if it was a scout from the Bureau, a spy from the Nought, or a high-ranking official from within our own inner circle. But I knew the math of it. A seam left open is a door. And a door, once unlatched, cannot be closed by stone and silver alone.

I pressed the wax into my palm, squeezing it until the heat of my skin melted the edges, crushing the Bureau’s eye into a shapeless, red smear against my skin. It felt like blood.

I tucked the remaining broken fragment of the seal into my belt, hidden deep beneath the grit and the dust of my work.

The work would continue. The stone would be mended. I would patch the hole and smooth the surface until the floor looked whole once more. But the architecture was already compromised. The math was broken.

In the distance, I heard the low, rhythmic tolling of the bell from the outer watch, a heavy, mournful sound that vibrated through the very floor I knelt upon. The Nought were coming. The signal had been given.

And somewhere, in the heart of the Fold, the rot had already come home.
