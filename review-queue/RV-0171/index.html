Chapter 5: Tobin

The hearth in the Fold did not require tending, but I tended it anyway. It was the way of things. I moved the heavy iron poker through the embers, turning the white ash to reveal the orange pulse beneath. The heat was a steady weight, a companion in the quiet of the afternoon. In the Fold, the light always felt like late autumn—golden, heavy, and suspended.

I thought of the liturgy I had written for the coming feast. I thought of the way the silver chalices would catch the candlelight. My hands moved with the precision of a seamstress working a fine silk. There was a rhythm to the upkeep of the house, a way to keep the edges of the world from fraying.

A chime sounded at the Threshold.

I did not start. To start was to admit that the peace was fragile. I simply set the poker in its cradle and wiped my hands on my apron. Only Drevik held the key to the outside, and only he could open the seam.

I walked toward the hall. The floorboards were cool beneath my feet. As I approached the Great Door, I saw him.

Tobin stood in the threshold, his silhouette breaking the perfect geometry of the doorway. He did not look like a man who had travelled through the void. He looked like a man who had just stepped off a carriage in a sunlit square. His coat was of fine, heavy wool, the color of a bruised plum, and his eyes held a brightness that the Fold rarely permitted.

"Sera," he said.

He smiled, and the room seemed to take a breath. It was the old warmth—the kind that belonged to the years before the shrine-town fell, before the ash had covered the altars.

"Tobin," I said. I stepped forward and took his hands. They were warm, solid, and smelled of rain and distant roads. "You are early. The summons was not yet sent."

"The roads were kind," he said. He let go of my hands to pull me into a brief, fierce embrace. He smelled of the world outside—of dust and wind and the sharp, salt scent of the living. "And the news was too heavy to wait for a messenger."

"Is it the Aegis?" I asked. I did not add Please, let it not be the Aegis. To ask was to invite the fear.

Tobin pulled back. He did not let the smile vanish, but it retreated, moving from his lips to the corners of his eyes. He looked around the hall, his gaze lingering on the high, vaulted ceiling, the shadows that pooled in the corners like spilled ink.

"The Bureau is moving," he said. His voice was plain, stripped of its earlier lightness. "They are not just watching the borders anymore, Sera. They are measuring the seams. They are looking for the way the light bends around the Threshold."

I felt a small, cold needle prick my skin. I reached for the edge of the table, finding the grain of the oak. I needed the wood to be real. I needed the grain to be a fact.

"They cannot find it," I said. "Drevik has anchored it. The weight of the house is too great for their instruments."

"They have new instruments," Tobin said. He walked deeper into the hall, his boots clicking with a sharp, rhythmic authority. "They have lawyers who trade in dimensions and mages who weigh souls like grain in a market. They are looking for the debt, Sera. They are looking for the cost of the keeping."

I watched him. He was my brother, and I knew the architecture of his heart. He was a man of many pieces, but the piece that loved me was always the most stable. Yet, even as he spoke of the Bureau, he reached out to touch a small, carved figurine on the side table. He handled it with a reverence that belonged to a priest, not a traveller.

"You have grown a kingdom here," he said, his voice softening. "A fortress of shadows and gold. It is beautiful. It is terrifying."

"It is a home," I corrected.

"It is a target," he said.

He sat in the chair by the hearth, the one Drevik had placed there specifically for guests of importance. He sat with a relaxed confidence that masked the tension in his shoulders. He began to talk then, of the trade routes, of the shifting alliances in the lower cities, of the way the wind had begun to carry the scent of iron and ozone.

He told me stories of the people we had known, of faces lost to the dust of the old world. For a moment, the Fold felt smaller, more intimate, as if the void outside had been pushed back by the sheer force of his memory. We were two children again, sitting by a fire that could not be extinguished.

But beneath the stories, the subtext was a low, thrumming note. Every mention of a new law, every report of a new garrison, was a strike against the foundation. The Bureau was not just looking; they were hunting.

I watched the way the light caught the silver in his hair. He was older, the lines around his eyes deeper, but he remained the anchor I had relied upon when the world first began to break.

"Does he know?" I asked. I did not need to say his name. In this house, the air itself was his name.

Tobin looked toward the heavy doors of the inner sanctum, where the shadows were deepest. "He knows everything, Sera. He is the archive. He does not need a messenger to tell him what the wind is whispering."

Tobin leaned forward, his face entering the pool of light from the hearth. The playfulness was gone now. The mask of the charming traveller had slipped, leaving only the man who had seen too much.

"But even an archive can be burned," Tobin said. "And even a house can be unmade if the foundation is built on a lie."

I felt the silence of the room grow heavy. It was not the peaceful silence of the Fold, but a silence filled with the weight of things unsaid. A silence that waited for a blow.

"Why are you really here, Tobin?" I asked. My voice was steady. I had learned to speak in the plainest terms when the stakes were high.

He looked at me for a long time. He did not answer immediately. He reached into his coat and pulled out a small, folded piece of vellum. It was not the fine, heavy paper used for the liturgy. It was cheap, thin, and stained with something dark.

"The Bureau has found a way to track the resonance," he said. He placed the paper on the table between us. "They aren't looking for the door. They are looking for the man who holds it."

I stared at the vellum. It sat on the oak like a wound.

"They are asking about the Fold," Tobin said. His voice dropped, becoming a mere vibration in the air. "They are asking how a man can hold a piece of the void without paying the toll. They are asking who is helping him hide the ledger."

I reached out, my fingers hovering just above the paper. I did/not touch it. To touch the news was to make it part of the house.

"And what did you tell them?" I asked.

Tobin stood up. The movement was sudden, breaking the spell of the hearth. He moved back toward the threshold, toward the way he had come. The warmth was leaving the room with him.

"I told them nothing," he said. "I told them I knew nothing of such a place."

He paused in the doorway, his silhouette once more a break in the perfect geometry. He looked back at me, and for a second, I saw the fear he had been hiding behind the charm—a fear that was not for himself, but for the hearth we had built.

"But Sera," he said. "They are asking about the rooms. They are asking how the house grows. They are asking about the others."

He looked toward the inner sanctum, toward the rooms where the other women moved in the shadows of the Fold.

"They are asking about the cost of the others," he whispered.

He stepped out into the void. The Great Door swung shut with a heavy, definitive thud. The latch clicked into place.

I sat alone by the fire. The embers were dying, turning from orange to a dull, ashen grey. I looked at the small, stained piece of vellum on the table. It was a single, dark thread in the tapestry of our lives, but I could feel it pulling. I could feel the seam of the world beginning to strain.

I picked up the poker. I turned the ash. I searched for the pulse, but the fire was growing cold, and the dark behind the door stayed dark.
