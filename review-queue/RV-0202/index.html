The Bureau did not use magic to secure its archives. It used tolerances. It used the precise, unyielding friction of steel against steel, and the weight of brass tumblers that knew exactly when a key was false.

Mira lived in the mathematics of the click. She knew the soul of a lock by the way the pins resisted—the slight, stubborn drag of a well-oiled cylinder, or the gritty protest of a mechanism choked by dust. To her, a door was not a boundary; it was a problem of measurement.

Tonight, the problems were unravelling.

She stood in the center of the Bureau’s inner sanctum, the room where the most sensitive registries of the Aegis Bureau were kept. The air smelled of cold iron and the sharp, ozone tang of the Fold’s proximity. Her fingers, calloused by years of picking and setting, moved over the heavy ring of keys at her hip. They felt wrong. The weight was uneven, as if the metal itself had lost its center.

The first failure had been small. A deadbolt in the secondary storage wing had slid back on its own, leaving the door ajar like a gasping mouth. Mira had assumed it was a matter of settling foundations—the Fold’s expansion often strained the architecture of the physical. She had reset the bolt, checked the strike plate, and gone back to her ledgers.

The second failure had been a scream.

A heavy iron grate, designed to bar the passage to the deep vaults, had simply ceased to exist as a barrier. When Mira had reached it, the gate stood upright, its mechanism intact, its hinges unmarred, yet it had swung open at the mere touch of her hand. There was no broken tooth, no sheared pin. The lock had not been defeated; it had been undone.

Now, she stood before the Master Archive. This was the heart of the Bureau’s knowledge, a door of void-glass and reinforced adamant that required three distinct, simultaneous alignments to open. It was a door that Mira had personally calibrated. It was a door that should have been impossible to breach without the knowledge of the sequence.

She reached for the handles. They were cold—not the chill of winter, but the hollow, sucking cold of the space between worlds.

The door stood open.

The light from the hallway spilled into the vault, illuminating the empty pedestals. The registries were gone. The heavy, vellum-bound histories of the Aegis, the records of the first contacts with the Sundered, the very census of the Bureau’s strength—all of it had vanished. The room was not looted; it was hollow. It was a husk.

Mira did not scream. She did not run. She did the only thing a woman of her craft knew how to do. She looked for the seam.

She stepped into the vault, her boots clicking on the stone. She traced the edge of the empty pedestals. She searched for the scratch of a tool, the residue of a spell, the microscopic shavings of a forced pick. There was nothing. The room was as clean as a fresh-minted coin.

The truth was a heavy thing, and it settled in her gut like lead. A lock does not fail because of age. A lock fails because the mechanism has been told to forget its purpose.

A shadow moved in the doorway.

Mira did not turn. She knew the rhythm of that step. It was the slow, deliberate pace of a man who carried the weight of a world and found it light. Drevik.

He did not enter the room so much as the room accepted him. The darkness of the vault seemed to retreat, not in fear, but in deference. He did not speak, but his presence filled the space, a vastness that pressed against the walls of the Bureau. He was dressed in his full vestments, the deep purple of a bruised sky, the gold embroidery catching what little light remained. The tall crystal mitre sat upon his head, the void crystals within it drifting in their slow, hypnotic orbit.

He stopped a pace behind her. She could hear the soft, rhythmic slide of his staff against the floor.

"The tolerances are off," Mira said. Her voice was flat, a ledger entry.

"The seams are widening," Drevance replied.

His voice was a low resonance, the sound of a bell struck deep underground. It was a kind voice, but it held the gravity of the Threshold.

"I checked the pins," Mira said, her hands trembling despite her will. She gripped the edge of a stone pedestal. "I checked the strike plates. I checked the alignment. The mechanisms are perfect. The metal is true."

"And yet," he said.

He moved around her. He did not touch her, but she felt the heat of him, the anchored warmth that kept her from drifting into the void. He looked at the empty pedestals. He looked at the hollowed room. In the light of his drifting crystals, his white-ceramic mask gleamed, the gold accents bright and mocking.

"The archives were the foundation of the Bureau's claim," Mira said. She finally turned to face him. "Without the records, we are just men with swords. We have no history to defend. We have no debt to prove."

Drevik leaned on his staff. His eyes—those vast, dark voids with their star-bright pupils—surveyed the emptiness. He looked like a man observing a minor inconvenience, a smudge on a clean window.

"The records were a lie," he said. "They were a way to pretend that what is written can hold what is happening."

"That is a scholar's sentiment, not a locksmith's," Mira snapped. The anger was a sudden, sharp thing, a jagged edge in her precision. "Records are the only thing that stay shut. They are the only things we can trust to remain where we put them."

"Nothing stays where it is put, Mira. Not even the stars. They are only spent light, moving toward their own end."

He stepped closer. The scent of him—ash, incense, and the cold, clean scent of the void—overwhelmed the smell of the iron. He reached out, his gloved hand hovering near her cheek, not quite touching, a promise of warmth that remained just out of reach.

"The Unmaking Choir is not coming for the books," he said softly. "They are coming for the truth behind them."

Mira looked at the empty pedestals and felt the floor beneath her feet lose its solidity. The Bureau was a fortress of precision, a monument to the measurable. But the measurements were failing. The math was breaking.

"Who took them?" she asked. The question was a plea.

Drevik did not answer. He looked toward the doorway, toward the dark corridor that led to the rest of the Fold, where the other women waited. His expression was unreadable behind the mask, but his silence was a heavy, suffocated thing.

"The locks are not the problem, Mira," he said.

He turned and began to walk away, his silhouette tall and imposing against the encroaching dark.

"The problem is the key."

Mira stood alone in the hollowed vault. She looked at her hands—the hands that knew every tumbler, every spring, every secret of the metal. She felt a sudden, frantic urge to find a lock, to force a bolt, to prove that something, somewhere, could still be held.

But as she listened to the receding rhythm of his staff, she realized the terrifying truth of her own craft.

A lock only works if the door is meant to stay closed. And she realized, with a coldness that no fire could warm, that the doors were no longer being held.

She walked toward the exit, her footsteps echoing in the emptiness. As she passed the threshold of the vault, she saw a flicker of movement in the shadows of the hallway.

A figure stood there, half-shrouded by the gloom. It was Vesper. Her hand was resting on the hilt of her blade, her posture rigid, her eyes fixed on the floor. She did not look up. She did not acknowledge Mira's presence.

But as Mira passed, she saw it. Vesper's fingers were stained with something dark and viscous. It looked like oil, or perhaps old ink. But as the light from a passing mote of void-dust hit it, Mira recognized the shimmer.

It was the residue of the void-glass. The same substance that had formed the faces of the stolen archives.

Mira stopped. The air in the hallway turned brittle.

"Vesper," Mira said.

The swordsman did not move. Her silence was a wall, thick and impenetrable.

"The archives are gone," Mira said, her voice a low, dangerous rasp. "The locks failed. I checked the pins. There was no force. There was no break."

Vesper's hand tightened on her sword. The leather of her glove creaked in the stillness.

"The Bureau relies too much on what can be measured," Vesper said. Her voice was a dry whisper, stripped of its usual edge. "Some things are meant to be lost."

"Not the history," Mira said. "Not the foundation."

Vesper finally looked up. Her eyes were not the eyes of a soldier defending a post. They were hollow, reflecting the same emptiness that now inhabited the Bureau’s heart.

"The foundation is rot, Mira," Vesper said. "And the rot has finally reached the surface."

She stepped back into the shadows, and for a moment, Mira thought she saw the darkness move to swallow her whole. The hallway stretched, the distance between them growing with an impossible, elastic speed.

Mira stood in the corridor, the weight of her keys pulling at her hip, a heavy, useless burden. The Bureau was breached. The archives were dust. And the enemy was not at the gates.

The enemy was sitting at the table.
