Drevik stood in the pantry doorway with frost on his boots and did not come in.

Mira had the lock in pieces on the oilcloth — five pins, two drivers, the ward — laid out in the order they went home in. She did not look up. A lock mid-thought deserved the courtesy of being finished.

The kitchen was the same size it had always been. It never was, with him in a doorway.

The Bureau had opened this lock once, in the spring. Not with her. With a casting of her ring — Bureau steel, filed to her tolerances by a journeyman who had only ever read her forms. She had certified every key she ever cut on Form 9-C, and signed the line that said the tolerances were accurate and were hers. The Bureau kept the forms. The forms kept her.

So she was giving the house new tumblers, one door at a time, cut to numbers that lived in her hands and nowhere else. The Bureau's writs had gone to ash in the winter's accounting. The copy of her ring had not. It sat in an evidence drawer in a city she had burned off her paperwork, opening nothing that mattered. She kept a private list of the doors it still opened. The list was short. Her work was to make it shorter.

In her apron pocket was Form 12-B, Termination of Attachment, with the heading filled in and the date blank. It had ridden there a month. The paper had gone soft at the fold from handling, which was a thing paper did when you carried it and did not file it.

"You file by count," he said from the doorway. "No notes."

"Notes get copied."

"I know," he said.

That was all he said for the better part of an hour. He leaned on the doorframe with his arms folded, wearing the smirk he did his listening in, and watched the work the way no inspector ever had: all the way through, no defects, no stamp at the end of it. The garden light came through the pantry window, late in the afternoon the way it was always late, and gave the pins their shadows.

She drove the last pin home, seated the plug, oiled the ward with a dropper. "Three left."

"Two," he said. "The front door last."

"The front door doesn't take a key."

"It will, or you'll tell me why it can't. Tonight, after supper."

She stood and wiped her hands on the cloth, and before she could stop herself she had the old ring out on her palm. Seven keys of Bureau steel. The third one she had cut for a dormitory room the Bureau had since torn down.

"You should hold this," she said. "The castings came off it. Everything on it is a liability that knows my hands."

"Put the new key on the old ring."

"Bureau steel."

"Every tooth on it is your hand. I don't ask a warden for clean keys. I ask for known ones." He closed her fingers over it with both of his and let go. "Keep the third."

"It opens nothing."

"Keep it."

The front door of the Fold stood where the kitchen ended, and everyone in the house said its name the way you said weather: the front door, the rain. It had never in her time taken anything but his hand.

Sera was there before them, censer unlit in her hands. "I wrote you forty minutes of liturgy," she said. "Four hymns and a psalm of doors."

"Sing it at table," Drevik said. "Tonight belongs to the trade."

Sera set the censer on the shelf and stayed to watch.

Mira knelt and read the door the way she read any door: seam, stile, iron, grain. Where no keyway had ever been, a keyway was. She slid a feeler into it. The ward behind it was cut — exact, patient, waiting for a blank she had not filed yet. She put her thumb over it and the wood was warm.

She had read Wren's notes on the house, the rooms it grew and the shelves it left the way offerings are left, and had filed all of it under sentiment. She took it out from under sentiment and set it down in fact, and it lay there in fact, and stayed.

"The cost," Drevik said. "I say costs out loud. Say this one back to me. Whoever holds this key is the house's first door and the world's first target. Every clever hand in the world learns your name. Your ring lived in their drawer. You know what their keeping means."

"Whoever holds it is what they come for first," she said.

"The rest is return, not cost. I don't say returns out loud." He set his hand flat against the door. "One door and one keeper is a strongbox. Two keepers is a house."

She filed the blank by count — four cuts, the deepest third from the bow, brass telling her the truth one stroke at a time. The key turned on the second try. The door had one lazy pin. She would remember it.

"Go out," he said. "Come home."

The landing was cold and held nothing, and the stars sat in their sockets like coins in a box no one had opened. She stood out there alone for the length of one breath, which was the length of a decision she had not made all winter. Then she put her own key in the world's one door and let herself home.

Sera met her on the inside. She set the lit censer in Mira's hands — one coal, a ribbon of smoke, hers to hold for the length of a breath — and took it back. That was the whole of the rite Sera insisted on. It was more than the Bureau had ever let her hold.

She pinned the last two locks before supper. Halla banked the hearth. Wren mended at the window with her humming. Somewhere below, Vesper was at her drills, wood on wood, a rhythm with an argument in it.

He knocked.

She had heard every door in this house from both sides except this one. The knock was a new sound in it. She had a deflection loaded, three words, about the hour. The door was open before she spent it.

He stood in the hall with a lamp and nothing else. No staff. The mitre he carried in one hand.

"Warden," he said.

"Come in," she said. "The lock's mine."

He came in and set the mitre on the shelf the house had grown, where no light would bother it. She turned her key in her own door, from her own side. The Bureau trained her never to do that: you might have to leave fast. She turned it anyway, and the rule had nowhere left to apply.

The mask had two small catches at the temple. She had never once touched them. She knew them anyway; knowing was the trade. He held still, and gave the mask over the way you hand someone a tool you trust them with, and then it was her hands at the stiff collar-hook, and then the long work of the fastenings, which her hands did at the pace of reading.

He asked with his hands, once, plainly. She answered the way she answered anything she meant: once, and in full.

After that it was slow, and then not slow, and they minded the fit and not the clock. His weight was a fact she leaned into. The lamp. The wool. The oil and brass smell of her own hands in his hair. When it was good she said so out loud. He said her name back with the want in it, and no form attached.

Down the corridor, one bolt at a time, in the order she had pinned them, the new locks turned over. Every door she had re-keyed that week, bolting itself in the dark, a chord rung on brass and oak. Neither of them said anything about it. It was the first thing they had ever agreed on without speaking.

Afterward his breath went slow against her shoulder. She counted it, the way she counted anything she meant to keep.

She woke to bread, and to a house with one more door in it.

The new door stood at the end of the east hall, plain wood, unvarnished, in a wall that had been a wall at supper. She put the third key of the old ring in it — Bureau steel, cut for a dormitory room torn down two winters gone — and it turned the way a key turns in the lock it was cut for.

Inside was a small room. A bench, a vice, a wall of drawers, a lamp. The lamp was lit. It had not been lit by her.

She stood in it a while and said nothing, and the room was warm. The house kept its accounts in rooms. She had finally audited one into being.

She passed Drevik in the hall with his tea. He looked at the door, and at her ring, and said good morning, and asked nothing. It was the second thing they had agreed on without speaking.

On her way to the table she fed Form 12-B to the hearth. Forms burn all at once; they are made for it. Her name went up off the heading, no date attached, and that was the filing done.

At the long table, Halla had the count out: jars against winter, salt against spring. Wren hummed four notes, one for each pin of the new key, and got them in the right order, having heard the lock through a wall, which was Wren. Vesper turned her cup a quarter-turn when Mira sat down, which from Vesper was a salute. Sera passed the salt before the bread. Mira had sat at that table long enough to know what that was in Sera's grammar. She took it, and did not pretend to have missed it.

"How long does a re-pin hold?" Drevik said.

"A year, if the weather's honest. I'll do it again in spring."

Halla wrote her into the spring rotation without asking which spring. Nobody at that table asked which spring.

Drevik held his bread and looked past the table at the garden door, at the late light that never moved, and he was somewhere on the far side of the late light. Sera put her hand flat over his wrist. He came back, and thanked her by name, and ate. Mira wrote no form for that. She kept the count of it instead, the way she kept the count of doors that drag: watch, don't say, oil it later.

Halla came around the table with her apron on and the count done.

"You count what keeps people out," Halla said. "I count what keeps them fed through a winter. I want the counts to speak to one another. One ledger or none."

"Locks are patient," Mira said. "Jars are honest. It could work."

"It will work." Halla set down her pencil. "And there is a door under my stores."

She said it the way she said the price of salt, and went on.

"It came with the first winter. It has never opened for me. I've fed it patience since I started the count, and I am done rationing my curiosity. The house has a Warden now. Bring your ring."

The stores were cold and clean and smelled of salt and lamp oil. The shelves went down into the stone: apples in straw, jars ranked by the year of their making, sealed with wax and string, each one entered in Halla's hand in a ledger older than Mira's residency.

The door stood at the bottom. Iron over oak, plain as a loaf. The lock was a pattern she had never met — not pin, not lever, not warded, not detainer, nothing in her grammar — and the wood around it was older than the grain of everything beside it.

"Who cut it?" Mira said.

"No one I have ever fed," Halla said.

Mira took the ring out anyway. You did not stand in front of a lock with your hands empty.
