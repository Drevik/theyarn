Core idea

Agreements are not a rigid legal code, but a personalized menu of boundaries, desires, and logistical rules designed to manage intimacy and autonomy. In an established relationship, opening up is not a single event but a process of managing three distinct psychological roles: the Adventurous Lover, the Outside Lover, and the One Who Choded None of This. Success depends on moving away from "veto power" as a tool of control and toward a framework of "making space for difference." You must treat the opening process as a learning curve, utilizing tiny, reversible risks to test the structural integrity of your agreements before committing to larger shifts.

Key concepts and terms

  • The Menu Approach: The understanding that agreements can be sexual, relational, logistical, or sentimental, and that they vary wildly between couples. There is no "standard" agreement.
  • Veto Power: The right of a primary partner to block a specific outside connection or type of interaction. While used to manage safety, it is a high-risk tool that can easily morph into emotional abuse or the suppression of a partner's growth.
  • The Adventurous Lover: The partner in the existing relationship who seeks to expand their sexual or romantic landscape.
  • The Outside Lover: The third party entering the dynamic. They are entitled to honesty and autonomy, but they do not owe the existing couple deference or subservience.
  • The One Who Chose None of This: The partner who remains in the relationship out of commitment or love but did not initiate the opening. Their distress is a legitimate signal of a boundary breach, not "sabotage."
  • Designing a Learning Curve: The practice of implementing small, low-stakes changes to an existing relationship structure to observe the emotional fallout before implementing permanent changes.

Techniques worth drilling

  1. The Agreement Audit (Categorization Drill)
  • Take a blank sheet of paper and divide it into four columns: Sexual, Relational, Logistical, and Sentimental.
  • List every current "rule" or "expectation" you have with your partner.
  • Identify which items are "Thou Shalt" (positive requirements) and which are "Thou Shalt Not" (prohibitions).
  • Review the list to find "Mutually Contradictory" points. Do not resolve them yet; simply identify where your desires for autonomy clash with your desires for security.
  1. The Role-Play Simulation (Perspective Shifting)
  • Select a specific proposed change (e.g., "We are going to allow casual dating with no overnight stays").
  • Write a one-page "Agreement Draft" from the perspective of the Adventurous Lover (focus on freedom and excitement).
  • Rewrite the exact same agreement from the perspective of the One Who Choses None of This (focus on safety, fear, and the preservation of the core bond).
  • Rewrite the agreement from the perspective of the Outside Lover (focus on clarity, honesty, and what they can expect from the couple).
  • Compare the three drafts to find the "Gaps"—the places where one person's freedom creates another person's trauma.
  1. The Tiny Risk Implementation
  • Identify a "Micro-Boundary" (e.g., "I will go on one solo date, but I will be home by 11 PM and will text when I arrive").
  • Set a specific expiration date for this trial (e.g., "We will review this after two weeks").
  • Execute the micro-boundary without negotiating during the event.
  • Conduct a "Post-Action Review" with your partner immediately following the event to assess if the "Learning Curve" was too steep or just right.
  1. The Veto Deconstruction
  • List every instance where you have used "veto power" or "blocking" in the past.
  • Label each instance as Safety-Based (protecting health/logistics) or Fear-Based (protecting ego/jealousy).
  • For every Fear-Based veto, draft an alternative agreement that addresses the underlying fear without removing the partner's autonomy.

Practice drill

The Three-Way Diff Drill

This week, choose one specific new "rule" or "opening" you wish to implement. You will perform a written "diff" (difference analysis) to prepare for a negotiation.

  1. Draft A (The Adventurer): Write a 200-word agreement focused on the expansion of pleasure and the removal of restrictions.
  2. Draft B (The Reluctant Partner): Write a 200-word version of that same agreement, focused entirely on how to prevent the existing relationship from dissolving and how to ensure "safety."
  3. Draft C (The Third Party): Write a 200-word version focused on what an outsider needs to know to feel respected and informed.

The Goal: Lay all three papers on a table. Highlight every sentence in Draft A that causes "red flags" in Draft B. Highlight every sentence in Draft C that is missing from Drafts A and B. Your task for the following week is to write a "Version D"—a single, unified agreement that bridges these gaps before you ever go on a first date with an outsider.

Critiques and caveats

  • The Danger of the "Pseudo-Agreement": Beware of agreements that are actually just "polite requests." If an agreement is not enforceable through a shared consequence, it is not an agreement; it is a wish.
  • The Veto Trap: Using veto power to manage jealousy is a temporary fix that creates long-term resentment. If you find yourself vetoing everyone, you are not practicing polyamory; you are practicing isolation.
  • The Speed Limit: The most common cause of relationship failure in opening up is "rushing the learner." If the "One Who Chose None of This" is forced into a high-stakes scenario before they have processed the initial shock, you are likely causing permanent psychological damage.
