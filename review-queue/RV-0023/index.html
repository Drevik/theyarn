eslut-05-abundance

RV-0023 · approved · submitted 2026-10-20

1070 words · book-digest · status approved

Part: Part Two: The Practice of Sluthood | Authors: Janet W. Hardy & Dossie Easton | Generated 2026-10-20 on PC Ollama (sonct988/gemma4-26b-a4b-it-q4km-256k) | DRAFT for review

Core idea

You must distinguish between psychological scarcity and physical constraints. Most relationship anxiety stems from a "starvation economy"—the learned, false belief that love, sex, and attention are finite resources where one person's gain is necessarily your loss. While your time and energy are objectively limited, your capacity for connection is not a zero-sum game. To move toward ethical non-monogamy, you must identify where you are hoarding attention out of fear and learn to separate emotional "scarcity mindsets" from actual "resource limits."

Key concepts and terms

  • Starvation Economies: A psychological state rooted in childhood or past trauma where you believe intimacy and commitment are finite. This mindset triggers defensive behaviors like scorekeeping or jealousy framed as "protecting your share."
  • Scorekeeping: The practice of tracking favors, time spent, and emotional labor to ensure you aren't being "cheated" out of your perceived share of a partner's attention.
  • Resource Defense: A symptom of scarcity where jealousy is rationalized as a necessary defense of a dwindling resource (e.g., "If they spend time with someone else, there is less time left for me").
  • Real-World Limits: The objective, non-negotiable boundaries of your existence, including your calendar, your bank account, your physical stamina, and your sexual bandwidth.
  • Sexual Economies: The assessment of what you actually have to offer (time, affection, sex, stability) and the realistic distribution of those offerings to different partners.
  • The Oneida Experiment: A historical reference to a community that attempted to function on shared resources, used here to illustrate the radical possibility of moving away from individualistic hoarding.

Techniques worth drilling

  1. The "Notice and Test" Method (Letting Go)
  • Step 1: Identification. When you feel a surge of jealousy or the urge to "hoard" a partner, stop and label the feeling: "I am experiencing a scarcity belief."
  • Step 2: The Audit. Ask yourself, "Is there an actual physical constraint here (e.g., they are literally physically unavailable), or am I feeling a lack of importance?"
  • Step 3: The Stress Test. Intentionally allow a period of "unprotected" time. If a partner goes on a date, do not check their location or social media. Observe if your "resource" (your sense of security) actually diminishes or if it remains intact despite their absence.
  1. The Boundary/Belief Separation
  • Step 1: Categorization. Create a two-column list. Column A is "Feelings/Beliefs" (e.g., "I feel less loved when they are with others"). Column B is "Constraints" (e.g., "I only have 4 hours on Tuesday nights for dates").
  • Step 2: Negotiation. For items in Column B, negotiate the schedule. For items in Column A, apply the "Notice and Test" method.
  • Differentiator: Never try to "negotiate" a feeling; only negotiate a calendar.
  1. The Bandwidth Assessment
  • Step 1: Inventory. List your current commitments (work, family, health, existing partners).
  • Step 2: Quantify Energy. Assign a numerical value (1-10) to your current emotional and sexual energy levels.
  • Step 3: The Expansion Check. Before adding a new person or dynamic, determine if you are attempting to stretch a "Real-World Limit" (which leads to burnout) or if you are simply operating under a "Starvation Economy" (which leads to unnecessary restriction).

Practice drill

This week, you will perform a Resource Audit and Budget Conversion. You must identify three specific "there isn't enough" beliefs that are currently driving your anxiety and convert them into a concrete weekly budget of hours and energy.

Part 1: Identify the Scarcity Beliefs List three recurring thoughts that imply a zero-sum game.

  • Example: "If my partner spends Friday night with someone else, our intimacy will vanish."
  • Example: "If I give more energy to Person B, I will have nothing left for Person A."
  • Example: "If I am not the primary focus, I am losing my status in the relationship."

Part 2: Evidence Analysis For each belief, list one piece of evidence for the scarcity (the feeling) and one piece of evidence against it (the reality).

  • Example (For): "I feel a physical pang of anxiety when they leave."
  • Example (Against): "Our connection remains stable even when we are apart for three days."

Part 3: The Weekly Budget Conversion Convert those three emotional fears into a literal, non-negotiable weekly budget of Hours and Energy Units (EU). This forces you to stop treating "love" as a dwindling pie and start treating "time" as a managed calendar.

  • Belief 1 (The Time Budget):
  • Target: [Partner Name/Activity]
  • Fixed Hours: [e.g., 10 hours/week]
  • Energy Allocation: [e.g., 4/10 EU]
  • Belief 2 (The Energy Budget):
  • Target: [Self-Care/Solo Time]
  • Fixed Hours: [e.g., 15 hours/week]
  • Energy Allocation: [e.g., 8/10 EU]
  • Belief 3 (The Social/Community Budget):
  • Target: [Friends/Other Partners]
  • Fixed Hours: [e.g., 5 hours/week]
  • Energy Allocation: [e.g., 3/10 EU]

Goal: By the end of the week, you should be able to point to a moment of jealousy and say: "This is a feeling of scarcity, but my budget for [Partner] is still intact and has not been depleted by [Other Person]."

Critiques and caveats

  • The Burnout Risk: Do not confuse "stretching" a limit with "ignoring" a limit. You can stretch your bandwidth temporarily, but if you treat your "Real-World Limits" as purely psychological, you will hit physical burnout, which will inevitably trigger a return to "Starvation Economy" thinking.
  • The Gaslighting Trap: Be careful not to use "it's just a scarcity belief" to dismiss legitimate grievances. If a partner is actually withdrawing time or affection, that is a change in the "Sexual Economy" and a real-world constraint, not just a feeling you need to "let go" of.
  • The Power Imbalance Warning: In situations of extreme power or resource imbalance (e.g., one person holds all the financial or social capital), "scarcity" may be a rational response to structural inequality rather than a psychological neurosis.

Reading notes

The "Abundance" section serves as the psychological foundation for the more technical logistics discussed later in the book. While other chapters provide the "how-to" for communication and scheduling, this section addresses the "why-we-can't," targeting the deep-seated fears that prevent practitioners from actually implementing new relationship structures.
