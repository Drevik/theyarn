Eleven doors. Mira counted them twice, the way the Bureau had taught her to count exits, and the number held both times, which was more than could be said for the doors.

The Nought had not broken them. Broken she could have fixed in her sleep. It had unmade the fit of them: mortises gone smooth, screws standing upright in wood with no thread left to bite, one hinge gripping a door that had stopped being a door somewhere in the night. She locked all eleven. A locked door holds even with nothing behind it. That was Bureau doctrine. She had left the Bureau, not the doctrine.

The east wing was worse. Four doors there opened on the same absence, a room-shaped nothing that smelled of cold iron and took no light from her lamp. She shut them and turned her key in all four, and the absence stayed shut behind good locks, and that was as much as anyone could do about it this week.

Where the press had cooled along the outer wall, the windows had gone to void-glass, stars set in the black like coins in a struck-shut purse. She tapped one with a knuckle. It rang true. Small mercies, itemized.

The garden kept its late afternoon everywhere but the north bed, which stood in no hour at all. Wren had pinned linen over the bed, sheeted it like a broken window, until it could be mended proper.

Last of all Mira walked the Threshold door. Black wood, no keyhole, warm to the knuckle in the hour before dawn. She had made her peace with it her first winter. Some doors are locks. Some doors are hands. It had held through worse weeks than this one, and it did not need her key, and she did not need to know why that was true.

She did not tally the way the Nought got in. That door had been shut two days since, on the chapel steps, by Drevik, with no weapon and no harm done. He had said the man's own words back to him, all of them, in order, the whole ledger of it, and the man had sat down on the steps and wept, and nobody had died, and the household had come home. She still did not know how he had done it. She had stopped asking how he did things in her first winter.

She chalked the board by the pantry: eleven doors, thirty-four hinges, nine locks, four lamps out, one bed without an hour. Then she went to find the others.

The household rebuilt the Fold by hand. There was no other way. There was no street outside to hire masons from; there was nothing outside, which was the point of the place. The work divided itself without argument, the way it does among five women who have held a wall together for nine days.

Wren took the seam under the east stair, where the Nought had picked the world's pattern loose at the selvedge. She sat cross-legged in the doorway with linen across her knees and hummed while she worked, and the stitch kept time to the hum, and the seam closed behind the needle like water closing over a dropped coin.

Sera took the lamps. She went down the corridors with the censer swinging and relit them oldest rite first, and where the flame took she marked the lintel in ash, the same stroke at every doorway, a signature the Fold had learned to answer.

Vesper took the wall and the timber. The garden grew mending-wood in the way the kitchen never cooled; nobody had ever explained it to Mira's satisfaction, and she had stopped asking the garden questions. Vesper felled and split and notched the joints to Mira's scribe lines, and at dusk she stood on the new courses with her back to the late light and watched the Sundered out past the hedge, the way other people count what is owed them.

"If anything presses tonight," Vesper said, "it can wait until the mortar's dry."

Halla took the table. She had taken it nine days ago, in the worst hour, when the east wing went dark, and she had not put it down since. She fed them on ration weight and fed them hot, and her ration book balanced to the crumb.

Mira took the doors. She planed the swollen ones back to the jamb, cut fresh mortises where the old ones had been unmade, set hinges to a hairline and oiled everything twice, and set the lock last, because a door without a lock is a wall that lies about itself.

He came through the yard before noon with a door leaf on his shoulder and the mask off, and the yard bent around him. Wren's hum steadied to his step. The lamps Sera had just relit leaned their flames toward him. Vesper turned her head on the wall, marked him, and went back to watching out. He racked the leaf against the porch and read the Fold over, slow, the way Mira read a lock before choosing her pick.

He hauled what they cut and cut what she marked and kept out of the joinery.

By mid-afternoon he had driven forty nails, and she had stopped watching after the tenth. Every nail went in to the same depth, head seated flush, forty times, no line of sight, no settling tap. A man following a form he had never been shown. She had seen that exactness before. It had been in the Bureau, in the hands of men who filed other men. She did not want it in his.

Frost stood on the inside of the void-glass where he worked. She chalked it on the board. She had no column for it.

In the second month of the Fold she had hung the garden door a sixteenth low, her error, and he had been in another world that week, and coming home he had said, "Hang the garden door a pin high, the way it liked." It had. He was the only blueprint the Fold had. Today he stood at her bench and recited the wear of every hinge she had ever replaced, the depth of the pantry lintel, which locks she had oiled her first winter and which her second, and she fitted the east doors to his memory, and the old wear took the new iron like it had missed it.

He set a tin down beside her. "Your old pins. Third month, the first re-hang. You threw them out. I kept them. New iron won't take old wear. These will."

She took the tin. She did not say thank you. She said, "I inventory my own scrap," which was a lie, and he smiled like she had said the other thing, and went back to the stack.

At noon Sera crossed the yard, took the hammer out of his hand, and put the bread end in it. He ate standing, in the sun. When he reached for the auger she put the broom in that hand instead. He swept the shavings. The strokes went short and crooked, human, and some got away from him, and by suppertime the frost had let go of the glass. Sera brought Mira tea at the third hour and sat with her on the boards while she drank it and said nothing about the frost, and that was the correct amount.

His mitre hung on the peg by the pantry door with the staff leaned under it, like a coat. The void crystals in it drifted slower this week, silt in still water. She did not chalk that either. Some tolerances you don't write down.

Supper was barley and salt pork and the end of the pickled things, portioned by a woman who could balance a ration book in her sleep and eat standing afterward. The long table had come through with one scar, a shallow scorch along the west end from the ninth night. After the dishes Halla planed the burn smooth, oiled the grain, and let the color stand.

"A scar oiled is a record," she said, to the table more than to them. "I keep records."

Mira filed the words the way she filed a worn key: to copy later, when nobody was watching. She cut the duplicate that night at the bench in the kitchen doorway, by hearth light, testing the cut against the pantry lock itself, because the lock was the only witness she trusted.

At breakfast she put the key on the boards beside Halla's bowl.

"You picked my pantry latch twice this week," she said. "With a hairpin. You left burrs in the brass. Take the key before you cost me a re-bushing."

Halla looked at the key and did not touch it. "I never picked your latch."

"Wednesday, past midnight. And Friday, for the salt. The burrs say so."

"Wednesday the stores were mine to fetch and yours to guard," Halla said, "and I was not going to wake a tired woman to do it. Friday I wanted the salt and did not feel like arguing with the guard."

"The guard would have handed you the key."

"The guard was asleep on the bench with a file in her fist," Halla said. "I moved the hairpin, not her."

"Then take the duplicate. It isn't a gift. Losing mine twice in one season is a fire hazard, and I am not cutting a third."

Halla took it. She turned it over once and read the file marks the way anyone else reads a signature. "You cut this last night."

"Wednesday."

"Wednesday," Halla said, "was the day the east wing stopped screaming."

Drevik came in with his bowl and sat at the scarred end, and the kitchen gathered on him like filings on a magnet. He looked at the key in Halla's hand longer than a key needs looking at.

"The hearth domain," he said. "You know I say the cost."

"I'd leave the table if you didn't," said Halla.

"The hearth is the anchor's warm end. Take it and the Fold grows around you, rooms you haven't seen yet, coming up like bread around a starter. It will keep you, and being kept is the cost. A guest can leave between two meals. The hearth can't. When the Threshold is pressed, it will be pressed on you first, and if the stores fail, they fail in your hands, and I will not lie to you about which hands hold worst. There's no form to sign. I don't write things down. I remember them. Ask anyone I've ever fed."

"I have fed five women and a god through nine days on stores meant for four," Halla said. "The cost you're naming is called Tuesday. Where do I put the key?"

"On the ring," said Sera.

She unhooked the supper bell from the lintel, where it had hung since the founding, rang it once, and put it in Halla's other hand.

"Nine years I kept altars that never answered," Sera said. "You keep a table that answers every day. The gate is yours. I vouch for the bread and for the woman, and I'm the first wife, and I don't vouch twice."

Wren hummed the blessing that keeps a seam. "The stair sings now," she said, into the quiet. "It used to be nothing there. I left it singing."

Vesper rose from the bench and put her back to the kitchen door, facing out, which was how she said yes.

Mira threaded the pantry key onto the household ring, the library, the garden shed, the north stores, the shrine, and handed it across. Five keys on one ring. The hearth was keyed now. Her part had been done since Wednesday, which nobody needed to know.

Drevik dipped two fingers in Sera's censer and marked the kitchen lintel in ash: the stroke she used at every doorway, and then a second stroke Mira had never seen, and the old wood took it the way a tapped thread takes its screw.

"There," he said. "It's remembered."

In the morning the Fold had grown. Not a wing, a rounding-out. The kitchen ran four paces deeper, and a second oven sat in the north wall with its flue already dark from a fire it had not lit yet. The pantry went back past the reach of her lamp, shelved, cool, the stores racked to a hand that reached high. A small court had opened off the garden door, the door she had hung to his memory, walled, gravel-floored, for washing and for herbs, its light at the usual late afternoon. And a stool stood by the hearth that fit a woman of Halla's build the way a cut key fits its ward.

The long table had grown a leaf. Nobody had planed it. The grain ran on from the old boards, and the scar at the west end was in it too, oiled, copied. Halla stood over the leaf a long time with her hand flat on the wood.

The apron that came with the hearth hung on the peg. Unbleached. No mend anywhere, no wear, cut full in the strings. She put it on and did not roll her sleeves.

Mira chalked the board by the pantry: doors, fourteen now, hinges, locks, one oven, one court, the pantry in paces. At the bottom she wrote the count of keys on the household ring and looked at the number a while. She had a column for locks. She had none for what five keys did to a morning. She left the chalk where it stood.

That evening the supper bell rang from the kitchen, one clean note down the corridors, and the household came without being called twice. Sera saved her the seat at Drevik's left, the seat she had stopped asking about months ago and sat in anyway. Halla served the bread warm, held over from Tuesday, and took her own end of the table, and the kitchen fit her the way a collar fits that you stop noticing.

Drevik sat down last, and the room settled around him, and Mira watched him look down the length of the table. The five of them. The new leaf. He was counting something, and it was not nails. It was slower work, the count a man makes of a debt he intends to pay in person. He did not say the cost out loud. He was not going to, yet.

The bread came in. The hearth never cooled. The home held.
