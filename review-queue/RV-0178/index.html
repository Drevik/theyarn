The flour coated Halla’s forearms like a second skin, fine and white and smelling of sun-baked grain. She did not mind the dust. In the kitchen of the Fold, dust was merely the evidence of work, and work was the only thing that kept the void from settling in the corners.

She moved between the heavy oak trestle and the stone hearth, her movements the steady arithmetic of a woman who knew exactly how much salt a loaf required and how much heat a stew could endure. The kitchen was a room of weights and measures. There was the weight of the iron pot, the measure of the rising dough, and the rhythmic, pulsing heat of the fire. Each task was a tether, a way to anchor herself to the physical world so she wouldn't drift into the grey expanse that pressed against the edges of the house.

The long table sat in the center of the room, scarred by knives and stained by the juices of a thousand meals. It was the anchor of the house. When the others sat, the table became a map of the Fold—a place where the boundaries of the women met, where the geography of their shared survival was charted in breadcrumbs and spilled wine.

Sera came in first, always. She moved with the gravity of a litched prayer, her steps quiet, her eyes already searching the air for the scent of incense or the shift in the light. She did not speak; she simply took her place by the window, her silhouette framed by the dimming sky, watching the late-afternoon light play across the void-glass.

Then came the others. Wren, with the faint, frantic hum of the Bazaar still clinging to her clothes, bringing the scent of damp thread, old silk, and the metallic tang of the street. Vesper, her presence a sharp, clean line like a well-honed blade, moved with a predatory grace that never truly relaxed. Mira, carrying the silent, meticulous tension of the Bureau’s ledgers, arrived with the smell of dried ink and the weight of much-needed order.

Halla watched them through the steam of a simmering pot. She saw the way they sat, the way they held the space he had carved for them. She saw the way they waited.

They were all waiting for him.

He did not enter so much as the room shifted to accommodate him. The air in the kitchen grew dense, weighted with a sudden, quiet importance, as if the atmospheric pressure had spiked. Drevik walked through the threshold, the hem of his deep-purple vestments sweeping the stone floor without a sound. His silver-mantled hair caught the dying light, and the gold accents of his kitsune mask gleamed like small, trapped suns.

He did not look at the food. He looked at the women.

He moved to the head of the table, his presence a vast, unreadable thing. He did not sit; he leaned against the heavy timber, his gloved hand resting on the hilt of his void staff. The crystals in his mitre drifted in slow, hypnotic orbits, casting fractured, prismatic light across the scarred wood.

"The stores are steady," Halla said. She kept her eyes on the dough, kneading it with a force that required her full attention. It was a small truth, a safe truth.

"The stores are a lie if the hungry find the larder empty," Drevik said. His voice was low, a dark chord played on a heavy string. He did not smile, but the tilt of his head suggested he was listening to a frequency no one else could hear.

"The bread is rising," Halla replied, her thumbs pressing deep into the elastic mass. "The meat is salted. There is enough for the week."

"And the week?" He paused. The weight of his gaze moved from Sera to Vesper, then rested on Halla. It was a heavy, searching look. "The week is never promised, Halla. We live in the breath between heartbeats."

"Then we should make the breath count," Vesper interjected, her voice cutting through the tension like a knife through soft cheese. She didn't look up from the small dagger she was cleaning. "If the food is there, let us eat. Worrying about next week’s hunger won't fill tomorrow's bowl."

Drevik turned his mask toward her. The gold glinted. "Vesper is right in the practical sense. But even a full belly provides no shelter from a storm that is already here."

Wren leaned forward, her fingers twitching near the edge of the table. "Is there news from the perimeter? The wind felt... heavier today. Like the air was being pushed."

"The wind is just the wind, Wren," Mira said, her tone clipped, her eyes fixed on a small ledger she had pulled from her sleeve. "The atmospheric shifts are within the predicted margins for this season. We should focus on the inventory. The spice shipments from the lower tier are delayed."

"Everything is delayed," Wren muttered, her voice dropping to a small, fearful whisper. "Everything is slowing down."

A heavy silence descended, broken only by the crackle of the hearth. Drevik reached out and picked up a small piece of crust, a remnant from the morning’s bake. He ate it with a slow, deliberate grace, his movements ritualistic. He did not ask for more. He did not demand attention. He simply occupied the space, a monument of shadow and light.

The meal was a quiet affair, a liturgy of utensils against stoneware. The sound of chewing, the scrape of spoons, the low murmur of the wind outside the Threshold. It was the sound of a world being held together by nothing more than the habit of eating.

Halla watched him from the corner of her eye. She watched the way his hands, steady and strong, moved through the space of the table as he passed the communal pot. He was a man made of secrets and starlight, a man who held the doors of the universe shut with the sheer force of his will. She saw the way the others looked at him—Slammed with devotion, Wren with a desperate yearning, Vesper with a wary, professional respect.

They saw the Ascendant. They saw the protector.

Halla saw the way his shoulders carried the invisible weight of the debt. She saw it in the way he breathed, a heavy, rhythmic pull that seemed to draw the very light from the room. She saw the way he looked at the shadows in the corners, as if expecting them to take shape and demand payment.

She knew things the others did not. She knew the cost of the grain. She knew the exact number of days until the salt ran low. And she knew the secret she carried, tucked away in the folds of her apron, as hidden as the flour in her skin.

It was a small thing. A piece of paper. A scrap of something that should not be in the Fold.

She had found it tucked into the corner of a crate of dried herbs, a thin vellum slip that felt too cold to the touch, a cold that seemed to bypass the skin and settle directly against the bone. It bore no seal, no signature, only a single line of script that seemed to pulse with a faint, dying light, as if the ink itself were exhaling its final breath.

The interest is due.

She had hidden it beneath the floorboards of the larder, beneath the heavy jars of preserved plums. She had told herself it was nothing. A fragment of a discarded ledger. A trick of the light.

But as she watched Drevik lean back, his star-pupilled eyes fixed on the dark expanse beyond the window, Halla felt the truth of it. The debt was not just his. The debt was the house.

The meal ended as it always did. The plates were cleared; the candles were trimmed. The others drifted away to their duties, leaving the kitchen to the cooling hearth and the lingering scent of yeast.

Drevik remained for a moment. He stood by the fire, the orange glow turning his silver hair to copper. He did indeed look at the fire, but his eyes were not on the flames; they were on the darkness just beyond their reach. He did not speak. He simply watched the embers die.

"Halla," he said.

She paused, a damp cloth in her hand, wiping the surface of the heavy oak. "Yes, My Lord?"

"The hearth is steady tonight."

"It is," she said, her heart thudding a frantic rhythm against her ribs. "As it must be."

He nodded, a short, sharp movement. Then, he turned and walked toward the door, his shadow stretching long and thin across the floor, a dark finger pointing toward the void.

Halla waited until the sound of his footsteps had faded completely. She waited until the silence of the Fold became heavy, pressing against her ears like the weight of deep water.

She walked to the larder. She moved the jar of plums, the glass clinking softly against the wood. She lifted the loose stone, her fingers trembling.

The vellum was still there. She pulled it from the dark earth beneath the floor. It felt colder than before, an icy needle pressing into her palm.

She did not read the words again. She did not need to. She simply held the paper, feeling the way it seemed to pull at the very air in the room, a small, silent puncture in the fabric of their sanctuary.

The Fold was growing. The rooms were expanding. The family was larger. But as Halla looked at the dark, empty space where the fire had been, she realized that a house can only grow so much before the foundation begins to crack under the weight of what it holds.

She replaced the stone. She wiped her hands on her apron. She went back to the table and began to scrub, the repetitive motion the only thing keeping the dread from settling into her bones.

In the corner of the kitchen, the shadows stayed dark, and for the first time in all her years, Halla found herself wishing for the light.

Then, a sound.

It was not the wind. It was not the settling of the house.

It was the sharp, unmistakable sound of something sliding—a thin, metallic rasp against the stone floor.

Halla froze. Her cloth stayed pressed against the wood. The kitchen was silent, save for the dying hiss of the embers.

Slowly, she turned her head toward the doorway.

There, caught in the sliver of moonlight filtering through the high vent, was a movement. A piece of parchment, far larger than her secret, had been caught in a draft. It was sliding across the floor, drawn toward the center of the room by some unseen, magnetic force.

It didn't look like a note. It looked like a law.

As it came to rest in the middle of the floor, the light hit the heavy, embossed lettering of a master seal. It was a Charter—a document of ownership, of boundaries, of irrevocable commands.

Halla stared at it. The paper seemed to pulse with a light that was not her own. The heavy, gold-leafed crest of the High Hegemony stared back at her, unblinking and cold.

The weight of the secret in her pocket suddenly felt much, much heavier than the paper on the floor.
