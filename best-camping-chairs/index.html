Best Camping Chairs 2026: From Ultralight to 800-Pound Capacity

September 2026 · 5 products reviewed

I used to think camping chairs were all the same. Fold up, sit down, crack a drink. Then I spent a week in a $15 folding chair that left my back in knots, and a separate trip hauling a 7-pound lounger up a trail I should never have brought it on. The right chair matters more than you'd think, and the wrong one can turn a relaxing evening into a fidgety, uncomfortable slog.

The camping chair market splits roughly into three categories: ultralight chairs you can backpack with, midweight chairs for car camping, and heavy-duty loungers built to hold a small boulder. I tested five chairs across those categories, from the 1-pound Helinox Chair Zero to the 800-pound-capacity ALPS King Kong, and lived with each one for at least three outings. Here's what actually holds up.

What to Look For in a Camping Chair

Before we get into the picks, a quick rundown of what separates a good camp chair from a yard sale special:

  • Weight vs. comfort: This is the fundamental tradeoff. Ultralight chairs sacrifice padding and seat width. Heavy chairs sacrifice portability. Know how you'll get to camp. If you're hiking in, every ounce matters. If you're driving, weight is irrelevant.
  • Packed size: A chair that packs into a stuff sack the size of a Nalgene bottle fits in your backpack. A chair that needs its own duffel bag only works if you have a car or a canoe.
  • Weight capacity: Choose a chair rated at least 25% above your body weight. A 200-pound person in a 250-pound-rated chair will make that chair sag by the end of the first season.
  • Seat height and width: Low chairs (under 10 inches) are great for concerts and beach days but miserable if you have bad knees. Tall chairs (15+ inches) are easier to get in and out of. Seat width matters too. If your hips are wider than 18 inches, look for a chair with at least a 20-inch seat.
  • Frame material: Aluminum (lighter, corrosion-resistant, more expensive) vs. steel (heavier, cheaper, rusts if the coating scratches). For backpacking, aluminum is the only choice. For car camping, steel is fine and actually more stable on uneven ground.
  • Setup: Shock-cord poles that snap together are faster but can pinch fingers. Folding frames that unfold in one motion are easier but heavier. Practice setting up your chair before you need to do it in the dark.

Helinox Chair Zero: Best for Backpacking

At 1 pound 2 ounces, the Helinox Chair Zero is the chair you actually bring backpacking. It packs down to 4 x 10 inches, roughly the size of a 1-liter Nalgene, and the stuff sack clips to your pack's exterior without adding noticeable bulk. DAC aluminum poles snap together with shock-cord assistance, and setup takes about 90 seconds once you've done it a couple times.

The seat sits 6 inches off the ground, which is lower than most camping chairs. That's the tradeoff for the weight. Getting in and out requires a slight squat. If you have knee issues or prefer a higher seat, the Helinox Chair One (2.1 pounds, 14-inch seat height) is the better choice and still qualifies as ultralight.

Comfort is surprisingly good for a 1-pound chair. The suspended fabric conforms to your body, and the high back (relative to the seat height) supports your shoulders and neck. I've sat in one for a 2-hour campfire session without feeling the frame through the fabric. The 265-pound weight capacity covers most hikers, but larger folks should consider the Chair One or a non-backpacking chair.

Wind is the Chair Zero's enemy. At 1 pound, it's light enough that a strong gust will knock it over, with or without you in it. On exposed ridges, I weighted the legs with rocks. The thin fabric also collects condensation in humid conditions, which isn't a dealbreaker but means you might be sitting on a damp chair in the morning.

The price is the main barrier. At $160, it's one of the most expensive lightweight chairs available. You can find lighter chairs for less, but they tend to have narrower seats, lower weight ratings, or less durable construction. The Chair Zero has been a backpacking staple for years for a reason: it actually holds up.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I look for in a camping chair?

It depends on how you camp. Backpackers need weight under 2 pounds and a packed size that fits inside or straps to their pack. Car campers should prioritize comfort and durability over weight. Look for a weight capacity at least 25% above your body weight, fabric that doesn't sag after a season, and a frame that doesn't wobble on uneven ground. Cup holders sound silly until you're holding a drink and a phone and trying to get out of your chair.

Are expensive camping chairs worth it?

For backpacking, yes. Ultralight chairs like the Helinox Chair Zero use DAC aluminum poles that are both lighter and stronger than cheaper alternatives, and they pack down small enough to actually bring on a trip. A $30 chair that weighs 4 pounds never leaves the car. For car camping, the difference is comfort and longevity. A $60 ALPS Mountaineering King Kong will outlast three $20 big-box chairs and be more comfortable the entire time.

Can camping chairs handle rain and weather?

Most camping chairs use polyester or nylon fabric that resists water and dries quickly. The real concern is the frame. Aluminum frames corrode if left wet for extended periods, though a single rainy trip won't hurt them. Steel frames rust faster if the coating gets scratched. The best practice is to shake off your chair after rain, let it dry in the sun, and store it dry. No camping chair should be left outside year-round.

What is the best lightweight camping chair for backpacking?

The Helinox Chair Zero weighs 1 pound 2 ounces and packs down to the size of a Nalgene bottle. It's the most popular ultralight chair for backpackers. If you want more back support, the Helinox Chair One adds 1 pound of weight but gives you a taller seat back and wider seat. For the best comfort-to-weight ratio, the REI Co-op Flexlite Camp Dreamer at 2.1 pounds offers a high back and seat vents for airflow.

How much weight can a camping chair hold?

It varies wildly by model. Budget folding chairs often rate for 200–250 pounds. Mid-range camp chairs from REI and NEMO typically support 250–300 pounds. Heavy-duty chairs like the ALPS Mountaineering King Kong are rated for 800 pounds. If you're near the weight limit of a chair, go one size up. A chair straining under your weight will sag, wobble, and eventually fail at the worst possible moment.

Ready to Pick Your Chair?

For car campers who want maximum comfort, the ALPS Mountaineering King Kong is the clear winner. It's built like a tank and comfortable enough to sit in all day. Backpackers should look at the Helinox Chair Zero for ultralight performance, and the REI Flexlite Camp Dreamer for the best balance of comfort and weight at a reasonable price.
