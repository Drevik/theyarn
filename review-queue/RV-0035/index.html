Core idea

Relationships are not static structures; they are dynamic processes characterized by natural cycles of expansion and contraction. Endings are not failures or verdicts on your worth, but rather a normal phase of the relational ebb and flow. In a networked style of relating, a breakup does not have to mean the destruction of a connection, but can instead be a renegotiation of intensity or a transition into friendship. The ultimate goal of ethical non-monogamy is to foster a "Slut Utopia"—a community strengthened by the radical honesty and consensual connection found in transparent intimacy.

Key concepts and terms

  • The Ebb and Flow: The concept that intimacy levels, frequency of contact, and emotional depth naturally fluctuate over time due to external circumstances or internal shifts.
  • Breakup Etiquette: The set of social responsibilities one holds when a relationship ends, specifically regarding the management of shared friend groups, social media boundaries, and the "visibility" of exes within a communal network.
  • Renegotiation: The process of changing the "rules" or the "structure" of a relationship (e.g., moving from romantic partners to platonic friends) rather than simply terminating the connection.
  • Slut Manifesto/Utopia: A political and social vision where the principles of polyamory—consent, honesty, and communication—are applied to the broader community to create stronger, more resilient social bonds.
  • Network-style Relating: A way of organizing connections where people are viewed as nodes in a web; because you have multiple points of contact, the loss of one node does not collapse the entire system.

Techniques worth drilling

  1. The Honesty Audit for Terminations

When deciding to end a relationship, you must audit your motives to ensure you are meeting the "honesty requirement."

  • Step 1: Identify the primary driver of the breakup (e.g., loss of attraction, incompatibility, or desire for more autonomy).
  • Step 2: Separate "circumstantial" reasons (e.g., "I am too busy for this") from "fundamental" reasons (e.g., "I no longer value this person").
  • Step 3: Script the conversation using "I" statements that focus on your internal truth rather than the other person’s flaws, preventing unnecessary resentment.
  1. The Social Media and Shared Space Protocol

To prevent "breakup fallout" in a shared community, you must proactively design your digital and physical boundaries.

  • Step 1: Identify "high-friction" zones (e.g., shared Instagram tags, mutual group chats, or a shared hobby group).
  • Step 2: Propose a "Mute or Unfollow" agreement: decide if you will temporarily unfollow each other to allow for emotional healing.
  • Step 3: Set a "Public Neutrality" rule: agree that neither party will disparage the other to mutual friends or use social media to "signal" distress to the group.
  1. The Multi-Tiered Agreement Template

Create a structural framework for how contact will change post-breakup to avoid the "ghosting" or "hovering" trap.

  • Step 1 (Immediate): Define the "No-Contact" or "Low-Contact" period. Determine if you need a total break or just a cessation of romantic/sexual intimacy.
  • Step 2 (Six Weeks): Define the "Re-entry" parameters. Determine if a check-in text is allowed or if you should stick to strictly group-setting interactions.
  • Step 3 (Six Months): Define the "New Normal." Determine if the goal is to transition to friendship, become "friendly acquaintances," or remain "distant nodes" in the network.

Practice drill

This week, you will perform a two-part structural exercise to build your "relational contingency plan."

Part A: The Post-Mortem (Refining the Past) Think of a past relationship or connection that ended painfully or left you with unresolved tension. Write three brief, different stories (one paragraph each) of how that breakup could have been constructive. In each story, you must:

  1. Describe a specific way both you and the other person worked through the difficult emotions.
  2. Invent a specific "Agreement Tier" for that version of the story: what would you have agreed upon for immediately after, six weeks later, and six months later?

Part B: The Contingency Template (Preparing the Present) Apply this same template to a current relationship (romantic, sexual, or close platonic). Do not wait for a crisis to decide how to handle an end. Write down your "Protocol for Change" right now:

  • Tier 1 (Immediate): If we were to stop seeing each other romantically today, what is the rule for communication for the next 14 days?
  • Tier 2 (Six Weeks): What is the rule for how we interact in group settings or group chats?
  • Tier 3 (Six Months): What is the goal for our connection at this stage (e.g., friendship, distant acquaintance, or complete transition)?

Critiques and caveats

  • The Burden of Emotional Labor: The "renegotiation" model assumes both parties have the emotional capacity and maturity to engage in complex communication. If one partner is avoidant or abusive, "renegotiating into friendship" is not a healthy option; total severance may be necessary for safety.
  • The "Polyamory Privilege" Trap: The idea that "exes stay visible" and can be managed via etiquette assumes a level of social stability. In environments of high instability or intense jealousy, the "network-style" approach can inadvertently lead to "drama-looping" if boundaries are not strictly enforced.
  • The Risk of False Hope: Attempting to "renegotiate" a breakup into a friendship can sometimes be a way of delaying the necessary grief of a loss. You must distinguish between a genuine desire for friendship and a fear of letting go.

Reading notes

This final section serves as the synthesis of the book's entire philosophy. It moves the reader from the tactical management of individual relationships to a macro-view of how these ethics impact society at large. It connects the "micro" work of managing jealousy and communication to the "macro" goal of building a more honest, resilient community.
