Scene Doctor — Part 1: The Diagnostic System

Why Scenes Break

Scenes are the atoms of fiction. When a scene works, readers don't think about it — they just feel pulled forward. When a scene breaks, readers feel it immediately, even if they can't name what's wrong.

"I got bored." "Something felt off." "I skimmed that part."

These are scene failures. And they always come from one of six missing elements.

The 6 Elements of a Working Scene

Every functional scene has these six elements. If any one is missing or weak, the scene feels "off."

1. Goal

The POV character wants something specific in this scene. Not a vague desire — a concrete, achievable thing they're pursuing right now.

Strong goal: "Get the security code from Marcus before he leaves." Weak goal: "Make progress on the investigation." Missing goal: Character drifts through the scene without wanting anything.

2. Conflict

Something actively opposes the goal. Not background tension — direct, immediate resistance.

Strong conflict: Marcus refuses to give the code. Weak conflict: The protagonist feels uncertain about asking. Missing conflict: The protagonist asks, and Marcus just... gives them the code.

3. Stakes

Failure has consequences the reader cares about. If the character doesn't get what they want, something bad happens — or something good doesn't happen.

Strong stakes: Without the code, the mission fails and people die. Weak stakes: It would be nice to have the code. Missing stakes: There's no reason to care whether the character succeeds.

4. Turn

The scene ends in a different place than it started. The situation has changed.

Strong turn: They get the code, but now Marcus knows they're a spy. Weak turn: They get the code (success with no complication). Missing turn: The scene ends exactly where it started (status quo).

5. Emotion

The reader feels something during the scene. Not just observes — feels.

Strong emotion: Tension, dread, relief, surprise, heartbreak. Weak emotion: Mild interest, intellectual engagement. Missing emotion: The scene is a data transfer — information without feeling.

6. Integration

The scene connects to the larger story. It moves the plot, develops a character, or sets up a future event.

Strong integration: The scene resolves a plot thread and opens a new one. Weak integration: The scene is nice but could be removed without changing the story. Missing integration: The scene is a self-contained vignette that doesn't connect to anything.

How to Diagnose a Scene

  1. Fill in the Scene Diagnostic Worksheet (one worksheet per scene)
  2. Score each element from 0 (missing) to 3 (strong)
  3. Look at the pattern — which elements score low?
  4. Look up your pattern in the Fix Library
  5. Apply the specific prescription
  6. Re-score the scene after your revision

The Scoring System

| Score | Meaning | Indicator | |---|---|---| | 3 | Strong | Element is present, specific, and drives the scene | | 2 | Functional | Element is present but could be sharper | | 1 | Weak | Element is vague, passive, or barely there | | 0 | Missing | Element doesn't exist in this scene |

Total possible: 18 points.

| Total Score | Diagnosis | Action | |---|---|---| | 15-18 | Healthy scene | Polish only | | 12-14 | Functional but weak | Strengthen low-scoring elements | | 8-11 | Problem scene | Diagnose low scores, apply targeted fixes | | 4-7 | Broken scene | Major rewrite or cut | | 0-3 | Dead scene | Cut without mercy |

But the total score isn't the important part. The pattern of scores is what tells you what's wrong. Two scenes scoring 10/18 can have completely different problems.

Common Score Patterns and What They Mean

| Pattern | Example Scores | Diagnosis | Fix Focus | |---|---|---|---| | Goal + Conflict weak | G:1 C:0 S:3 T:3 E:3 I:3 | Setup scene — stakes exist but nothing's happening | Give the character a goal and opposition | | Stakes weak | G:3 C:3 S:0 T:2 E:1 I:3 | So-what scene — things happen but reader doesn't care | Raise the stakes (what bad thing happens if they fail?) | | Turn weak | G:3 C:3 S:3 T:0 E:2 I:2 | Spin-in-place scene — effort but no change | Force a turn (success with complication, or failure with revelation) | | Emotion weak | G:3 C:3 S:3 T:3 E:0 I:3 | Data-dump scene — everything works on paper but feels flat | Ground the conflict in character emotion | | Integration weak | G:3 C:3 S:3 T:3 E:3 I:0 | Island scene — great on its own but doesn't serve the story | Connect to a plot thread or character arc | | Everything weak | G:1 C:1 S:0 T:0 E:1 I:1 | Dead scene — doesn't earn its place | Cut it or completely reconceive it |

When to Use Scene Doctor

  • After your first draft — diagnose every scene before revising
  • During revision — when you know a scene is broken but can't figure out why
  • Before cutting — verify a scene is truly dead before you remove it
  • After beta feedback — when readers say "I got bored here" or "this dragged"
  • When outlining — fill in the diagnostic for planned scenes to catch problems before you write them

Scene Doctor — Part 2: The Fix Library

30 Scene Problems, 30 Specific Prescriptions

Each prescription targets a specific diagnostic pattern. Find your pattern, apply the fix.

GOAL PROBLEMS

Problem 1: No Goal (Score: G-0) The POV character drifts through the scene without wanting anything. → Fix: Give them a specific, concrete desire. "Survive the meeting" isn't enough. "Convince Marcus to lend her the car" is specific. The goal should be achievable within the scene and opposed by something.

Problem 2: Vague Goal (Score: G-1) The character "wants to make progress" or "wants to figure things out." → Fix: Make it concrete. "Wants to find the missing page before sunset" is specific. "Wants to solve the mystery" is vague. Ask: what would they tell someone they're trying to do?

Problem 3: Passive Goal (Score: G-1) The character wants something but doesn't act on it. → Fix: Give them a scene action. They should DO something to pursue their goal, not just hope it happens. Instead of "wants to be accepted," they "tries to prove themselves by volunteering for the dangerous mission."

Problem 4: Wrong-Character Goal (Score: G-2) The protagonist has a goal, but a secondary character's goal is more interesting. → Fix: Switch POV to the character who has the most to lose in this scene. Or transfer the secondary character's goal to the protagonist.

CONFLICT PROBLEMS

Problem 5: No Conflict (Score: C-0) The character wants something and gets it without resistance. → Fix: Add opposition. Someone or something must block the goal. If the character needs information, make the source reluctant. If they need entry, make the door locked. If they need agreement, make the other person disagree.

Problem 6: Internal-Only Conflict (Score: C-1) The character debates with themselves but faces no external opposition. → Fix: Externalize the conflict. Another character should want the opposite thing. Even internal conflict works better when there's an external trigger forcing the decision.

Problem 7: Conflict Without Stakes (Score: C-2, S-0) Characters argue but nothing matters if one of them loses. → Fix: Attach consequences to the conflict. If Character A wins, what does Character B lose? Make both sides want something real.

Problem 8: Late Conflict (Score: C-2) The conflict doesn't start until halfway through the scene. → Fix: Front-load it. Introduce the opposition in the first paragraph. The goal and conflict should be clear within the first 3-4 lines.

STAKES PROBLEMS

Problem 9: No Stakes (Score: S-0) If the character fails, nothing bad happens. → Fix: Answer: "What bad thing happens if they don't get what they want?" If the answer is "nothing," you don't have a scene. Raise the stakes until failure costs something.

Problem 10: Vague Stakes (Score: S-1) "The fate of the kingdom" or "everything will change" — too big to feel real. → Fix: Make stakes personal and specific. Not "the kingdom will fall" but "her brother will be executed at dawn." One person's specific loss hits harder than a million faceless casualties.

Problem 11: Delayed Stakes (Score: S-1) The consequences are far in the future ("someday this will matter"). → Fix: Add a ticking clock. The consequence should feel imminent. "If she doesn't get the code by midnight, the backup team walks into an ambush."

Problem 12: Unearned Stakes (Score: S-2) Big stakes that the reader hasn't been prepared to care about. → Fix: Build investment before the stakes escalate. The reader needs to care about the thing that's threatened. Show it, make the reader love it, then threaten it.

TURN PROBLEMS

Problem 13: No Turn (Score: T-0) The scene ends exactly where it started. Nothing has changed. → Fix: Force a change. The answer to every scene should be "yes, but" (they get what they want, but with a complication) or "no, and" (they don't get it, and things get worse). Never "yes, period" or "no, but it's fine."

Problem 14: Arbitrary Turn (Score: T-1) Something changes, but it's coincidence or deus ex machina, not earned. → Fix: The turn must come from the conflict. If the character gets the information, it's because they earned it through action. If they don't, it's because the opposition was stronger. No coincidences.

Problem 15: Reversible Turn (Score: T-1) The scene changes something, but the next scene reverses it. → Fix: Make turns irreversible. Once the character knows something, they can't unknow it. Once a relationship changes, it stays changed. Give the turn weight by making it permanent.

Problem 16: Minor Turn (Score: T-2) The scene changes something small when it should change something big. → Fix: Escalate. Each scene's turn should be slightly bigger than the last. If your scene turns on a minor detail, ask: what would make this turn land harder?

EMOTION PROBLEMS

Problem 17: No Emotion (Score: E-0) The reader observes without feeling. → Fix: Ground the scene in the POV character's emotional state. What are they feeling? Not "nervous" — "her stomach clenched like she'd swallowed a stone." Specific physical sensation > abstract emotion label.

Problem 18: Told Emotion (Score: E-1) "She was sad" or "He felt angry." → Fix: Show through body language, action, and specific detail. Not "She was devastated" — "She stood at the counter, fingers on her phone, rereading the message for the tenth time without seeing it."

Problem 19: Wrong Emotion (Score: E-1) The scene is trying to be tense but reads as flat, or trying to be sad but reads as melodramatic. → Fix: Match the prose rhythm to the emotion. Tension needs short sentences and clipped dialogue. Sadness needs longer, flowing sentences. Action needs verbs, not adjectives. Match form to feeling.

Problem 20: Monotone Emotion (Score: E-2) One emotion sustained throughout without variation. → Fix: Layer emotions. Tension with a flash of hope. Relief undercut by guilt. Humor in the face of fear. The most powerful emotions are complex ones — give the reader two things to feel at once.

INTEGRATION PROBLEMS

Problem 21: Island Scene (Score: I-0) The scene is self-contained and doesn't connect to anything. → Fix: Thread it into the story. The scene should either (a) advance the main plot, (b) develop a character arc, or (c) set up a future event. If it does none of these, cut it.

Problem 22: Redundant Scene (Score: I-1) This scene does the same job as another scene. → Fix: Merge them. If two scenes both reveal the villain's plan, combine them into one stronger scene. Or give each a different purpose: scene A reveals the plan, scene B shows the first consequence.

Problem 23: Setup Without Payoff (Score: I-1) This scene sets something up that never pays off later. → Fix: Either add the payoff in a later scene, or cut the setup. Every gun must fire (Chekhov's principle). If you plant a detail, it must matter.

Problem 24: Payoff Without Setup (Score: I-1) Something important happens with no prior setup. → Fix: Add setup earlier. If a character pulls out a lockpick in chapter 20, show them carrying lockpicks in chapter 5. If the villain knows the hero's weakness, show them learning it.

COMPOUND PROBLEMS

Problem 25: The Tourist (G:1 C:0 S:1 T:0 E:1 I:1) Character wanders through the setting, observing things, but not pursuing a goal or facing opposition. → Fix: Give them a reason to be there and something blocking them. Tourism isn't drama.

Problem 26: The Info Dump (G:1 C:0 S:0 T:0 E:0 I:2) Information delivered without conflict or stakes. → Fix: Embed the information in conflict. Two characters argue about what the information means. Someone doesn't want the information known. The information comes at a cost.

Problem 27: The Tea Scene (G:2 C:1 S:0 T:0 E:0 I:1) Characters chat pleasantly but nothing changes and nothing's at risk. → Fix: Add tension. Even friendly scenes need an undercurrent. One character wants something from the other. One is hiding something. The conversation has a subtext that creates low-grade tension.

Problem 28: The Ping Pong (G:2 C:2 S:1 T:0 E:0 I:1) Rapid back-and-forth action that doesn't change the situation. → Fix: Every exchange should shift the balance of power. If the scene starts with Character A in control and ends the same way, there's no turn. Make the power shift during the scene.

Problem 29: The Mirror (G:1 C:0 S:0 T:0 E:1 I:0) Character thinks about their feelings without doing anything. → Fix: Make the internal struggle external. Put them in a scene where they have to ACT on their feelings, not just think about them. Decision under pressure beats contemplation every time.

Problem 30: The Placeholder (G:0 C:0 S:0 T:0 E:0 I:1) The scene exists only to get characters from point A to point B. → Fix: Either add the 5 missing elements (making it a real scene) or compress it to a single transitional paragraph. No scene should exist solely for transportation.

Scene Doctor — Part 3: Scene Types & Common Problems

The 8 Scene Archetypes

Most scenes fall into one of these categories. Each type has predictable strengths and predictable failure modes. Know the type, and you know what to check first.

1. The Confrontation

Two or more characters in direct opposition.

Common strengths: Conflict (C) is usually strong. Stakes (S) tend to be clear. Common weakness: Turn (T) — confrontations often resolve without changing the situation.

Diagnostic shortcuts:

  • Check T first. Does this confrontation change anything?
  • Check E. Are we feeling the tension, or just watching two people argue?
  • Watch for "ping pong" — characters trading lines without shifts in power.

Typical fix: Make sure the balance of power shifts during the confrontation. One character enters in control; by the end, the other has leverage.

2. The Revelation

A character learns something that changes their understanding.

Common strengths: Turn (T) is usually strong — new information changes everything. Common weakness: Emotion (E) — the revelation is told, not felt.

Diagnostic shortcuts:

  • Check E first. Does the reader feel the impact, or just receive data?
  • Check S. Does this revelation matter? If the reader doesn't care, the stakes aren't personal enough.
  • Watch for "data dump" — information without emotional weight.

Typical fix: Ground the revelation in the character's physical and emotional reaction. What does it feel like to learn this? Show the before (what they believed) and the after (how it shatters them).

3. The Decision

A character faces a choice between two or more options.

Common strengths: Goal (G) is clear — the character is deciding. Common weakness: Conflict (C) — the decision is too easy, or there's only one real option.

Diagnostic shortcuts:

  • Check C. Are both options genuinely viable? If one is obviously better, there's no real choice.
  • Check S. What's lost regardless of which option they choose? The best decisions have a cost either way.
  • Watch for "obvious choice" — if the reader knows which option is right, the character isn't really deciding.

Typical fix: Make both options costly. Whatever the character chooses, something valuable is lost. The strength of a decision scene is the cost of choosing.

4. The Chase / Escape

A character pursues or flees. Action-driven, fast-paced.

Common strengths: Conflict (C) and Emotion (E) tend to be present (physical danger creates both). Common weakness: Integration (I) — the chase happens but doesn't advance the plot.

Diagnostic shortcuts:

  • Check I first. Does this chase change the story's trajectory, or is it a set piece?
  • Check T. What's different after the chase? Does the character end up somewhere new, or with new information?
  • Watch for "empty action" — thrilling but inconsequential.

Typical fix: The chase must resolve something. Either the character catches/fails to catch their target (turn), or they discover something during the pursuit (revelation). A chase that ends where it started is just noise.

5. The Quiet Moment

Characters connect, reflect, or prepare. Low action, high emotion.

Common strengths: Emotion (E) can be strong if written well. Common weakness: Conflict (C) and Turn (T) — nothing seems to happen.

Diagnostic shortcuts:

  • Check C. Even quiet moments need tension. What's the unspoken thing between these characters?
  • Check T. Does the relationship shift during this scene, even slightly?
  • Watch for "tea scene" — pleasant chat with no subtext or change.

Typical fix: Add subtext. The characters are saying one thing and meaning another. The surface is calm; the undercurrent is turbulent. And something in their relationship must shift, even if it's small.

6. The Setup

Information or positioning for a future payoff.

Common weakness: Everything. Setups often fail every element because they feel like homework the writer is making the reader do.

Diagnostic shortcuts:

  • Check G. Does the POV character want something in THIS scene, not just in a future scene?
  • Check E. Is the reader engaged NOW, or just tolerating this scene because it's "important later"?
  • Watch for "placeholder" — a scene that exists only to set up a future scene.

Typical fix: The setup must work as a scene in its own right. Give the character a goal that's relevant NOW, add conflict, make the reader feel something. If the scene only works because of what happens later, it doesn't work.

7. The Reversal

Something the character believed turns out to be wrong.

Common strengths: Turn (T) is built in — the reversal IS the turn. Common weakness: Setup (I) — if the reader hasn't been prepared, the reversal feels cheap.

Diagnostic shortcuts:

  • Check I first. Was this reversal set up earlier? Can the reader go back and find the clues?
  • Check E. Does the character react with appropriate intensity, or does the revelation land flat?
  • Watch for "unearned twist" — a reversal that comes from nowhere.

Typical fix: Plant clues in earlier scenes (setup without payoff, which now pays off). The reversal should feel surprising in the moment but inevitable in retrospect.

8. The Climax

The scene everything has been building toward. Maximum stakes, maximum emotion.

Common strengths: Stakes (S) and Goal (G) tend to be strong. Common weakness: The climax can feel rushed, or the resolution can feel too easy.

Diagnostic shortcuts:

  • Check C. Is the opposition as strong as it can be? The climax should face the protagonist with their hardest challenge.
  • Check T. Does the climax resolve the central conflict, or just end the scene?
  • Watch for "deus ex machina" — an external force resolves things instead of the character earning it.

Typical fix: The protagonist must earn the outcome through their own action and growth. If a convenient event resolves the climax, rewrite it so the character's choice and sacrifice create the resolution.

Quick-Reference: Scene Type → First Check

| Scene Type | Check This First | Most Common Fix | |---|---|---| | Confrontation | Turn (T) | Power shift during scene | | Revelation | Emotion (E) | Ground in character reaction | | Decision | Conflict (C) | Both options must cost something | | Chase / Escape | Integration (I) | Must advance the plot | | Quiet Moment | Conflict (C) | Add subtext and relationship shift | | Setup | Goal (G) and Emotion (E) | Must work as a scene NOW | | Reversal | Integration (I) | Plant clues earlier | | Climax | Conflict (C) | Character earns the resolution |

Product Brief: Scene Doctor

Product ID: SCN-DOC-003 Price: $29-39 Category: Writing & Publishing → Tools Format: PDF + printable worksheets (digital download) Target Audience: Fiction writers who know a scene "doesn't work" but can't diagnose WHY

Problem Statement

Every writer knows the feeling: you read a scene and something's wrong. The pacing drags. The dialogue falls flat. The tension evaporates. But when you try to fix it, you don't know where to start — so you either rewrite the whole thing or polish the prose and hope that fixes it.

The problem isn't that you can't write scenes. The problem is you can't diagnose them. Free advice tells you "every scene needs conflict" or "show don't tell" but doesn't give you a systematic way to figure out WHICH element is missing, WHICH fix to apply, and HOW to test whether your fix actually worked.

Nobody sells a mechanic's diagnostic checklist for broken scenes — a fill-in sheet that walks you through each scene, identifies the specific problem, and prescribes the specific fix.

Solution

Scene Doctor is a scene diagnostic system with printable worksheets. You fill in a structured checklist for each scene, and the worksheet tells you what's wrong and what to do about it.

The Diagnostic Framework

Every scene needs 6 elements to work. The Scene Doctor tests each one:

  1. Goal — Does the POV character want something specific?
  2. Conflict — Is something actively blocking that goal?
  3. Stakes — Does failure have meaningful consequences?
  4. Turn — Does the situation change from beginning to end?
  5. Emotion — Does the reader feel something?
  6. Integration — Does this scene connect to the larger story?

Each element gets a score. The pattern of scores tells you what's wrong and what to fix.

Key Differentiator

This isn't writing advice. It's a diagnostic tool. You don't need to learn scene theory — you fill in the worksheet, and it tells you what's broken and how to fix it. Like a mechanic running diagnostics on a car, not a textbook on how engines work.

Deliverables

  2. Scene Diagnostic Worksheet — The core 6-element diagnostic (printable, one per scene)
  3. Scene Triage Card — Quick-reference "what's wrong and how to fix it"
  4. Scene Type Reference — The 8 scene archetypes and their common problems
  5. Fix Library — 30 specific prescriptions for 30 specific scene problems
