Chapter 3: The Initiate's Trial

The blindfold smelled like old copper and dried sage.

Drevik sat in a wooden chair that had probably been new when his grandmother was a girl, hands resting on his knees, waiting for whatever came next. The room around him hummed with whispered conversations he couldn't quite make out—like listening to a radio tuned to the wrong frequency. Someone had told him three days of testing. Someone else had mentioned that initiates failed. No one had mentioned what failure meant.

He didn't ask. He wasn't sure he wanted to know.

"The first trial concerns knowledge," a woman's voice said from somewhere to his left. Cultured. Precise. Like a professor who'd been teaching the same material for decades and lost patience somewhere around year ten. "You will answer truthfully, or you will answer nothing. The choice is always yours, Initiate Markinsoul. Choose poorly, and the trial ends."

"I understand." His voice came out steadier than he felt.

"Good. Remove your covering."

Drevik pulled the blindfold off and squinted against sudden candlelight. The room was smaller than he'd expected—a semicircular chamber with stone walls that wept moisture in the flickering light. Three figures sat at a curved table: a woman with iron-gray hair pulled into a severe bun, a man whose face was half-hidden by shadow, and an elderly man with folded hands and closed eyes.

The written examination lay before him on a plain wooden desk. A single piece of parchment covered in symbols he didn't recognize, questions written in a language that looked like it had evolved from Latin and something older. Much older.

"Begin," the woman said.

Drevik picked up the pen.

The first question asked about the relationship between lunar phases and shadow conductivity. He'd never studied astronomy. He'd never heard of shadow conductivity. But as he read the words, something stirred in the back of his mind—knowledge that felt like remembering rather than learning. The moon's pull affected the veil between worlds. During the new moon, the barrier thinned. During the full, it strengthened. Shadow magic flowed like water through those channels, and a skilled practitioner could—

He blinked. Where had that come from?

He wrote his answer.

The second question concerned the taxonomy of Shadowlands entities. Class One through Five, with subcategories he didn't understand but somehow knew. Feeders. Stalkers. Weavers. The names felt right, like fitting a key into a lock he'd forgotten existed. The third question asked about binding oaths—the consequences of breaking them, the mechanics of their creation.

By the tenth question, Drevik's hand was cramping and his head throbbed with a dull ache behind his eyes. He kept writing. History of the Order. Notable breaches in the veil. The proper method of dispatch for various entity classifications. The significance of the Eclipsed Veil symbol—a moon partially obscured by shadow, representing the balance between light and dark, knowledge and mystery.

When he finished, he set down the pen and waited.

The elderly man opened his eyes. They were pale blue, almost colorless, and they fixed on Drevik with unsettling intensity.

"You answered every question."

"I did."

"You have never studied our texts."

"No, sir."

The man smiled. It wasn't a comforting expression. "And yet you wrote as though you'd been raised within our walls. As though you'd spent years learning what should take decades to absorb." He tilted his head. "Tell me, Initiate Markinsoul. Where does knowledge come from?"

Drevik thought about his grandmother's stories. About the dreams he'd had since childhood—dreams of running through endless twilight, of voices speaking in languages he shouldn't understand. About the way shadows had always seemed to move when he wasn't looking directly at them.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "It feels like... remembering. Like I knew it somewhere else, and I'm just now finding it again."

The three examiners exchanged glances. The woman made a note on her own parchment. The man in shadow shifted slightly, revealing a scar that ran from his jaw to his collarbone before retreating back into darkness.

"The first trial is concluded," the elderly man said. "You have passed, Initiate. Rest now. The second trial begins at dawn."

His cell was barely larger than a closet.

A cot, a basin of cold water, a window set too high in the stone wall to see anything but the ceiling of whatever larger chamber lay beyond. Drevik lay on the thin mattress and stared at the ceiling, trying to process what had happened.

He'd never believed in anything supernatural. Ghost stories were for children. Magic was for movies and escapist fiction. But the knowledge he'd accessed during the examination—that hadn't come from nowhere. Something had opened inside him, like a door swinging wide in a dark room he hadn't known existed.

His phone had been taken. His watch. The small leather journal where he kept notes for his literature class. Everything that connected him to the world outside these walls. He was alone with questions he couldn't answer and a headache that wouldn't fade.

Sleep came in fragments. Dreams of running. Dreams of searching for something he couldn't name. Dreams of eyes watching him from the darkness—amber eyes, golden eyes, the pale blue of the examiner who'd smiled like a predator scenting prey.

When the knock came at his door, he was already awake.

The second trial took place in a chamber that smelled like sweat and old blood.

Drevik stood in a loose group of perhaps a dozen others—all young, all dressed in the same plain gray clothes he'd been given, all wearing expressions that ranged from terrified to determined. He recognized none of them. They'd been processed separately, kept isolated, fed just enough information to know that they were being tested and that failure carried consequences.

The chamber was circular, with a packed dirt floor and high stone walls. Weapons lined the walls—swords, spears, chains, things Drevik couldn't identify. He'd never held a weapon in his life. The closest he'd come was a fencing demonstration at a campus fair, and he'd spent most of it trying not to look like an idiot.

"Combat is the oldest test," a man's voice boomed from somewhere above. Drevik looked up and saw a gallery of observers—faces in shadow, watching from behind a wrought-iron railing. "The Order requires strength as well as knowledge. The Shadowlands do not respect scholarship alone."

A figure emerged from a side door, moving with the easy grace of someone who'd spent years training. Female, athletic, with auburn hair pulled back in a practical tail. She wore the same gray clothes as the initiates, but she carried herself differently—shoulders back, chin lifted, the confidence of someone who'd never been uncertain of her place in the world.

Drevik recognized her immediately.

Lexi Thornwood. The woman from the campus coffee shop. The one who'd watched him like he was a problem to be solved, a variable in an equation she hadn't finished calculating. She'd been in the gallery during his first trial, he realized now. Watching. Assessing.

She stepped into the center of the chamber and surveyed the group of initiates with cold green eyes.

"I need volunteers," she said. Her voice was clear, commanding. "Combat assessment. Full contact, submission rules only. Show me what you can do."

No one moved.

"Let me clarify." She smiled, but it didn't reach her eyes. "I'm testing you. Whether you volunteer or I select you, you will be tested. The only question is whether you want to show some initiative, or whether you want me to decide that you're too cowardly to step forward."

A young man near Drevik's left raised his hand. "I'll go."

"Good." Lexi gestured to the center of the chamber. "Come forward. Name?"

"Marcus Webb."

"Any training, Webb?"

"Some boxing. A little Judo in high school."

"Better than nothing." She didn't sound impressed. "Begin when ready."

The fight was short and brutal. Marcus was competent—Drevik could see that much—but Lexi moved like water. Every attack he offered, she flowed around. Every defense he raised, she found the gap and exploited it. Within thirty seconds, he was on his back, her knee on his chest, her hand raised in a gesture Drevik didn't recognize.

"Submission," she said. "Next."

Two more volunteers. Two more brief, humiliating defeats. Lexi wasn't even breathing hard.

"Anyone else?" She scanned the group, and her eyes landed on Drevik. Held. "You. In the back. You haven't moved since you came in."

He could feel the attention of everyone in the room. The other initiates, grateful not to be chosen. The observers in the gallery, watching with expressions he couldn't read. And Lexi, waiting for his response with something that might have been anticipation.

He stepped forward.

"Name?"

"Drevik Markinsoul."

"Any training, Markinsoul?"

He thought about the question. "I've been in a few fights."

"That's not the same thing."

"I know."

She studied him for a long moment. "You were the one who passed the knowledge trial yesterday. The one who answered questions he shouldn't have known."

"I didn't cheat, if that's what you're asking."

"I'm not asking that." She shifted her stance, settling into a position that looked almost casual. "I'm asking whether you're as unusual as they say, or whether you're just another initiate who got lucky."

"I don't know." He rolled his shoulders, trying to loosen muscles that had tensed beyond reason. "Why don't you find out?"

Her smile changed. Still cold, but with an edge of something else now. Interest, maybe. Or the anticipation of a challenge she hadn't expected.

"Begin."

She moved first—a testing strike, fast and precise, aimed at his jaw. Drevik saw it coming before she threw it. Not with his eyes, but with something deeper. A sense of where she would be, where she was going, the shape of the fight stretching out ahead of them like a path through unfamiliar terrain.

He slipped the punch and stepped back.

"Good reflexes." She circled left. "But reflexes aren't enough."

She came at him again, faster this time. A combination—punch, punch, kick. He saw the sequence before it began. Blocked the first strike, dodged the second, caught her leg on the kick and shoved.

She recovered her balance instantly, but he saw the surprise flicker across her face before she masked it.

"Not bad." Her voice was lower now. Focused. "You've got some instincts."

"I told you I've been in fights."

"You did." She attacked again, and this time she wasn't testing.

Drevik had never studied martial arts. He'd never had formal training of any kind. But he'd survived three years in a group home that wasn't kind to quiet, intelligent boys, and he'd spent two more years learning how to avoid fights he couldn't win. The streets around his grandmother's house had taught him that the best fight was the one you walked away from. The second best was the one you ended quickly.

Lexi pressed him hard. She was faster, more skilled, and clearly stronger. But he could see what she was going to do before she did it. Not always—sometimes the visions were unclear, like watching television through a fog—but often enough. Often enough to block, to counter, to stay standing when he should have been on the ground.

She swept his legs. He rolled with the fall, came up with dirt on his face and a bruise forming on his shoulder. She pressed the advantage, driving him back toward the wall. He saw her next three moves, found the gap in her offense, and slipped through it.

For a moment, they were pressed close—her shoulder against his chest, his hand locked around her wrist, both of them breathing hard.

"Interesting," she murmured. "Very interesting."

Then she twisted out of his grip and swept his feet again, and this time she followed him down. A knee on his chest, her hand raised in that submission gesture, her face inches from his.

Her eyes were greener than he'd realized. A ring of amber around the pupil, catching the light.

"Yield," she said.

He yielded.

She stood and offered him a hand. He took it, let her pull him to his feet, and tried to read the expression on her face. She was harder to parse now. Still cold, still assessing, but something had shifted in the way she looked at him. As though he'd become a puzzle she wanted to solve rather than a problem she wanted to eliminate.

"Who trained you?" she asked.

"No one. I just... see it."

"See what?"

"The fight. Before it happens." He wasn't sure how to explain it. "Like remembering something that hasn't occurred yet."

Her eyes widened fractionally. Whatever he'd said, it meant something to her. Something she hadn't expected.

"Get cleaned up," she said. "The third trial begins tonight. You'll need your strength."

She turned and walked away without another word, but Drevik caught the glance she threw over her shoulder. The wariness. The reluctant respect.

He'd passed. He wasn't sure how, but he'd passed.

The third trial began at midnight.

They brought him to a chamber deep beneath the compound—a place that felt older than the building above it, older than the university, older than the city itself. The walls were rough stone, carved with symbols that seemed to shift when he wasn't looking directly at them. In the center of the room stood a pedestal, and on the pedestal rested an artifact that looked like a sphere of solidified shadow.

It wasn't glass. It wasn't crystal. It was something else entirely—something that absorbed the light around it and gave back nothing in return.

"Touch it," the elderly examiner said. He'd introduced himself as Brother Aldric, though Drevik suspected the title was ceremonial rather than familial. "Let it show you what lies within."

Drevik approached the pedestal. The sphere seemed to pulse as he drew closer, though he couldn't have said whether the movement was real or imagined.

"What will it show me?"

"Truth." Aldric's voice was soft. "The truth of your potential. Your gifts. Your weaknesses. Some initiates see nothing—they are dismissed. Some see shadows—accepted but unremarkable. A few see clearly, and those are marked for advancement." He paused. "What do you expect to see, Drevik Markinsoul?"

"I don't know." He reached out. "I've never been good at predicting the future."

His fingers brushed the surface.

Cold. So cold it burned. The sensation shot through his hand, up his arm, into his chest, his mind, his—

Visions.

Not one future. Not a single path stretching ahead of him, clear and certain. Dozens. Hundreds. Futures branching and splitting and merging again, each one different, each one possible. He saw himself standing in this chamber, accepting a robe and a title. He saw himself walking away, returning to his grandmother's house, living an ordinary life. He saw himself dying—here, there, a hundred different deaths in a hundred different places.

He saw Lexi. Fighting beside him. Fighting against him. Standing over his grave with an expression he couldn't read. Pressed against him in a room he didn't recognize, her hands in his hair, her mouth—

The visions shifted. He saw the compound in flames. He saw an Elder standing over a body, holding a blade. He saw himself making choices he didn't understand, consequences he couldn't predict. He saw—

He pulled his hand away.

The visions faded, but they didn't disappear entirely. He could still feel them, just beyond the edge of his awareness. Futures that might come to pass. Futures that never would. All of them possible, none of them certain.

Aldric was staring at him with an expression Drevik couldn't interpret. The other examiners had emerged from the shadows, and they all wore the same look.

"How long?" the woman asked. Her precise voice was less controlled now. "How long did you touch the sphere?"

"I don't know. A few seconds."

"Longer." She looked at Aldric. "Much longer. Nearly three minutes."

"Most initiates break contact within thirty seconds," Aldric said softly. "The sphere overwhelms them. They see too much, or too little, and they pull away before the truth can settle." He stepped closer, studying Drevik's face. "What did you see, Initiate?"

"Many futures." Drevik's voice sounded strange to his own ears. "Branching paths. Possibilities."

"You saw more than one?"

"Hundreds. Maybe more."

The examiners exchanged glances. Aldric's expression had shifted into something that looked almost like reverence.

"Foresight," he said. "True foresight. Not the cheap fortune-telling of charlatans, but the genuine sight—the ability to see what might be, to understand the consequences of choices before they're made." He shook his head slowly. "In all my years, I've seen perhaps a dozen initiates with that gift. And you... you saw clearly? You could distinguish the paths?"

"I think so. They were... distinct. Different."

"And you remained conscious throughout." The woman's voice was sharp, but there was respect beneath it. "Impressive. More than impressive." She made a note on her parchment. "The Order will have questions for you, Initiate. Many questions."

"I understand."

"I'm not sure you do." She looked up from her notes. "But you will."

They gave him new clothes—black robes, simple but well-made, with the symbol of the Eclipsed Veil embroidered at the collar. They gave him a room that was larger than his cell had been, with an actual bed and a window that looked out on an underground garden he hadn't known existed. They gave him a title—Initiate—and a set of responsibilities he didn't yet understand.

And they gave him a handler.

Lexi caught him in the hallway outside his new quarters, her arms crossed, her expression caught somewhere between resignation and irritation.

"You've been assigned to my care," she said without preamble. "For training, orientation, and assessment. I'm to report on your progress to the Elders directly."

"I didn't ask for—"

"I know you didn't." She cut him off. "I know you didn't ask for any of this. You stumbled into our world by accident, passed trials you shouldn't have been able to pass, and demonstrated abilities that haven't been seen in decades." Her voice was controlled, but Drevik could see the tension in her shoulders. "And now I'm responsible for you. For your training, your integration, your survival."

"I'm sorry." He meant it. "I know this isn't what you wanted."

"No. It isn't." She studied him for a long moment, and her expression softened fractionally. "But I don't have a choice. The Elders have decided you're valuable, and valuable things must be protected." She turned and started walking. "Come. There's much to discuss, and little time to discuss it."

He followed her through corridors that twisted and branched in ways that seemed impossible. The compound was larger than he'd realized—rooms branching off from rooms, staircases leading up and down, passages that appeared to double back on themselves. He tried to memorize the route, but after the third turn, he gave up.

"You're wondering why you," Lexi said without looking back. "Why you passed the trials. Why you can see futures that haven't happened yet."

"The thought occurred to me."

"Some people are born into the Order." She stopped at a door and turned to face him. "They grow up knowing our ways, learning our history, developing abilities that manifest gradually over years. Some people are recruited—identified through various means, brought in as adults, trained to serve specific purposes. And some..." She paused. "Some are found. They don't know what they are until they're tested. Their abilities have always been there, dormant, waiting for the right catalyst to awaken them."

"You think I'm one of those."

"I think you're something we haven't seen before." Her voice was careful. "Your abilities don't match the standard profiles. Your knowledge shouldn't exist. And your foresight..." She shook her head. "Foresight is rare. True, clear, conscious foresight is nearly unheard of. And yet you touched the sphere for three minutes and walked away."

"Is that significant?"

"The last person who touched the sphere for longer than two minutes died." Her green eyes held his. "The last person with foresight like yours was my grandmother. She could see the consequences of every choice, every action. She could predict the movements of our enemies and the failures of our allies. She was the most powerful seer the Order has seen in five generations."

"What happened to her?"

"She was killed." Lexi's voice was flat. "By someone who feared what she could see."

The words hung in the air between them. Drevik felt a chill that had nothing to do with the temperature of the corridor.

"I'll be careful," he said.

"You'll have to be." She opened the door and gestured him through. "Because right now, you're the most interesting thing that's happened to the Order in decades. And interesting things have a way of attracting dangerous attention."

He stepped past her into a room lined with books and artifacts, his mind already turning over the implications of everything she'd said. Lexi followed, closing the door behind them, and for the first time since he'd arrived, Drevik felt something that might have been hope.

He was an initiate now. He had a place, a purpose, a path forward.

But as Lexi began explaining the structure of the Order—the hierarchy, the factions, the delicate balance of power that kept the organization functional—he couldn't shake the feeling that he'd stepped into something far more complicated than he'd imagined.

The trials were over.

The real test was just beginning.

They brought him to his quarters as the compound settled into the quiet rhythms of late night. The corridors were emptier now, the examiners and observers having dispersed to their own spaces. Drevik walked between two senior initiates—quiet, formal young men who seemed to view him as a curiosity rather than a colleague.

"Your training begins tomorrow," one of them said as they reached his door. "Lexi Thornwood will collect you at dawn. I suggest you rest."

"I will."

The second initiate lingered after the first walked away. Younger than Drevik had initially thought, with close-set eyes and a nervous energy that radiated from every movement.

"Hey," he said, pitching his voice low. "Can I give you some advice? Something that might help you survive this place?"

Drevik turned. "Sure."

The initiate glanced down the corridor, confirming they were alone. "Watch your back." His voice dropped even lower. "Lexi's father killed the last guy who disappointed him."

Before Drevik could respond, the initiate was gone—sprinted down the corridor and around a corner before he could ask any of the questions suddenly crowding his mind.

He stood in the doorway of his new quarters, alone with the weight of the warning and the strange, uncertain future stretching out before him.

In the visions the sphere had shown him, he'd seen many versions of Lexi Thornwood. Enemy. Ally. Something else entirely—something that made his pulse quicken when he remembered it.

He'd also seen himself standing over graves.

He wasn't sure which future he was walking toward.

But he knew one thing for certain: in the Order of the Eclipsed Veil, nothing was exactly what it seemed.

And everyone had secrets worth killing for.
