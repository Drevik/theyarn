Core idea

Boundaries are the essential infrastructure of an ethical non-monogamous lifestyle. Contrary to the stereotype of the "indiscriminate slut," increasing your number of romantic and sexual points of contact requires higher levels of discrimination and structure, not less. You must operate under the fundamental principle that no one can own another person; you are solely responsible for determining your own needs and arranging to have them met. You cannot rely on a partner to intuit your requirements, nor can you live vicariously through them. Effective boundaries must be clear, strong, and flexible, but above all, they must be conscious.

Key concepts and terms

  • Boundary: A personal limit that defines where you end and another person begins. It is an internal standard of what you will do or allow within your own body and space. The authority for a boundary rests entirely with you.
  • Agreement: A negotiated, mutual understanding between two or more people regarding shared behaviors or expectations. Unlike a boundary, an agreement requires consent from all parties involved. The authority for an agreement is shared.
  • Rule: A unilateral imposition of control over another person’s behavior. Rules are often seen as attempts to "own" a partner and are generally considered unhealthy in ethical non-monogamy. The authority for a rule is improperly placed on someone else.
  • Ownership of Choice: The practice of accepting the full spectrum of consequences that follow your decisions. If you choose a specific action or relationship dynamic, you "own" the outcome rather than "billing" your partner (blaming them or making them responsible for the emotional fallout).
  • Discrimination: In this context, not in the sense of prejudice, but in the sense of discernment. It is the ability to use your boundaries to selectively choose which connections are healthy and which are not.

Techniques worth drilling

  1. The Authority Audit

Review your current relationship dynamics to identify the source of your expectations.

  • Step 1: List every "expectation" you currently hold regarding a partner's behavior (e.g., "They shouldn't see other people on weekends").
  • Step 2: Ask yourself: "Am I attempting to control them (Rule), or am I stating what I will do if this happens (Boundary)?"
  • Step 3: If you find a Rule, rewrite it as a Boundary. Instead of "You cannot go out on Friday," use "I will not participate in a relationship where Friday nights are reserved for others."
  1. The Responsibility Reclaim

Stop using "intuition" as a substitute for communication.

  • Step 1: Identify a recurring unmet need in your relationship.
  • Step 2: Trace the origin of the silence. Did you assume they would "just know"?
  • Step 3: Formulate a direct request. Move from "I wish they would..." to "I need [X], and I am asking for [Y]."
  • Step 4: Prepare to manage the outcome. If the answer is "no," you must decide how to handle that reality without blaming the partner.
  1. Consequence Mapping

Practice "Owning Your Choices" to prevent resentment.

  • Step 1: Identify a recent decision you made regarding a sexual or romantic encounter.
  • Step 2: List the specific emotional or logistical consequences that followed (e.g., jealousy, scheduling conflicts, fatigue).
  • Step 3: Verbally or mentally state: "I chose this path, and I am responsible for managing these consequences." Do not assign the "cost" of the choice to your partner.

Practice drill

The Three-Column Audit This week, carry a notebook or use a digital note-taking app. Create three distinct columns: My Boundaries, My Agreements, and My Wishes.

  • Step 1: Data Entry. Throughout the week, every time you feel a pang of jealousy, resentment, or confusion in a relationship, categorize the feeling into one of these columns.
  • Boundaries: "I will not allow sexual contact with people who haven't been tested."
  • Agreements: "We have agreed to text each other when we arrive home safely."
  • Wishes: "I wish they would spend more time with me on Tuesday nights."
  • Step 2: The Flagging Phase. At the end of the week, review your list and look for two specific errors:
  • The Unconsented Agreement: Flag every item in your "Agreements" column that you never actually sat down and negotiated/confirmed with the other person. These are actually just "unilateral rules" or "assumptions."
  • The Wish-as-Agreement: Flag every item in your "Agreements" column that is actually just a "Wish." If you are treating a desire as a binding contract without the other person's explicit "yes," you are creating a trap for both of you.
  • Step 3: Resolution. For every flagged item, you must either initiate a negotiation to turn it into a real Agreement or reclassify it as a Wish/Boundary.

Critiques and caveats

  • The Rigidity Trap: While boundaries must be "strong," they should not be "brittle." If a boundary is so inflexible that it prevents all growth or compromise, it may function more as a defensive wall than a healthy limit.
  • The Burden of Communication: The "Responsibility Reclaim" technique places a heavy cognitive load on you. It requires constant self-monitoring and the courage to initiate difficult conversations. Do not mistake "not communicating" for "maintaining a boundary."
  • Safety vs. Control: Always distinguish between a boundary for physical/emotional safety (e.g., sexual health, protection from abuse) and a boundary used to stifle a partner's autonomy.

Reading notes

This chapter serves as the structural foundation for the book's more complex discussions on communication and jealousy. While earlier chapters focus on the philosophy of freedom, "Boundaries" provides the practical mechanics required to prevent that freedom from collapsing into chaos or resentment.
