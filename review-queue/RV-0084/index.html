Voice Lab — Part 1: The Voice Fingerprint System

Why Characters Sound the Same

When every character in your story sounds alike, the problem isn't your imagination — it's your method.

Most character tools start with backstory: Where did they grow up? What's their trauma? What's their zodiac sign? These create rich characters in your head, but they don't create distinct voices on the page. Two characters can have wildly different backstories and still speak in the same smooth, grammatically correct, moderately articulate sentences.

Voice isn't backstory. Voice is behavior on the page.

Voice is the specific, observable linguistic choices a character makes every time they open their mouth:

  • The words they choose (and the ones they'd never use)
  • The length of their sentences (and how they vary them)
  • The rhythm of their speech (staccato, steady, meandering)
  • The topics they avoid (and how they dodge them)
  • The way they lie (everyone has a tell)

This system starts from the observable, not the imaginary. You don't need to know a character's entire history to build their voice. You need to make seven specific decisions about how they talk.

The 7 Dimensions of Voice

Every character's voice is defined by these seven dimensions. Two characters can share a dimension — but they can't share all seven and still sound different.

1. Vocabulary Tier

What level of words does this character use?

| Tier | Characteristics | Example | |---|---|---| | Simple | Short, common words. Sentences built from the 1,000 most-used English words. | "I don't know what to do. This is bad." | | Moderate | Everyday vocabulary. Some variety, nothing fancy. | "I'm not sure what to make of this. It's concerning." | | Elevated | Larger vocabulary. Enjoys precise or unusual words. | "I find the situation rather perplexing. It's most disconcerting." | | Specialized | Jargon-heavy. Uses terms from their profession, hobby, or subculture. | "The parameters are outside acceptable tolerance. We're looking at a cascading failure." |

Key: Most characters should default to one tier and occasionally shift for effect. A simple-tier character using an elevated word is more distinctive than an elevated-tier character using elevated words consistently.

2. Sentence Length

How long are this character's typical sentences?

| Pattern | Characteristics | When Used | |---|---|---| | Short | 1-8 words. Punchy. Direct. | Action scenes, angry characters, terse personalities | | Medium | 9-18 words. Standard. Balanced. | Most dialogue, most characters' default | | Long | 19+ words. Flowing. Complex. | Thoughtful characters, academics, ramblers | | Variable | Mixes short and long deliberately. | Experienced speakers, emotionally complex characters |

Key: Two characters can both use medium-length sentences, but one might vary more while the other stays consistent. That variation is part of their voice.

3. Rhythm Pattern

How does this character's speech flow?

| Rhythm | Characteristics | Analogy | |---|---|---| | Staccato | Short bursts. Clipped. Efficient. Stops and starts. | Machine gun | | Steady | Even pace. Predictable. Reliable. | Metronome | | Meandering | Long, winding. Gets to the point eventually. Or doesn't. | River | | Erratic | Unpredictable. Short then long. Fast then slow. | Heartbeat under stress |

Key: Rhythm is about the music of the voice, not the content. Two characters could say the same thing with completely different rhythms.

4. Emotional Default

What does this character sound like when they're not trying to sound like anything?

| Default | Characteristics | Underlying Vibe | |---|---|---| | Flat | Unemotional. Matters-of-fact. Low affect. | Calm, detached, or depressed | | Warm | Friendly. Inclusive. Uses "we" and "you" a lot. | Caring, social, or manipulative | | Sharp | Quick. Direct. Often sarcastic. | Intelligent, defensive, or aggressive | | Gentle | Soft. Qualifying. Hedging. "Maybe," "perhaps," "I think." | Kind, uncertain, or passive | | Intense | Passionate. Emphasized words. Strong declarations. | Excitable, driven, or overwhelming |

Key: A character's default isn't their only mode — it's their baseline. Everyone shifts under pressure. But the shift away from default is what makes dialogue interesting.

5. Verbal Tics

What words, phrases, or structures does this character overuse?

| Type | Examples | Effect | |---|---|---| | Filler words | "like," "you know," "I mean," "so," "well" | Casual, uncertain, or stalling | | Pet phrases | "Here's the thing," "At the end of the day," "Look—" | Catchphrase-ish, defines the character | | Sentence structures | Always starts with "So," always ends with a question, always uses "I guess" | Subtle but powerful voice marker | | Grammar patterns | Drops articles, uses double negatives, never uses contractions, always uses contractions | Class, education, regional markers |

Key: One verbal tic per character is enough. Two is memorable. Three is a caricature. Use sparingly.

6. Avoidance Patterns

What does this character NOT talk about — and how do they dodge it?

| Avoidance | Dodge Strategy | Example | |---|---|---| | Emotions | Deflects with humor, facts, or action | "How do I feel? I feel like we should keep moving." | | The past | Changes subject, gives a non-answer, or lies | "That's ancient history. What matters now is—" | | Vulnerability | Gets angry, gets logical, or flips the question | "Why are you asking me that?" | | Responsibility | Blames others, minimizes, or reframes | "It's not that simple. There were a lot of factors." | | Intimacy | Makes jokes, gives physical distance, over-talks | Long rambling answer that says nothing personal |

Key: What a character avoids is more distinctive than what they talk about. Two characters who are both funny sound similar. Two characters who are both funny but one avoids emotions while the other avoids vulnerability — those voices are distinct.

7. Lying Style

Everyone lies differently. How does this character lie?

| Style | Characteristics | Tell | |---|---|---| | Over-specific | Adds unnecessary detail to sound convincing | "I was at Joe's Bar on Fifth Street from exactly 9:15 to 10:40." | | Deflective | Answers a different question | "Where was I? Why are you asking about me?" | | Minimalist | Says as little as possible | "Out." | | Over-friendly | Becomes suddenly helpful or agreeable | "Of course! I'd be happy to help you look into that!" | | Honest pivot | Tells a truth that isn't the answer | "I was working late." (true — but not where they were) |

Key: A character's lying style is their voice under maximum pressure. It reveals what they do when they can't rely on their default patterns.

Building a Voice Fingerprint

For each major character, fill in one Voice Fingerprint Worksheet (next section). The worksheet captures all 7 dimensions in a one-page profile.

Then use the Dialogue Blind Test (worksheet 3): write the same line of dialogue in each character's voice. Remove the dialogue tags. Can you tell who's speaking?

If you can't tell, your voices aren't different enough. Go back to the dimensions and make them more distinct.

The Golden Rule of Voice

If you can't tell who's speaking without the dialogue tag, the voice isn't done yet.

Read your dialogue out loud. Cover the tags. If you can identify the speaker by their voice alone, you've built a distinct voice. If you can't, go back to the 7 dimensions and push them further apart.

Voice Lab — Part 2: The Speech Pattern Builder & Voice Testing

Building Voices from Scratch

If you're starting a new character, don't answer a backstory questionnaire. Make these seven decisions instead:

Step-by-Step Voice Construction

Step 1: Choose a Vocabulary Tier → Pick one: Simple, Moderate, Elevated, or Specialized → This is the character's default. They may shift up or down for effect, but they return to this level.

Step 2: Choose a Sentence Length Pattern → Pick one: Short, Medium, Long, or Variable → If Variable, decide their ratio: 60% short/40% long, or 30/70, etc.

Step 3: Choose a Rhythm → Pick one: Staccato, Steady, Meandering, or Erratic → This determines how their dialogue FEELS when read aloud.

Step 4: Choose an Emotional Default → Pick one: Flat, Warm, Sharp, Gentle, or Intense → This is their resting state. How do they sound when not under pressure?

Step 5: Choose ONE Verbal Tic → Pick one type: filler word, pet phrase, sentence structure, or grammar pattern → Just one. This is the audible signature that makes them recognizable.

Step 6: Choose ONE Avoidance Pattern → What do they NOT talk about? How do they dodge it? → This is what happens when the conversation gets uncomfortable.

Step 7: Choose a Lying Style → How do they lie? Over-specific, deflective, minimalist, over-friendly, or honest pivot? → This is their voice under maximum stress.

Making Voices Different

The most common voice problem isn't that individual voices are bad — it's that they're too similar.

The Voice Differentiation Matrix

List all your major characters. Fill in each dimension. Look for columns where every character has the same answer.

| Character | Vocab | Length | Rhythm | Default | Tic | Avoidance | Lie Style | |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---| | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | | |

Red flags:

  • More than 2 characters with the same vocabulary tier
  • More than 2 characters with the same rhythm
  • More than 2 characters with the same emotional default
  • Any two characters sharing 4+ dimensions

How to fix: Push voices apart on the dimensions that are too similar. Two characters can share 1-2 dimensions. They shouldn't share 3 or more.

The Contrast Principle

For every pair of characters, identify at least TWO dimensions where they're opposites:

| Character A | Dimension | Character B | |---|---|---| | Short sentences | Sentence Length | Long sentences | | Flat emotion | Emotional Default | Intense emotion | | Specialized jargon | Vocabulary Tier | Simple words |

Two contrasting dimensions is usually enough to make two characters sound distinct, even if they share the other five.

Testing Voices: The Dialogue Blind Test

How to Do the Blind Test

  1. Write the SAME line of dialogue in each major character's voice. The line should be something any character might say — a reaction to news, a greeting, an opinion.
  1. Test lines (use one or create your own):
  • "What do you mean, they're gone?"
  • "I don't think that's a good idea."
  • "We need to talk about what happened."
  • "Are you serious right now?"
  • "I knew this would happen eventually."
  1. Remove all dialogue tags and action beats. Just the words.
  1. Give the lines to someone who's read your work. Can they identify each speaker?
  1. If they can't, push the voices further apart on the dimensions that are too similar.

Example: One Line, Three Voices

The line: "What do you mean, they're gone?"

| Character | Vocabulary | Length | Rhythm | Default | Result | |---|---|---|---|---|---| | Marcus (soldier) | Simple | Short | Staccato | Flat | "Gone? Define gone." | | Elise (diplomat) | Elevated | Long | Meandering | Warm | "I'm not entirely sure I understand what you mean by gone, darling. Could you perhaps elaborate?" | | Tomás (street kid) | Simple | Variable | Erratic | Sharp | "Gone? Gone how — like left, or like dead? Don't just stand there, which is it?" |

Remove the names. Each line is instantly identifiable because the voices differ on 4 of 5 dimensions.

Common Voice Problems and Fixes

Problem: All Characters Sound Like the Author

Diagnosis: Same vocabulary tier, same rhythm, same emotional default across all characters. Fix: Build each character's Voice Fingerprint deliberately. Start by pushing them to the OPPOSITE of your natural writing voice on at least 2 dimensions. If you write long, elegant sentences, make one character speak in short, punchy bursts.

Problem: Characters Sound Like Genre Stereotypes

Diagnosis: You're using surface markers (accent, slang) instead of structural voice differences. Fix: Slang and accent are window dressing. The real voice comes from sentence length, rhythm, emotional default, and avoidance patterns. Build the structure first, add dialect last.

Problem: Antagonist and Protagonist Sound Similar

Diagnosis: The two most important characters share too many dimensions. Fix: Make them opposites on at least 3 dimensions. If the hero is warm and long-winded, the villain should be sharp and terse. Contrast creates conflict — even in dialogue.

Problem: Secondary Characters Blur Together

Diagnosis: You haven't built Voice Fingerprints for secondary characters. Fix: You don't need full fingerprints for minor characters, but give each one a single distinctive dimension: a unique tic, an opposite emotional default from the main cast, or a very different vocabulary tier. One strong choice is enough for a character who appears in 3-5 scenes.

Problem: AI-Assisted Drafts Sound Homogenized

Diagnosis: AI defaults to moderate vocabulary, medium sentence length, steady rhythm, and warm emotional default. Every character sounds like a helpful, articulate assistant. Fix: After an AI draft, run every character through the Voice Fingerprint. Push each character away from the AI default on at least 3 dimensions. Then rewrite each line of dialogue to match the fingerprint.

Product Brief: Voice Lab

Product ID: VOICE-LAB-004 Price: $29-39 Category: Writing & Publishing → Tools Format: PDF + printable worksheets (digital download) Target Audience: Fiction writers whose characters all sound the same — especially writers using AI for drafts, where voice homogenization is a known problem

Problem Statement

Readers can tell when characters sound alike. "Everyone talks the same" is one of the top complaints in fiction feedback — and it's the #1 tell of AI-assisted writing. AI generates dialogue in a consistent, polished voice regardless of which character is speaking.

Existing character tools give you questionnaires: favorite color, childhood trauma, zodiac sign. These create backstory, not voice. Knowing your character grew up in Montana doesn't tell you how they talk.

Nobody sells a systematic method for building distinct character voices — a tool that starts from speech patterns, vocabulary, rhythm, and perspective, not from backstory questionnaires.

Solution

Voice Lab is a character voice differentiation system with printable worksheets. Instead of "tell me about your character's childhood," it starts from the observable: how does this character sound on the page?

The Voice Fingerprint

Each character gets a Voice Fingerprint — a one-page profile that captures:

  • Vocabulary tier (simple, moderate, elevated, specialized)
  • Sentence length (short punchy, medium, long flowing)
  • Rhythm pattern (staccato, steady, meandering)
  • Emotional default (what they sound like when not acting)
  • Verbal tics (filler words, pet phrases, sentence structures)
  • What they avoid (topics, words, emotional registers)
  • How they lie (everyone has a tell)

Key Differentiator

This isn't a character questionnaire. It's a voice construction kit. You build a voice from specific, observable linguistic choices, then test it by writing the same line in multiple voices. If you can't tell who's speaking without the dialogue tag, the voices aren't different enough.
