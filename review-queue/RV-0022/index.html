Core idea

Sexual freedom is not merely the absence of rules, but the presence of active, structural safety. You cannot achieve true autonomy if your environment is defined by sexism or the threat of sexual violence. Because external legal systems are often unreliable or biased, you must take responsibility for building a culture of consent within your immediate community through active intervention and trauma-informed practices. Ultimately, the goal is to move away from "default" relationship structures—which are often inherited unconsciously—and toward intentional designs that reflect your actual values and desires.

Key concepts and terms

  • Culture of Consent: A proactive community standard where safety is maintained through shared norms rather than just individual boundaries. It requires believing disclosures, intervening in problematic behavior, and setting clear expectations for what is acceptable in your social circle.
  • - Clean Love: A concept derived from Buddhist non-attachment. It refers to loving others without the weight of possessiveness, desperation, or the need to control them. It is the practice of loving someone for who they are, rather than for how they serve your ego or provide security.
  • - Relationship Anarchy (RA): A framework that rejects the "one size fits all" approach to relationships. It involves deconstructing hierarchies and treating all connections (sexual, romantic, platonic) as unique, negotiable, and independent of traditional labels.
  • - Couple-centrism/Couple Privilege: The societal assumption that the romantic, monogamous dyad is the only "natural" or "stable" unit. This bias often marginalizes anyone in polyamorous, platonic, or non-hierarchical structures.
  • - Automatic Hierarchy: The tendency to grant "primary" partners more decision-making power, resources, or emotional priority by default, rather than through an explicit, negotiated agreement.
  • - Chosen Family: A support network of individuals who are not related by blood or marriage but who fulfill the emotional and social roles traditionally held by kin.

Techniques worth drilling

  1. The Intervention Protocol
  • Step 1: Identify the breach. Monitor your social environment for subtle signs of disrespect, sexism, or boundary-crossing (e.g., "negging," ignoring a "no," or coercive persistence).
  • Step 2: Direct intervention. If safe, address the behavior in the moment. Use "I" statements: "I find that comment disrespectful to [Name]," or "That behavior makes this space feel unsafe."
  • Step 3: Support the target. After an intervention, check in with the person affected. Practice the "believe first" rule: listen to their experience without interrogation or skepticism.
  • Step 4: Set the communal norm. Follow up with the group to reinforce that the behavior is not part of the community's shared values.
  1. Practicing Non-Attachment (Clean Love)
  • Step 1: Audit your triggers. Notice when you feel a spike in anxiety or anger due to a partner’s independence (e.g., they are spending time with someone else).
  • Step 2: Deconstruct the impulse. Ask yourself: "Am I reacting to a threat to my safety, or am I reacting to a threat to my control?"
  • Step 3: Reframe the connection. Consciously remind yourself that the person’s autonomy is a prerequisite for a healthy connection. Practice enjoying their presence without attempting to "anchor" them to you.
  1. The Label Deconstruction
  • Step 1: List your current roles. Write down every "label" you currently use (e.g., boyfriend, primary, best friend, sexual partner).
  • Step 2: Strip the expectations. For each label, list the "unspoken rules" you think come with it (e.g., "A boyfriend must spend every weekend with me").
  • Step 3: Renegotiate. Identify which rules are actually necessary for your satisfaction and which are just "defaults" you inherited from society. Prepare to communicate these new, stripped-down expectations to your partners.

Practice drill

The Structural Audit and Redesign

This week, you will perform a formal audit of your current relational architecture to identify where you are operating on autopilot.

  • Phase 1: The Map. Take a piece of paper. Draw a diagram of your current relationship structure. Use lines to represent connections between yourself and the people in your life (partners, muchness, friends, etc.).
  • Phase 2: The Audit. For every line you drew, mark it with one of two symbols:
  • [C] Chosen: You explicitly sat down, discussed, and agreed upon the rules, boundaries, and hierarchy of this connection.
  • [D] Default: You fell into this arrangement because it was the "standard" way things happen (e.g., you assumed you were exclusive because you've been dating for three months, or you assume a friend is "secondary" because they aren't a romantic partner).
  • Phase 3: The Redesign. Choose one connection marked with [D]. Redraw that specific connection using a completely different structure (e.g., if it was a default hierarchy, redraw it as an anarchic, non-hierarchical connection; if it was a default monogamous dyad, redraw it as part of a polycule).
  • Phase 4: The Requirement List. Below your new drawing, list exactly what this new structure would require of you. What new communication, what new boundaries, and what new level of emotional labor would this "alternative" demand?

Critiques and caveats

  • The Burden of Intervention: While the authors advocate for community intervention, be aware that intervening in social situations carries personal risk. You must weigh the necessity of the intervention against your own physical and emotional safety.
  • The Complexity of Non-Attachment: Practicing "Clean Love" or non-attachment is significantly more difficult in the face of actual trauma or abandonment. Do not use the concept of "non-attachment" to gaslight yourself or others out of valid fears regarding stability or security.
  • The Risk of Over-Negotiation: While moving away from "defaults" is vital, attempting to negotiate every single micro-interaction can lead to "decision fatigue" and can stifle the natural flow of intimacy.

Reading notes

These chapters serve as the transition from the "how-to" of sexual mechanics to the "why" of social architecture. While earlier sections focus on the logistics of polyamory, Chapters 6 and 7 demand that you examine the broader political and social frameworks—such as sexism, hierarchy, and heteronormativity—that shape your capacity for freedom.
