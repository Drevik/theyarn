Core idea

Chapter 3 transitions from theoretical science to personal application by providing a diagnostic tool to identify your specific attachment style. Instead of relying on vague self-perceptions, you use a structured questionnaire to measure your position on two fundamental axes of relational behavior. The goal is not to label yourself permanently, but to establish a baseline of your current attachment tendencies. By identifying where you fall on the spectrum of anxiety and avoidance, you gain the necessary data to begin targeted behavioral changes.

Key concepts and terms

  • Anxiety (Relationship Anxiety): The degree to which you worry about the stability of your relationship and the availability of your partner. High anxiety manifests as hyper-vigilance toward signs of rejection or loss of interest.
  • Avoidance (Attachment Avoidance): The degree to which you prioritize independence and self-reliance over intimacy. High avoidance manifests as a tendency to withdraw or create distance when a relationship becomes too emotionally demanding.
  • The Two-Dimensional Model: The framework that uses the intersection of anxiety and avoidance to categorize individuals into four distinct quadrants.
  • Secure Attachment: A state characterized by low anxiety and low avoidance. You are comfortable with intimacy and do not frequently worry about the relationship's survival.
  • Anxious Attachment: A state characterized by high anxiety and low avoidance. You crave high levels of intimacy and frequently monitor your partner for signs of withdrawal.
  • Avoidant Attachment: A state characterized by low anxiety and high avoidance. You value autonomy and often feel stifled or "smothered" by emotional closeness.
  • Anxious-Avoidant (Disorganized): A rare state characterized by high levels of both anxiety and avoidance. This often involves a cycle of seeking closeness and then retreating in fear.
  • Plasticity: The concept that while attachment styles are relatively stable over time, they are not fixed. You can shift your position on the axes through conscious effort and "earned security."

Techniques worth drilling

  1. The Two-Axis Scoring Method
  • Take the provided questionnaire (adapted from the ECR scale) and mark only the statements that are undeniably true for you.
  • Separate the statements into two distinct piles: those related to "Anxiety" (e.g., "I often worry that my partner will stop loving me") and those related to "Avoidance" (e.g., "I find it easy to be affectionate with my partner" — Note: In the scoring, you must track the direction of the sentiment).
  • Calculate your total "Anxiety Score" and your total "Avoidance Score."
  • Plot your coordinates on a mental or physical grid to determine which of the four quadrants you inhabit.
  1. Behavioral Verification (The "Audit" Technique)
  • Once you have a score, do not accept it at face value.
  • Identify three specific, recent interpersonal incidents from the last 14 days.
  • For each incident, categorize the "move" you made: Did you send a "check-in" text because you felt ignored (Anxious)? Did you skip a phone call because you felt pressured (Avoidant)? Did you stay calm and direct (Secure)?
  • Compare these three data points to your questionnaire score.
  1. The Discrepancy Re-Scoring Protocol
  • If your questionnaire score says "Secure" but your three recent behaviors show "Anxious" patterns, treat the questionnaire as a "false positive."
  • Review the statements you left blank. Ask yourself: "Did I leave this blank because it isn't true, or because I am afraid to admit it is true?"
  • Re-calculate your score based strictly on the behavioral evidence of the last two weeks.

Practice drill

The Weekly Attachment Log

For the next seven days, carry a small notebook or use a dedicated note app on your phone. Every time you experience a spike in "relational emotion"—this includes a moment of irritation, a pang of jealousy, a feeling of being smothered, or a moment of profound peace—record the following three entries:

  1. The Trigger: What happened? (e.g., "Partner didn't text back for 4 hours.")
  2. The Internal Thought: What did you tell yourself? (e.g., "They are losing interest in me.")
  3. The Move: What did you actually do? (e.g., "I checked their Instagram activity to see if they were online.")

At the end of the week, review your log. If your "Moves" consistently align with the Anxious or Avoidant quadrants, use this as your primary diagnostic tool for the week ahead. Do not focus on the feeling; focus on the move. The "move" is the only part of the equation that is actionable.

Critiques and caveats

  • The Identity Trap: Do not use your score as a fixed identity (e.g., "I am an Anxious person"). This creates a self-fulfilling prophecy that prevents growth. Treat the score as a "current weather report," not a "climate map."
  • The Threat Bias: Your self-image is often more "Secure" than your actual behavior under pressure. When you feel threatened or rejected, your attachment style will manifest in your actions regardless of what you think you believe. Always prioritize analyzing your actions under stress over your intentions during calm periods.
  • Ambiguity Warning: If your scores are borderline or ambiguous, do not over-analyze the questionnaire. Instead, look for patterns of "protest behavior" (Anxious) or "deactivating strategies" (Avoidant) in your daily interactions.

Reading notes

This chapter serves as the diagnostic foundation for the entire book. It moves the reader from understanding the science of attachment to performing a self-audit, setting the stage for the subsequent chapters which focus on identifying compatible partners and implementing specific communication strategies to move toward security.
