Core idea

Jealousy is not a signal of your partner’s betrayal or a flaw in your relationship structure; it is a map of your own internal fears and unmet needs. When you feel the sting of jealousy, you are experiencing a physiological and emotional reaction to a perceived threat to your security. The goal is not to eliminate the feeling—which is often impossible—but to change your relationship with it. By treating jealousy as data rather than a directive, you can move from reactive, destructive behaviors to proactive, self-sovereign communication.

Key concepts and terms

  • The Map of Fear: The concept that jealousy serves as a diagnostic tool. The specific "flavor" of your jealousy points directly to which of your vulnerabilities (abandonment, inadequacy, loss of autonomy) is currently being triggered.
  • Unlearning/Disempowering Jealousy: The cognitive process of separating the feeling (which is valid and real) from the interpretation (which is often a projection or a fiction). You accept the emotion without accepting the narrative that your partner is doing something "wrong."
  • Whitewater Rafting: A metaphor for navigating high-intensity emotional spikes. Instead of trying to stop the "river" (the emotion) or letting it capsize you (spiraling into panic), you learn to stay in the boat, use your paddle (breathing/grounding), and ride the wave until it reaches calmer water.
  • The Ick: The specific, often visceral, intrusive images or thoughts that cause distress. These are "pure" data points because they are generated by your own mind, not by your partner's actual actions.
  • Negativity Bias: The biological tendency for the human brain to weigh bad news or threatening thoughts more heavily than good news. In polyamory, this manifests as focusing on a partner's date with someone else while ignoring the stability of the primary bond.
  • Testing: A destructive communication habit where you create "traps" or withhold information to see if your partner will "prove" their loyalty. This is the opposite of healthy reassurance.

Techniques worth drilling

  1. The "Go for the Ick" Deconstruction

When an intrusive, upsetting image enters your mind, do not push it away or try to "be chill." Instead, lean into it.

  • Step 1: Identify the specific, granular image that is bothering you (e.g., "I am imagining them touching X part of their body").
  • Step 2: Trace the image to the underlying fear (e.g., "This image makes me feel replaceable").
  • Step 3: Acknowledge the "Blank Space" realization: Recognize that since you are not present in that moment, your brain is simply filling the vacuum with your own internal material. The image is your own creation; it is not a documentary of reality.
  1. The Self-Soothe Protocol (Baby Yourself)

When jealousy reaches a level of physiological panic, you cannot "reason" your way out of it using logic alone. You must address the nervous system.

  • Step 1: Identify the "Inner Child" state. Recognize that the part of you feeling jealous is a frightened, vulnerable version of yourself.
  • Step 2: Apply physical comfort. Use weighted blankets, warm tea, deep breathing, or rhythmic movement to signal safety to your body.

s* Step 3: "Tough it out" without outsourcing the crisis. Practice enduring the discomfort without making your partner responsible for "fixing" your panic. Your goal is to regulate yourself so you can eventually discuss the issue from a place of stability.

  1. The Reassurance Pivot

Replace "Testing" with "Direct Requesting."

  • Step 1: Catch yourself in a "test" (e.g., waiting to see if they text you first to prove they care).
  • Step 2: Formulate a direct, vulnerable request (e.g., "I am feeling a bit insecure right now; could you send me a quick text when you get home?").
  • Step 3: Deliver the request without accusation. Use "I" statements to ensure the focus remains on your needs rather than their perceived failures.
  1. The "Treasures" Counterbalance

To combat Negativity Bias, you must manually override your brain's tendency to fixate on threats.

  • Step 1: Maintain a "Treasures" list—a literal or mental inventory of the strengths, certainties, and joys in your relationship.
  • Step 2: When a jealousy spike occurs, force a mental rotation. Actively recall three specific, recent moments of connection or security to re-center your perspective.

Practice drill

The 24-Hour Trigger Audit

Identify one recent instance where you felt a spike of jealousy, resentment, or insecurity. Within the next 24 hours, write down exactly five lines following this template. Do not skip any lines; the goal is structural discipline.

  1. The Image: (Describe the specific intrusive thought or mental picture that occurred.)
  2. The Fear: (Identify the underlying vulnerability: abandonment, inadequacy, loss of control, etc.)
  3. The Need: (What does this fear tell you that you are currently lacking in your life or relationship?)
  4. The Request: (A direct, non-accusatory request you can make to your partner or an action you can take.)
  5. The Self-Action: (One specific way you will "baby yourself" or soothe your own nervous system.)

Constraint: Line 4 (The Request) must be communicated to the relevant party within 24 hours of completing this drill.

Critiques and caveats

  • The Risk of Internalization: While "Toughing it out" is vital for preventing emotional contagion, be careful not to use it as an excuse to suppress legitimate relationship issues. If a pattern of behavior is actually causing harm, it is a boundary violation, not just "jealousy."
  • The "Self-Soothe" Trap: Self-soothing should be used to regulate your nervous system so you can communicate, not as a way to avoid ever bringing up your needs. If you only ever "baby yourself," you are not building a partnership; you are practicing isolation.
  • Safety Note: If jealousy is accompanied by controlling behaviors, monitoring of devices, or isolation of a partner, these are signs of domestic abuse, not "navigating the waves" of polyamory. The techniques in this section assume a foundation of established trust and autonomy.

Reading notes

This section serves as the emotional toolkit for the structural frameworks discussed in earlier chapters. While the rest of the book provides the "rules of the road" (boundaries, communication, scheduling), "Roadmaps through Jealousy" provides the emergency protocols for when the engine overheats.
