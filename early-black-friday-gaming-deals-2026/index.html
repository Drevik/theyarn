Early Black Friday Gaming Deals 2026: 5 Worth Buying (and 1 to Skip)

October 31, 2026 · 5 deals tracked + 1 skip · By Marcus Chen

Black Friday 2026 lands on November 27, and the early deals are already leaking out like steam under a door. I've been timing my hardware buying around this weekend since a Pentium II build in 1998 taught me that patience is a spec. Most of what calls itself an early Black Friday deal is a costume. Some of it is genuinely the cheapest something has ever been. The difference is measurable, and I measured it.

Here's the shape of this year's market, because it explains every price below. Games are normal: publishers cut the year's big titles 30 to 60 percent early, Battlefield 6 digital sits at a real discount, and the usual sale-calendar rhythm is intact. Peripherals are generous, with headsets and controllers hitting tracked lows weeks before the actual event. Components are a disaster. The memory shortage that pumped GPUs 50 to 60 percent over MSRP since summer has now chewed through DRAM and NAND: a Samsung 990 PRO 1TB tracked at $259.99 on October 27, 2026, a drive whose all-time low was $59.99. SSDs are not on sale. SSDs are the sale's victim.

This is the deal I'd hand to someone who let me spend their money, and it wins on the ratio I actually care about: dollars per hour of use. A $49 headset that survives four years costs about a dollar a month, and the Cloud III is comfortable enough that four years is plausible. The 53mm angled drivers are retuned from the Cloud II generation and push dialogue forward instead of burying it under bass, which matters more in co-op lobbies than any RGB strip. The 10mm microphone has a mute LED you can see from your own face, a small thing that saves you from the classic streamer humiliation of talking to a muted team for ten minutes.

The wiring is the feature. Wired headsets lost a popularity contest, not a quality one. There's no battery to swell, no dongle to lose in a couch, no 2.4GHz dropout during a raid boss, and the 3.5mm fallback means it plays nice with every console ever made. DTS Headphone:X spatial audio processes on PC and gives decent footstep placement in shooters; console players get clean stereo instead, which is an honest limitation of the price, not a defect.

The honest downsides. It's wired, first and foremost, and if your desk is across the room from a TV, that cable is a dealbreaker, so buy the wireless Cloud III on a real sale instead. The microphone sounds clear but thin next to a dedicated USB mic; it's a chat tool, not a broadcast tool. The clamp force is firm for the first week before the headband relaxes, and glasses wearers will feel it during long sessions. The white colorway I'm linking shows grime on the earcups over a year of use. None of these move the price-per-hour math. At $49 this is the cheapest good audio you can attach to a head this holiday, and if it's under $55 on Black Friday itself, that's still a buy.

Controllers are where the industry tests how dumb it can get. First-party pads cost $70 to $200, drift inside the warranty window, and treat Hall effect magnets like forbidden technology. Then a company that made its name on retro pads ships drift-proof Hall sticks, Hall triggers, 1,000Hz polling, and rear buttons for $29.99, and discounts it to $23.99 every couple of months. The Ultimate 2C is the most price-per-performance object in all of gaming hardware right now, and I own one. The sticks feel tight, the triggers have a proper tension curve for throttle control in racing games, and the two back buttons carry paddles' most useful 20 percent for a tenth of the price.

Who it's for: PC players who want a second pad for couch play, handheld owners who chew through sticks, parents buying a kid's first controller who don't want to rebuy it in eight months. The 1,000Hz polling is real but unnecessary; even 250Hz outruns human hands in every game short of competitive aim trainers, and this pad's latency sits well inside what most players can perceive.

What you give up, stated plainly. This ASIN is the Windows and Android model: it connects to PC over 2.4GHz or wired and to Android over Bluetooth, with no Bluetooth mode for PC and no Switch support, so console households need the separate Bluetooth version of the same controller. There's no 3.5mm headphone jack, no motion controls on the PC build, and no charging dock, so it charges over USB cable like an animal. The face buttons are membrane-ish where first-party pads click. The RGB ring around the sticks is exactly the kind of light-up garnish I bill as a tax on the credulous, though it can be dimmed. None of that justifies tripling the price for a first-party pad unless you specifically need Switch or PlayStation functionality, and if you do, the Switch 2C Bluetooth version at $23.99 is the same physics for the same money.

The NAND shortage is a supply allocation problem tied to AI datacenter demand, it has already shown up in DRAM pricing, and holiday promotions cannot discount what the factory isn't producing at volume.

My honest guidance, since refusing to buy is only useful if you know what to do instead. If your current drive works, keep using it; game installs are large but survivable, and uninstalling is free. If you genuinely need storage for a new build, buy the 2TB tier, which currently runs meaningfully cheaper per gigabyte than 1TB, around $0.15 per GB versus $0.25, and accept that you're overpaying versus history on any tier. If a specific game demands a faster drive and your machine boots from spinning rust, buy the smallest drive that solves it. The only correct move is patience: wafer capacity is being added, and when the shortage breaks, these drives will fall faster than they rose. I've watched three memory cycles since 1998 and they all end the same way, with warehouses full of yesterday's crisis at 40 percent of peak price. How to Play the Two Weeks Before Black Friday

The pattern from the last decade of watching this weekend: deals published before mid-November are a mix of genuine lows and theater, and the genuine lows are usually peripherals. Headsets, mice, and controllers have thin margins and stiff competition, so retailers front-load their best prices to grab gift shoppers. Games get their deepest cuts the week of the event itself, when publishers coordinate storefront sales. Hardware with silicon in it, GPUs, SSDs, RAM, is hostage to the shortage this year and the sale is a mirage; the 990 PRO's chart is the evidence.

So the plan I'd run with $150: buy the Cloud III if the number under $55 holds, grab the 2C whenever its card shows $23.99, and let the game cart sit until November 25. Check the tracker charts, not the banners. And if Amazon's listing for Battlefield 6 dips to $35 before the holiday, that's last year's number reprinting; take it and be done.
