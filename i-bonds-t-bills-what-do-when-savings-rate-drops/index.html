I-Bonds and T-Bills in 2026: What to Do When Your Savings Rate Drops

Updated September 2026

I-Bonds and T-Bills in 2026: What to Do When Your Savings Rate Drops

If you opened a high-yield savings account in 2024, you were probably earning 5% or more. By mid-2026, most of those rates have dropped below 4%. The Fed started cutting, and HYSAs followed within weeks. That money sitting in your savings account is still safe, but it's not working as hard as it was.

Two Treasury products, I-Bonds and Treasury bills, still pay more than most savings accounts right now, and they come with tax advantages HYSAs don't have. The mechanics are straightforward. The hard part is picking which one fits your situation, because they lock up your money differently and the wrong choice leaves you stuck when you need cash.

How I-Bonds work

I-Bonds are savings bonds issued by the US Treasury that earn interest based on two components: a fixed rate set at purchase, and a variable rate that tracks inflation (measured by the CPI-U). The two rates combine into a single composite rate that adjusts every six months.

As of the May 2026 issuance, the fixed rate is 1.3% and the semiannual inflation rate is 1.93%, producing a composite rate of roughly 5.17%. That rate will change again in November 2026 when the Treasury updates the inflation component.

The catch is access. You cannot cash an I-Bond for the first 12 months after buying it. If you sell between year one and year five, you forfeit the last three months of interest. After five years, there's no penalty.

You also can't buy more than $10,000 in I-Bonds per person per calendar year through TreasuryDirect. You can get an additional $5,000 using your tax refund, but you have to file Form 8888 with your return and direct that portion to I-Bonds specifically.

Here's what that looks like in practice. Say you buy $10,000 in I-Bonds in July 2026 at a 5.17% composite rate. You earn roughly $517 in interest over the first year, but you can't touch that money until July 2027. If rates drop before then, your I-Bond rate adjusts at the next reset, which is both the advantage (you benefit from inflation adjustments) and the risk (your fixed rate component is locked in for the life of the bond, currently 30 years).

How Treasury bills work

Treasury bills, or T-bills, are short-term debt the US government sells at a discount. You buy a 4-week bill for less than face value, and the government pays you the full amount at maturity. The difference is your interest.

T-bills come in four maturities: 4 weeks, 8 weeks, 13 weeks, and 26 weeks. Right now, the 26-week T-bill yields around 4.3%, and the 4-week bill is closer to 4.1%. These rates change weekly based on auction results, but they've stayed above HYSA rates in 2026 because the market expects further Fed cuts.

The advantage over HYSAs is state and local tax exemption. If you live in a high-tax state like California (13.3% top rate) or New York (10.9%), a 4.3% T-bill yield is equivalent to roughly 5% from a savings account once you factor in the state tax savings. For someone in the 37% federal bracket living in California, the tax-equivalent yield of a T-bill at 4.3% is around 5.6%. That math alone can make T-bills worth it.

You can buy T-bills through TreasuryDirect or through most brokerages. Fidelity and Schwab both offer commission-free Treasury trading with minimums as low as $100. Brokerage accounts also let you build a T-bill ladder, where you buy bills maturing at regular intervals so some cash is always coming due. TreasuryDirect works fine for a single purchase but has a clunky interface and no secondary market, meaning you can't sell early if you need the money before maturity.

Which one to pick

It depends on your timeline and your tax situation.

If you're saving for something specific in the next year or two, like a down payment or a car, T-bills are the better fit. You pick a maturity that lines up with when you need the cash, you know your return upfront, and you can build a ladder so money is always maturing. The state tax exemption is a bonus if you live in a high-tax state.

If you're parking money you won't need for at least a year, I-Bonds are worth a look. The inflation protection means your real return stays positive even if inflation spikes. The $10,000 annual cap limits how much you can put in, but for a chunk of your safe money, the inflation adjustment beats watching your HYSA rate drift downward.

Some people use both. Put the emergency fund in T-bills with staggered maturities, and buy $10,000 in I-Bonds each January as a long-term inflation hedge. This isn't an either-or decision. The two products solve different problems.

Where to buy

You have three main options.

TreasuryDirect is the official government portal. You can buy I-Bonds and T-bills there directly, with no fees. The interface feels like it was built in 2002 and hasn't been updated since, but it works. Open a TreasuryDirect account if you want the no-intermediary route.

If you want more flexibility, Fidelity offers commission-free trading on all Treasury securities with a $100 minimum. You can build ladders, sell on the secondary market if you need out early, and manage everything alongside your other investments. Open a Fidelity account.

Schwab has similar terms and includes Treasury ladder tools that automate the buying schedule, which saves you from manually placing orders every few weeks.

For I-Bonds you plan to hold until maturity, TreasuryDirect is fine. For T-bills, especially if you want to build a ladder or might need to sell early, use Fidelity or Schwab.

The actual steps

  • Decide how much you're moving. Only move money you won't need immediately. T-bills tie up cash until maturity. I-Bonds lock it up for a year.
  • Check the current rates. TreasuryDirect posts I-Bond rates every May and November. T-bill auction results are published weekly. The rates I cited above will change, so check before you buy.
  • Buy through a brokerage if you want flexibility. TreasuryDirect is fine for I-Bonds you plan to hold, but for T-bills, a Fidelity or Schwab account lets you sell early on the secondary market, which matters if your plans change.
  • Set up a ladder if you're using T-bills. Buy a 4-week, 8-week, and 13-week bill at the same time. When the 4-week bill matures, roll it into a new 13-week bill. This way, cash is maturing every month.

    • Don't overthink this. If your HYSA dropped from 5% to 3.8% and you live in a state with income tax, moving some of your safe money into T-bills takes maybe 20 minutes and saves you hundreds in state taxes per year. If you have money you can lock up for 12+ months, I-Bonds give you inflation protection that no savings account offers. Neither product is exciting, but that's the point.
