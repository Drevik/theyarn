Steam Deck Alternative: Four Deals Under $100 That Beat a $789 Handheld

October 24, 2026 · 4 deals tracked · By Marcus Chen

On May 27, 2026, Valve did something I've watched GPU makers do for two years: they blamed the memory market. The Steam Deck OLED 512GB jumped from $549 to $789 overnight. The 1TB went from $649 to $949. Same hardware, same screen, same chip, a 43% hike because DRAM and NAND costs went vertical and Valve decided to pass them through whole. Every handheld maker followed within weeks. The used market followed too, with 512GB OLEDs flipping for $600 to $720 on eBay, often out of warranty. There is no version of this where you win by panic-buying the console.

But here's the thing nobody says out loud in the panic threads. The deal market for games didn't hike with the hardware. Discs that are two years into their lifecycle are sitting at all-time lows right now, and the resale market undercuts even those. So the honest question isn't "can I afford a Steam Deck in 2026?" It's "what does my dollar actually buy?" A $789 Deck played 100 hours costs you $7.90 an hour before any game purchase. A $47.79 Elden Ring disc with the expansion, played on a console you already own, costs 48 cents an hour. That gap is the entire article.

Method first, because deal roundups rot fast. Every price below comes from Amazon price-tracker records for the exact ASIN I'm linking, pulled the week of October 24, 2026, with the recording date shown where the tracker recorded it. If a buybox has moved since, the tracker's chart shows it before your cart does. I don't recycle other sites' numbers, and neither should you.

This is the clearest value in gaming right now, and it's sitting in full view. FromSoftware sells the Shadow of the Erdtree expansion digitally for $39.99. Separately, a physical edition bundles the base game and that expansion on a single disc, and it's been changing hands between $39.99 and $47.79 all month. At $47.79 you're paying $7.80 over the base game's $39.99 list price for one of the biggest expansions in RPG history, a landmass that took players longer to finish than most full games. At the $39.99 dip, the expansion is free with your base game. Either number is a win; the disc is the cheaper container.

The practical details get ignored in most roundups. The edition includes the complete base game on the disc, so this works as a first Elden Ring purchase, fresh save, no DLC code to redeem. It needs a PS5 model with a disc drive, so if you own the Digital Edition, this listing isn't for you and I won't pretend otherwise. Co-op and invasion play need a PS Plus subscription, though the expansion is fully playable solo offline. And you should know what this game asks of you: it will kill you for hours and never apologize.

Downsides, honestly given. The price flickers, so if you catch it at $47.79 a week after a $39.99 dip, set an alert and wait a beat. Buying this when you already own the base game on disc is the one clearly wrong move; the standalone digital DLC, bought on sale, undercuts it. Fromsoft's older engine hides loading screens aggressively on performance mode and the image quality betrays the 2019 build if you stand still long enough to notice. None of that changes the math: a hundred hours of top-tier content for less than the cost of the DLC code alone.

The annual Call of Duty cycle is a machine that prints discount opportunities, and this is the trough of the curve. Black Ops 6 shipped at $69.99 in October 2024, and two years later Amazon sells it retail-new at $29.99. I'll go further than most roundups on this one: used and resale trackers currently list complete copies around $20-25, which means a patient buyer can undercut the "deal" price entirely. The campaign is complete on the disc, no download gates for the story, and the multiplayer lobbies are still busy because the biggest shooter audience on Earth simply didn't all leave at once.

Why buy the older game when Black Ops 7 is on shelves? Because none of what makes Black Ops 6 fun got removed when its sequel launched. The movement system, the map design, the round-based Zombies content people actually return for; all of it shipped, all of it patched for two years, and none of it got worse because a new box appeared at full price. Meanwhile Black Ops 7's own resale listings are already sliding toward the mid-$50s at other retailers, weeks after launch. The ladder always falls. The only decision is which rung you buy on.

Downsides, plainly. The install is a storage event; between the base game and its mode packs it will evict two other games from your console SSD, so clear the shelf before you buy. Always-online is baked into every mode including the campaign, so a downed authentication server takes the whole disc hostage, and that's a publisher choice I resent paying for. Server matchmaking leans hard on skill-based balancing, which competitive players read as a rubber band and casual players read as fairness. And if you play mostly Warzone, know that the battle royale runs on its own update clock and this disc is increasingly just a key for it.

Helldivers 2 is the live-service cautionary tale that worked. Arrowhead launched it in early 2024, weathered a genuinely rough patch of community backlash, and then did the thing publishers never do: they fixed their mistakes and kept shipping content without walling it behind battle passes. Two years on, the tracker recorded $29.87 on October 19, about $10 under the physical list price and within a few dollars of its deepest sale prices. For a four-player bug-and-robot shooting gallery with this much personality, that's the price band where I stop writing and start linking.

The pitch in one line: it's the only co-op shooter where the funniest thing that can happen is winning. Friendly fire is always on, orbital strikes land on teammates with metronomic regularity, and the stratagem system of calling in support while a Charger chases you makes every mission a story you'll retell in voice chat. The content cadence has held up; new enemies, weapons, and mission types have arrived across two years of patches. At $29.87 the cost per evening-of-laughter beats every streaming subscription I pay for.

Downsides, honestly given. Playing with strangers needs a PS Plus subscription, so that $29.87 presumes a subscription you may already carry; solo play exists and works but the design clearly leans on squads. Mission structure repeats; you will defend the same four objectives on reskinned planets until the variety patches land, and some months they're sparser than others. Performance took a visible dent during heavier patches, which Arrowhead has been slowly clawing back. And the game's satire is funnier if you don't think too hard about how much it's costing you in PS Plus renewals over five years.

I said in the PC deals guide I wrote last week that I was refusing all storage until the memory market behaves. I'm making one exception, and the reason is the price chart. This card launched around $100, climbed to roughly $200 during the shortage, and has been sitting near $185. On October 22 it dropped to $99.99 at Amazon, tracked as the best price since the hikes began. At $0.195 per gigabyte it matches the per-gigabyte math of SSDs before the spike, which is a sentence of sanity in a quarter that has otherwise lost the plot.

The argument for capacity is the Switch 2's own numbers. The console ships with 256GB internal, and a single modern port eats a double-digit percentage of it; Final Fantasy XIV alone eats 117GB on Switch 2. Game-key cards mean re-downloads and cartridge juggling, and a 512GB Express card turns the whole conversation into "download what you want." microSD Express is the card class the Switch 2 slot reads at speed, which is why this costs what it costs; games load noticeably faster from Express cards than the old UHS-I class. Samsung fabs its own NAND, so unlike the generic card aisle there's no mystery silicon in the package.

Downsides, honestly given. This is a Switch 2 purchase, full stop. In a Steam Deck or an original Switch it runs at UHS-I speed, and you'd pay an Express premium to be capped at old speeds; buy a plain UHS-I card there instead, or for the Deck, an M.2 2230 SSD. The 512GB write tier is the slower sibling; Samsung's own 1TB Express cards are rated for higher sustained writes, so heavy-traffic installs (the kind that write and rewrite constantly) won't see the theoretical top speeds. And the broader 1TB Express market is still mispriced, near $350 for some licensed cards, which is console-money for storage. If you're within 150GB of a full internal drive, buy now; if you're at 100GB used and playing indies, a Black Friday dip of $10-15 is plausible, though stock at these prices has sold through fast.

What I'm Still Refusing at These Prices

The new Steam Deck OLED, for now. At $789 the 512GB is priced like a mid-range gaming laptop with a smaller screen and a locked-down store, and at $949 the 1TB asks console-buyer money for NAND that cost Valve a fraction of that when the Deck launched. Valve has said restocks carry the hiked price, and a supply-driven hike doesn't politely reverse itself for Black Friday. The used route at $600-720 forfeits the warranty on a device whose thumbsticks and fans do wear. If portability is genuinely the thing you need, budget for it deliberately next year when the memory cycle eventually turns. Until then, the games on this list cost one-third of a Deck and run on hardware in your house already.

The 1TB microSD Express tier, for another one. Licensed 1TB Express cards near $350 are the worst value in storage right now, worse per gigabyte than the consoles they plug into. Two 512GB cards at $100 each, swapped or split across machines, beat one $350 card with money left over. Watch that tier and don't buy the first discount you see.

And the carryover refusals from last week's guide still stand: GPUs running 70% over MSRP with a 5060 Ti 16GB at $730-820, 32GB DDR5 kits at $460-560, flagship 2TB NVMe drives near $384 to $390 when the same Samsung 990 Pro 2TB hit $119.99 in November 2023. Nothing has improved because banners said PRO, though prebuilt towers remain the one PC path where the math still works. The pattern behind every refusal is the same: anything carrying DRAM or NAND into 2026 can wait. It's the stuff that doesn't, games and cards that slot in, that's on sale.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a bundle of games really a better Steam Deck alternative in 2026?

On hours per dollar, yes. The four picks here total $207.64 against $789 for the 512GB OLED, and each one runs on hardware you may already own. A handheld only wins when portability itself is the thing you're buying. If you already game at a desk or on a couch, the $580 difference buys a library, storage, and two years of co-op nights.

Why buy Black Ops 6 when Black Ops 7 is out?

Publishers discount the prior entry faster than the community leaves it. Black Ops 6 is $29.99 new with complete copies reselling near $20-25, its lobbies are still full, and the campaign sits on the disc. Sequels move player counts at the margins, not wholesale, so the older game at 57% off is the value rung on the ladder.

Why is the Elden Ring DLC cheaper on disc than as a digital code?

Because the publisher prices the standalone DLC at $39.99 while the physical edition, base game plus expansion on one disc, lands between $39.99 and $47.79 on sale. You're trading plastic and shelf space for the base game bundled underneath. If you already own the base game, the standalone digital DLC on sale is the smarter buy instead.

Should I wait for Black Friday instead of buying now?

Split the decision. Games flicker: Black Ops 6 and Helldivers 2 have touched $20-25 in Black Friday weeks past, so waiting saves $5-10 per title if your backlog allows. Storage is different: the Samsung card's $99.99 is already the best price since the memory hikes, and deal stock sells through. Deck restocks carry the hiked price either way.

Can the Samsung P9 Express card go in a Steam Deck or original Switch?

It fits and it will work, at slowed UHS-I speed, which is the worst of both worlds. Both machines cap microSD at roughly 100 MB/s, so an Express card buys nothing there. For a Steam Deck, add an M.2 2230 SSD; for an original Switch, a plain UHS-I card. The P9's value lives entirely in the Switch 2's Express slot.
