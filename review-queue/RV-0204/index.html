The kitchen of the Fold did not smell of bread or rosemary. It smelled of ozone and cold iron, the sharp, electric scent of a seam being pulled tight by a hand that did indeed care for the fabric, but only to tear it.

Halla stood by the long, scarred oak table. She did not look at the windows. She could not. To look at the windows was to acknowledge that the late-afternoon light of the walled garden—the amber glow that had sustained the household for generations—was gone. In its place was the Unmaking. Behind the thick, enchanted glass, the Nought pressed against the world like a tide of grey, hungry ash. It was a static so profound it seemed to vibrate the very marrow of her bones, a visual roar of nothingness that promised to erase everything it touched.

She kept her hands busy. It was the only way to survive the silence. A cook’ even more so; a cook’s hands must always be occupied with the tactile, the measurable, the tangible, or the mind would surely succumb to the rot of the void. She was peeling potatoes, the skins falling in long, pale, translucent curls into a ceramic bowl of water. The knife was a heavy thing, forged from tempered steel, and its edge was wicked. The rhythm was steady: slice, peel, drop. Slice, peel, drop.

If she could maintain the rhythm, she could trick the house. If the rhythm remained unbroken, the house might believe the reality of it was still real.

"The perimeter is thinning," Mira said.

Mira did not enter the room so much as she occupied the space within it. She drifted in from the direction of the Bureau’s archives, her silhouette framed by the dim, flickering light of the corridor. Her fingers were stained with the indelible, frantic ink of a dozen dying ledgers, and her eyes were wide, rimmed with the red exhaustion of a scholar watching her life’s work dissolve into nonsense. She did not look at the windows either. Instead, her gaze was fixed on the table, specifically on the way the salt cellar had shifted two inches to the left, despite no hand touching it.

"The locks are holding," Halla said. Her voice was a flat, defensive wall. She did not look up from the potato. "The latches are greased. The hinges are heavy. I checked them myself this morning."

"The hinges are irrelevant if the door forgets how to be a door," Mira said. She sat heavily in the chair opposite the table, the wood creaking under her weight. She began to tap a rhythm on the tabletop—a precise, staccato beat that mimicked the ticking of the grandfather clock in the hall, but it lacked the soul of time. It was a mechanical, hollow sound. "The Nought isn't trying to break in, Halla. It’s not a siege. It is trying to make it so that 'in' and 'out' no longer exist. It is turning the geometry of the Fold inside out. It is folding the inside into the outside until there is no distinction left."

Halla pushed a potato slice into the bowl with a forceful splash. "Then we will be inside. We will stay here, behind the stone and the warding, and that will be enough. We have food. We have warmth. We have the hearth."

"It won't be enough for him."

The voice came from the threshold, and the temperature in the kitchen plummeted.

Drevik did not walk into the room; he arrived, a sudden solidification of the atmosphere. The tension of the Unmaking—that high, thin, psychic scream of vanishing reality—did not vanish when he entered, but it found a new, terrifying center. He brought with him the crushing weight of the Threshold, a gravitational pull that seemed to draw the loose scraps of the room toward him.

He was dressed in his full liturgical vestments, the deep, bruised purple of a dying twilight. The gold embroidery on his high collar caught the dim, guttering light of the hearth, casting sharp, frantic glimmers against the encroaching grey. His white-ceramic mask was fixed and unreadable, the gold accents gleaming with a cold, clinical light, and the crystals embedded in his mitre drifted in slow, silent, impossible orbits around his head, as if gravity had become a mere suggestion.

He looked exhausted. It was a fatigue that transcended the physical, a weariness that lived in the very way he gripped the void staff. The orb at its tip, usually a brilliant beacon of contained starlight, pulsed with a low, dying ember light, struggling to maintain its luminescence against the encroaching dark.

"The Unmaking is at the seam," Drevik said. He did not look at the windows. He looked at the bowl of water on the table, watching the ripples caused by Halla’s movements. "The Nought has found a way to mimic the frequency of the Fold."

Mira’s tapping stopped instantly. Her breath hitched. "How? The wards are tuned to a unique resonance. It is mathematically impossible for the Nought to match the harmonic structure of our foundations."

Drevik reached out. His gloved hand, steady despite the tremor in his spirit, picked up a small, discarded potato peel from the table. He turned it over in his fingers, studying the pale, organic texture against the backdrop of the encroaching void.

"It isn't mimicking us, Mira," he said softly. His voice was a low rasp. "It is remembering us. It is finding the places where we have already been undone, the psychic scars where our reality has frayed. It is using our own decay as a map."

He looked up. His eyes—those vast, dark voids where stars hung like trapped, silver coins—swept the room. When they rested on Halla, she felt a sudden, sharp, agonizing urge to do something domestic. She wanted to offer him a piece of warm bread, to wrap him in the thick wool of a hearth-blanket and tell him the pantry was full and the world was safe. She wanted to lie to him. But she could not. The pantry was a fiction now. The walls of the kitchen were becoming thin, translucent as beaten silver, revealing the grey static pressing against the other side.

"The betrayal is not at the gate," Drevik continued, his voice dropping to a pitch that made the water in Halla's bowl tremble. "The betrayal is in the architecture. Someone has taught the Nought how to read our foundations. Someone has shown the void where the stitches are weak."

A silence followed, heavier than the stone of the house. Halla felt a coldness that had nothing to precisely to do with the Nought. She looked at Mira, whose eyes were fixed on the floor, her face pale and etched with sudden, sharp suspicion. She thought of Sera, standing sentinel at the altars. She thought of Vesper, her blades sharpened and ready. They were all part of the foundation. If the foundation was betrayed, who was left to trust?

"Who?" Halla asked. The word felt small, pathetic, against the weight of the room.

Drevik did not answer. He set the potato peel back on the table with a deliberate, hollow click. He moved toward the hearth, where the fire was struggling against a draft that had no physical source—a wind that blew from nowhere and smelled of nothing. He placed his hand near the embers. He did not touch them; he simply watched them, as if waiting for the fire to remember its fundamental duty to burn.

"The seam is turning," he said.

The floor shuddered.

It was not the shake of the earth, nor the vibration of a passing storm. It was a shudder of the concept of earth. For a terrifying, breathless heartbeat, the kitchen felt inverted. Halla felt the sudden, sickening weight of her feet lift from the floor, and the ceiling pressed down against her brow with a crushing, atmospheric pressure. The heavy oak table seemed to float, its massive legs stretching upward toward the rafters like reaching, desperate limbs. The very gravity of the room had lost its orientation.

"Hold the hearth!" Drevik’s voice cut through the vertigo. It was not a command of magical power, but a plea of absolute focus. A command to remain anchored.

Halla gripped the edge of the wooden table. Her knuckles turned a ghostly white. She forced herself to focus on the grain of the wood, the knobby irregularities of the oak, the physical, unyielding reality of the heavy timber. She inhaled deeply, forcing the scent of damp earth and potato starch into her lungs, using the mundane to fight the monstrous. She anchored herself to the hunger, to the work, to the simple, stubborn fact of being alive.

The room snapped back. The table slammed down against the floor with a sound like a closing tomb, the impact vibrating through Hallaya's teeth.

"The Fold is losing its density," Mira said, her voice small and trembling. She was clutching her ledgers to her chest as if they were shields, her eyes darting around the room as if searching for a way out that no longer existed. "The boundaries are blurring. I can feel the math failing."

Drevik stood by the dying fire. The light from his mitre cast long, distorted, dancing shadows across the walls, making the kitchen look like a cavern of shifting shapes. He looked less like a man and more like a hole in the world, a silhouette carved out of something much darker and more profound than the night.

"The Unmaking is eating the memory of the house," Drevik said. He turned his head slightly, the light catching the edge of his ceramic mask. "If we do not find the source of the leak, there will be no house left to defend. There will be no memory of us. There will only be the Nought, and we will be part of its infinite, silent counting."

He turned to leave. He did not say goodbye. He did not promise a return. He simply moved toward the door, his heavy, purple vestments sweeping the floor with a sound like falling ash.

Halla watched him go. She watched the way the darkness seemed to follow him, a cloak of shadow that even the void crystals in his mitre could not pierce. He was the only thing holding the door shut, the only thing maintaining the boundary between the known and the unknown, and she could see the way he was leaking—the way the light of his presence was being eroded by the very thing he fought.

She turned back to the bowl of water. The potatoes were still there, pale and silent, stripped of their skins. They looked like small, drowned things.

She picked up the knife. Her hands were steady, but her heart was a heavy, useless thing, a stone in her chest. She began to peel again. Slice, peel, drop. Slice, peel, drop. She would keep the rhythm. She would keep the kitchen. She would keep the hearth.

Even if the hearth was nothing but a memory of heat in a room made of smoke.

Outside, the grey static pressed against the glass, thicker than before, a suffocating shroud. And for the first time, through the muffled silence of the enchantment, Halla heard it: a sound like a thousand voices, all whispering the same, forgotten name, rising from the ash.
