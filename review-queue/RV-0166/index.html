The long table was heavy with the weight of what had been lost, and the silver service was polished until it looked like mirrors for things we did not want to see. I had laid the cloth myself, a heavy linen of cream so bright it felt offensive in the dimming light of the hall. I had measured the distance between the plates with the precision of a duelist’s stride, ensuring the symmetry was absolute. In the Fold, symmetry is the only thing that keeps the void from leaking through the seams. To allow a tilt, a misalignment, was to invite the entropy to settle in the marrow of the house.

The air in the dining hall was unnervingly still. Usually, there was the hum of the house—the soft respiration of the heating vents, the distant settling of stone. Tonight, there was only the oppressive silence of a held breath.

Drevik did not enter the room so much as the room rearranged itself to accommodate him. He came from the direction of the Threshold, where the air always tasted of cold iron and spent light, a place where the laws of physics felt like mere suggestions. He did not strip away the day. He carried the weight of his station like a physical burden. His black and deep-purple vestments remained fully fastened, the gold celestial embroidery catching the low, flickering light of the hearth. The tall crystal mituring sat upon his head, the void crystals within it drifting in their slow, gravitational orbit, casting rhythmic, pulsing shadows across his features.

He did not speak. He took his place at the head of the table, the heavy oak chair groaning under a weight that seemed greater than his physical form. He leaned back, the shoulders of his mantle settling, and for a moment, the shadows in the corners of the room seemed to pull toward him, drawn by the sheer density of his presence.

"The Ninth Blazon has been struck," he said.

He did not use a flourish. He spoke as one records a debt—flat, undeniable, and irreversible.

I kept my hands steady as I poured the wine. My pulse was a frantic thing against my ribs, but my fingers remained the tools of a servant: precise, unshakeable. The liquid was dark, the color of a bruised plum, flowing into the crystal goblet with a rhythmic splash. "The scouts reported the breach at the border of the Iron Choir," I said, my voice finding the necessary cadence of reportage. "They said the law was broken."

"The law was not broken," Drevik said. He reached out, his gloved fingers hovering near the edge of his white-ceramic mask. The gold accents on the porcelain gleamed like predatory eyes. "It was rewritten. They are using the code of the Blazon to justify the encroachment. They call it an expansion of jurisdiction. I call it a siege."

I set the decanter down. The sound of the heavy glass hitting the wood was a small, sharp period at the end of a sentence. I could feel the coldness of the room creeping into my bones. "And the others? The ones who whispered in the corridors of the Bureau? The ones who seemed so certain of their neutrality?"

Drevik let a sliver of a smirk touch his lips. It was a dangerous thing, that smile; it possessed the jagged edge of a broken mirror. It was the smile of a man who had already seen the end of the ledger and found it balanced, even if the cost was ruin. "They believe they have found a seam," he murmured. "They think that by pressing against the weight of the even-numbered Blazons, they can force the Threshold to yield. They do not realize that the Threshold is not a door, Vesper. It is a wound. And wounds do not expand without bleeding."

I looked at him then, truly looked, moving past the terrifying majesty of his office. I saw the way the star-shaped pupils in his void-eyes fixed on the empty space above the table, as if he were reading the invisible ink of a treaty signed in a dead language. I saw the tension in his gloved hands, the way his knuckles remained white beneath the dark fabric. This was not the stillness of peace; it was the stillness of a predator waiting for the trap to spring.

"You are going back," I said. It was not a question. The truth sat between us, heavy and unmovable as a mountain.

"I have to go where the debt is due, Vesper. The Ninth is claiming territory that belongs to the Fold. If I do not answer the summons, the cost will be paid in the lives of the Devoted. I cannot sit in this hall and polish silver while the borders dissolve."

"You will go alone," I said. The words felt like steel sliding into a scabbard. I felt the sudden, sharp spike of my own conviction, a cold flame. "If they are using the Blazon, they are using the law. You cannot fight a law with a staff or a spell. You cannot strike down a decree with force. You fight it with a counter-claim. You fight it with legitimacy."

Drevik stood. The movement was sudden, a violent release of gravity that made the candlelight dance erratically. He moved around the table toward me, not like a man, but like a rising tide. The scent of him preceded him—ozone, old parchment, and the terrifying, clean emptiness of the space between stars.

"I have already prepared the claim," he said, his voice dropping to a low, resonant hum. "The legalities are etched in the very marrow of the archive."

He stopped inches from me. The height of him forced me to tilt my/my head back, exposing the vulnerable line of my throat. The air between us thickened, turning into a pressurized weight that made the fine hairs on my arms stand on end. I could see my own reflection in the polished surface of his mask—a small, pale figure caught in the orbit of a collapsing sun.

"But a claim requires a witness," he continued, his voice a silken threat. "A witness to certify that the breach occurred under the old terms. A witness whose presence cannot be dismissed as mere political theater. Someone to stand in the mouth of the choir and declare the law violated."

"I will witness it," I said. My voice was a low, steady thing, though my heart was hammering a frantic rhythm against my sternum. "I will hold the line of the argument until you can strike the blow. I will be the anchor for your claim."

Drevik reached out. His hand, encased in the dark, heavy fabric of his sleeve, moved to the side of my face. He did not stroke my cheek; he did not offer the comfort of a lover. He simply placed his thumb against my jaw, a heavy, grounding weight that felt as though it could crush bone if he willed it. His eyes searched mine, scanning for the slightest tremor, the smallest flicker of doubt, the microscopic scent of the betrayal that the world was constantly trying to carve into our household.

He found only the steel.

"Then the debt is shared," he said.

He leaned in. The cold, unyielding edge of his mask brushed my forehead. I closed my eyes and leaned into the weight of him, letting the terrifying reality of our situation consume me. This intimacy was not a soft thing; it a pact. It was the pressing of two blades together until the friction created heat. His breath was warm against my skin, a singular, living contrast to the void-darkness of his eyes and the chilling air of the hall.

I reached up and gripped his wrist. I felt the immense, unmapped power beneath the fabric—the strength of a man who held the reins of a collapsing reality. I wanted to know the depth of it, to find the edge of the man beneath the sage, to see if there was anything left of the boy I had once known. But I knew better. To seek the edge was to fall into the void. I chose instead to hold the anchor.

We stayed like that, two points of gravity in a shifting universe, while the fire in the hearth popped, throwing a single, dying spark against the grate. For a moment, the Fold felt smaller, tighter, as if the very walls were leaning in to protect us from the encroaching dark.

When he finally pulled away, the loss of his warmth felt like a physical blow. The room felt suddenly hollow, the silence even more profound than before. He turned back toward the shadows, toward the equipment of his station, toward the war that waited outside the Threshold.

"Eat," he said, his voice returning to its casual, wry tone, though the mask hid the rest. "The transition will be long, and I will not have you fainting from hunger while I am negotiating with the Iron Choir. I cannot have my witness looking pale and sickly."

"I have already eaten," I lied. My stomach was a knot of cold lead.

He paused, his hand resting on the back of the heavy oak chair. He looked back at me, the smirk returning, sharper now, mocking my transparent deception. "Your ledger is a mess, Vesper. You always forget to account for the simplest truths. You are starving for something far more dangerous than bread."

He walked away, his footsteps silent on the stone floor, leaving a wake of fading light behind him.

I turned back to the table, my hands trembling for the first time. The silver was still bright. The cloth was still straight. The symmetry was preserved. But as I reached down to begin the clearing, my fingers brushed against something wet near the edge of my setting.

I pulled the cloth back and froze.

There, on the pristine white linen, was a small, dark smudge. It was a drop of something viscous and heavy—not the deep red of wine, not the bright crimson of blood, but a residue of the void itself. It was a smear of nothingness, a piece of the dark that had bled through the very fabric of our reality.

The Ninth Blazon had already touched us. The breach was not a distant threat on a map; it was a stain on my table. The invasion had already begun.

I stared at the smudge, my breath hitching in my throat. I reached out, intending to scrub it away, to restore the perfection of the room, but my hand hesitated. To wipe it away was to pretend the lie was still true. To leave it was to acknowledge the end.

Slowly, with a hand that felt as though it belonged to someone else, I wiped the smudge away. I smoothed the linen until the surface was once again a perfect, deceptive white. I began to clear the plates, one by one, with a methodical, rhythmic devotion, preparing the rooms for a war I knew we would not win by force, but by the sheer strength of our seams.

As I moved through the hall, the heavy iron ring of my key ring clinked against my hip—a small, metallic, rhythmic sound in the deepening gloom. It was a sound of permanence. A sound of locks and latches.

I looked toward the heavy, locked doors of the archive, and then toward the dark, yawning mouth of the Threshold. Tomorrow, the negotiations would begin. Tomorrow, the lies would escalate.

I would need to find the locksmith.
