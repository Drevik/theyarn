Best Budget Gaming Headsets Under $100 (2026)

July 2026 · 5 products reviewed

I've bought more gaming headsets than I'm willing to admit. The cheap ones break in six months. The expensive ones aren't worth the premium. The sweet spot lives between $40 and $80 — and a few models there punch well above their weight.

What you hear is not what was said.The biggest lie in gaming audio is that you need to spend $150+ for decent sound. You don't. What you need is a headset with good drivers, a mic that doesn't make you sound like you're broadcasting from a tin can, and a headband that won't crack after a year. All five of these deliver on at least two of three, and our top pick nails all three.

What Actually Matters in a Gaming Headset

Before the picks, here's what separates a good budget headset from landfill fodder:

  • Driver size: 40mm is the minimum. 50mm drivers produce fuller bass but don't automatically sound better — tuning matters more than size.
  • Mic quality: Flip-up mics are convenient but tend to pick up more room noise. Boom mics with noise cancellation (even basic) sound noticeably better on calls.
  • Comfort: Memory foam ear cushions and an adjustable headband are non-negotiable for sessions over an hour. Leatherette gets sweaty; mesh breathes better but wears faster.
  • Build: Metal headbands survive drops. Plastic ones don't. Check the hinge points — that's where budget headsets snap first.

HyperX Cloud III — Best Overall

The Cloud III is what every budget headset wants to be when it grows up. 53mm drivers that deliver clear mids and punchy bass without the muddy boom that plagues cheaper headsets. The leatherette ear cushions are thick enough that my ears didn't touch the drivers during a four-hour session. The boom mic flips up to mute (a physical mute, not software — no accidental unmutes mid-raid), and teammates said I sounded clearer than on a $180 SteelSeries.

The downside: it's wired-only, the braided cable is stiff and tends to coil where you don't want it, and the RGB ring on each earcup is pointless. But for $70, this is the headset to beat. HyperX has been making the Cloud line for over a decade and it shows — everything that should work, works.

Best for: PC and console gamers who want reliability over flash.

Razer Kraken V3 X — Best for Long Sessions

The Kraken V3 X weighs 285 grams — light enough that I forgot I was wearing it after 20 minutes. The cooling gel-infused ear cushions actually work; my ears didn't get that sweaty hot feeling after two hours of Ranked. The 7.1 surround sound via Razer Synapse is decent for positional audio in shooters, though it's software-based (not real surround drivers).

Here's the catch: the mic is mediocre. It works, but teammates will know you're on a budget headset. The headband is all plastic and creaks when you adjust it. And Razer Synapse is one of the more intrusive companion apps — it runs in the background and occasionally forgets your audio settings after a Windows update. If you play long sessions and care about ear comfort more than mic quality, this is your pick.

SteelSeries Arctis Nova 7 — Best Wireless Under $100

Wireless headsets under $100 are usually bad. The Arctis Nova 7 is the exception. It uses a USB dongle with a physical switch that toggles between PC and PlayStation audio — no Bluetooth pairing dance, no latency issues in game. The 2.4GHz connection is rock solid within 30 feet. Logitech G432 — Best Entry-Level Pick

At $40, the G432 is what you buy when you're not sure if you even want a gaming headset. The 50mm drivers are surprisingly capable — music sounds thin but game audio (footsteps, explosions, voice comms) comes through clearly. The problems are obvious: the plastic build feels cheap (because it is), the ear cushions flatten after a few months, and the 7.1 surround is Logitech's G HUB software doing the work — it's decent but not great. If you game on Xbox, most wireless headsets won't work — Xbox uses a proprietary wireless protocol. The Stealth 500 is one of the few under-$100 options with native Xbox wireless. Frequently Asked Questions

Do cheap gaming headsets sound bad?

Not anymore. The $40-80 range has gotten genuinely good in the last few years. The HyperX Cloud III at $70 sounds better than headsets that cost twice as much five years ago. The gap between a $50 headset and a $150 one is real but smaller than most people think — you get diminishing returns fast. Where budget headsets still cut corners is build quality and mic clarity, not audio fidelity.

Wired or wireless for budget headsets?

Wired, full stop, under $80. Wireless at this price means latency, compression artifacts, and a battery that degrades after a year. A wired headset gives you lossless audio, zero latency, and no charging for $30-50 less. If you absolutely need wireless, the SteelSeries Arctis Nova 7 is the cheapest one worth buying, and it's right at the $100 mark.

What's the best gaming headset for both PC and console?

The HyperX Cloud III works with everything — PC, PS5, Switch, and mobile via its 3.5mm cable. The SteelSeries Arctis Nova 7 has a USB dongle that swaps between PC and PlayStation with a button press. If you play on Xbox specifically, look for headsets with the Xbox logo on the box — Xbox uses a proprietary wireless protocol that only licensed headsets support.

Is surround sound in gaming headsets real?

Most "7.1 surround sound" headsets are stereo drivers with software virtualization. It works decently for directional audio in games but it's not real surround — you're not getting seven separate speakers. Some headsets, like the Razer Kraken, have decent spatial audio software. But don't pick a headset just because the box says 7.1. A good stereo headset with Windows Sonic or Dolby Atmos enabled does the same thing for free.

How long should a budget gaming headset last?

Two to three years with daily use is reasonable at the $50-80 price point. The most common failure points are the headband (snaps at the adjustment point), ear cushions (peel and flatten), and the cable near the 3.5mm jack. Headsets with braided cables and metal headbands tend to last longer. The HyperX Cloud line is known for surviving multiple years of abuse.

Can I use a gaming headset for work calls and music?

Yes, but with caveats. Gaming headsets are tuned for game audio — boosted bass and treble, recessed mids. Music sounds fun but not accurate. For work calls, the mic quality on budget headsets ranges from passable (Cloud III) to tinny and echoey (most Razer models). If you split time between gaming and calls, the HyperX Cloud III is your best bet — its mic is unusually clear for the price, and the sound signature is balanced enough for music.
