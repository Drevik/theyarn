Chapter Five — Fen's Ledger

Mira filed the new key a stroke at a time and blew the brass off the bit between strokes. Two more hairs of depth and the ward would ride clean. A lock cut tired is a lock cut twice. She had said that to apprentices who no longer existed, and she said it now to the key, because the key was the only apprentice left to her.

Across the long room, Fen fair-copied the quarter's accounts, and his pen did not lift between lines. Mira noted it the way she noted a gate hung a quarter-inch off its post: without deciding anything yet.

The tithe-house at Waymeet held the north roads' paper because three roads met at the town and pilgrims came in tired. The hostel's ovens worked across the lane, and the smell came through the shutters warm. The Devoted tithe hours, not coin, and a steward's office turned hours into ink: hours pledged at the shrine, hours served on the wall, hours of needlework, cartage, watch. Ordinary bookkeeping, ordinary as bread. The Communion itself wrote nothing down. The Ascendant kept his memory for an archive and would not sign so much as a letter he had not first said aloud. Which meant the only book the Veiled Communion kept on the north roads was a steward's book, and a steward is only a man.

She asked for the fair copy at dusk and read it standing. It balanced to the hour and to the copper. Mira had been raised on Bureau forms; she had never once seen an honest book balance. Honest books had blots. They carried second thoughts in the margin and the smell of a lamp put out too late. Fen's book smelled of nothing.

The grain did not balance. That was the second note she made. Every factor on the north roads had doubled and redoubled his price since the granaries failed at Cold Harrow, and House Corin's line had not moved a copper. Deep granaries, Fen said. Old contracts, Fen said. She wrote tolerances on her pad and did not argue. An argument is where people go to practice their lies.

"Your ring," she said, and held out her palm, because it was audit, and an audit was the one hour she asked for anything with her hand open.

Fen worked the ring off his belt and put it in her hand without a word. Five keys. Her book said four. The fifth was new, and it was not her cut: some market smith had ground it up in soft brass, the bit still bright off the wheel, the throat hurried. Cheap keys look cheap. This one looked hurried, which was worse.

She set her gauge on it without asking. Courtesy was for shop floors. The bit height said strongbox. No cupboard in Waymeet said anything at all.

"I lost a cupboard key," Fen said.

"You lost this one too," she said, "whoever ground it for you. A copy voids its lock. I'll re-key the box tomorrow. You'll sign the re-key."

He signed the day's forms. His hand was steady. She had watched men at the Bureau sign away whole warehouses with steady hands; the tell was never the hand. The tell was what the hand did after. Fen's hand, after, squared the blotting pad three times.

The shrine bell rang once, at the wrong hour, and every head in the room turned to the niche in the north wall. Fen's pen stopped mid-line for the first time all evening.

The Threshold opened, and Drevik came out of it with road on him: ash worked into the gold embroidery, the staff's orb banked low like a covered coal. The door behind him shut, and it did not latch. His doors never latched. They decided, and then they were shut, and nobody heard the deciding.

The two tithe-wardens stopped stacking seal wax. A pilgrim woman who had come in late put her back against the wall to make more room for him, though the room had never been full.

"Waymeet," he said, to the room in general. "I smelled your bread from inside the seam. It isn't fair. We keep a kitchen that never cools, and nobody bakes in it." The wardens laughed. He wore the mask and the whole mitre and four days of road, and they laughed anyway. They always did. It was not the joke. It was that he paid for the joke in kind, and he told it with the smirk the half-mask never quite covered.

"Cold Harrow is mended," he said. He was half-turned toward the niche still, and his sentences were always shortest there, where the door was. "The seam was small. The grain remembers being grain again. Most of it. Forgetting is faster than mending. We went street by street."

Then he stood still and gave the tithe-house the names of the dead of Cold Harrow, because he keeps what nobody writes down. He does not read names from anything. He stands, and he says them, and that is the archive, and the archive is warm the way a banked fire is warm.

The fourth name came late. A full breath late. He let it come, and said it, and then said, "Forgive me," to no one in the room, and everyone looked at their hands.

Mira gauged backlash for a living. She knew what a hair of play cost a mechanism. In four years she had never once seen him reach for a name and miss. The stars in his eyes had drifted to the rim, small and far, like lamps moved to the walls of a long room, and she looked away first, which she always did, and which she would deny.

He took the wardens' report standing, thanked them by name and by their mothers' names, and forgave one of them a pledged hour from the spring fair with the amount correct to the minute. What everyone gives, he keeps. She had stopped explaining to herself why that steadied her.

On his way out he stopped at her bench and set a folded paper by her lamp.

"From Sera," he said. "A psalm for the road north. She copied it out herself." It was true; the paper smelled of the censer-room, sandalwood gone bitter with use. Mira resented how glad she was to carry it. Everything that reached her from the hearth came through Sera's hand these days, and the gate was Sera's to open, and Mira had signed nothing when she agreed to that, and had never once been sorry either.

"Mind the paper," he said. "Paper cuts."

"So do I," she said.

He laughed, and the door decided to be shut behind him.

She stayed the night in the record room, because a key wanted tempering and the bench was there, and she did not rush a key. The next day was the box. She pulled the old ward set in the morning, cut the new one to gauge by mid-afternoon, hung the spare on the audit ring, and by dusk the strongbox had a lock Fen's soft brass could not have opened anyway, which did not let him off anything.

She was filing the spare when the outer door went, well after the bell.

Fen crossed the office to open it, and a voice arrived ahead of its owner, a voice that had never once needed raising, the kind that owns its floor. Silk under a traveling wool. Clove over wet wool. A seal ring on the middle finger of the glove hand. Corin, of House Corin, grain and surety on the north roads. Mira knew the house from the fair book. The fair book was fond of it.

The record room kept the oldest papers behind a warded grille, diamond gaps sized for handing documents across, not for hiding behind. Mira set down her file and became furniture. She had been furniture at the Bureau often enough. Furniture hears everything.

"You keep late hours, steward."

"Audit, my lady. The Communion keeps its hours."

"So I have heard." A chair took silk. "And other people's hours, I am told. Show me the quarter. Fen, your columns sit like soldiers. I could march them down the north road and back."

Pages turned in the office. Mira's hand lay flat on the bench, and her thumb found the callus the files had raised there, and pressed.

"And the drift column," Corin read. "He left at dusk on the ninth and came in before the bell, with nine days of wear on his boots. Nine days in an hour. Time is a coin, steward. I built my house on coins, and I do love a coin with two faces."

"The Veiled One does not deal in coins," Fen said.

"Everything deals. The good ones just don't know they're doing it." The pages turned again, unhurried. "Your tares have improved. A tare is what a thing carries that isn't itself. A wagon carries its own wood. A boot carries its road. Keep weighing him. I want his tares for the whole winter, every arrival at this door, in your plain hand."

"You said spring," Fen said. "You said the bond could rest till spring if the columns came."

"And they came, and they march. So the bond rests. Seasons are patient, Fen. I bought your brother's paper for its patience; it has been patient three years; it can be patient while you learn to price what walks out of a shrine door and what walks back in wearing more road than the hour can hold."

"What do you want with his hours?"

"To know where they pool." The silk stood up off the chair. "Somewhere there is a floor where that man's hours are banked. A place that spends a week and an hour both and tells nobody which. Floors like that can be found. Floors like that can be entered. I am sixty-one years old, and I have never once seen a floor that could not be entered." She drew the glove off finger by finger. "And you will not tell him. Not because I frighten clerks; I don't. Because if you tell him, he will forgive you. And you could not stand it."

The pages stopped. The glove stopped. For a while nobody in the office moved, and Mira did not breathe at the grille, and then Fen picked up the lamp to walk the woman to the road, because he was a steward, and stewards see the factor out.

Mira watched the office stand empty. The fair book sat squared. The blotter sat squared. The room had been squared twice over.

She was standing in the office doorway when Fen came back, and she had touched nothing. Locks are a trade for people who do not touch first.

She laid the copied key on the desk, in the lamplight, where soft brass tells its story at speed. "Whose door did this open?"

Fen looked at the key for longer than the key needed. Men look at the evidence when the story of themselves is nearly done. The Bureau had taught her that, and the Bureau had been right about everything except itself.

"Her accounts," he said. "The factor's house takes the quarter's fair copies. Grain, cartage, tare. I write the master fair, in my hand."

"You keep two books," she said. "Every honest clerk keeps two. I want the one with the blots."

The lamp sat in its own oil smoke. Down the road a cart went by with a bad axle.

"She holds my brother's bond," Fen said. That was not an answer. It was an answer. "The cargo drowned with its ship. The paper didn't. She bought the paper off the underwriters and named the interest in columns. Three years. I have paid her in columns."

"And tonight the price went up."

"No," he said. "Tonight I learned it was never the bond. She could have called the bond any season she liked. She likes this better. She likes being owed in ink."

He brought the rough book out of his own desk, set it down, and stepped back from it. He looked lighter with the drawer shut. That was the worst of the night so far.

The rough book did not pretend to be grain. Dates ran down the margin. The shrine bell's hours. Departures and returns at the niche. A column headed wear, which was days of road read off one man's boots, and a column headed over, where the arithmetic lived: hours gained, week on week, a stair going up.

Against four dates someone had set small careful marks. Autumn. Winter. Spring. And midsummer, which she remembered from inside. The Communion's own festivals, the joinings, when the hearth grew by one and the whole town came to the niche to sing. After every mark, the over column stepped wider, step on step.

The last column had no heading. It named travelers. The censer-bearer, twice, come out with her psalms. The seamstress, twice, with a bundle that hummed. The one with the sword, once, at night, in the rain. The tall woman from the stores, at the spring fair, counting sacks like she was angry at them.

And: the small one with the ring of keys.

Mira read her own entry twice. Her hand went to the cord at her throat, where a plain iron key hung. The first key Drevik ever cut for anyone, cut for a door in the Fold that opened nothing at all out here. She wore it anyway. She had never once said why; he had never once asked; and that was the whole arrangement, and she was very fond of the arrangement.

The key was cold under her thumb. Keys are honest that way. They tell you what they are made of and nothing else.

Three lines at the bottom of the last page were in a hand that was not Fen's. She had heard that hand all evening, and it had never once needed raising, and it wrote small and level:

Weigh the door, not the man.

"What does she want with the over?"

"To know where the hours pool," Fen said. "She says everything can be lent."

"Do you believe her?"

"I believe her the way you believe weather," he said. "It doesn't need believing."

"Keep the book," she said. "Keep it exact. If a column ever goes soft, I will know whose key went soft with it." She took the copied brass and the old box lock off the desk, because both were hers and their work was done, and she left the re-key receipt squared on the blotter where he would find it in the morning. Everything you take, you write down. It was the one Bureau habit she had kept, and it was about to be the only honest paper in the building.

"Will you tell him?" Fen said.

"He writes nothing down," she said.

"That is not an answer."

"No," she said. "It isn't."

She sat at the bench and did the arithmetic, because arithmetic does not wait for your permission, and it was finished before she reached for the lamp.

The over column was a stair. The stair rose at the joinings, step for step, woman for woman. The Fold's hours did not pool because the Fold was clever. They pooled because the Fold was held: held open, held slow, held warm at the hearth, by the count and the kind of the people who lived inside it. Every bond was a beam. Every joining set a wider floor on the same few beams, and the beams were not stone and were not void. The beams were them. Take one bond away — unmake one — and the stair walked itself back down. Unmake was a word the north roads had been teaching her all season. She did not like how well it fit.

Foundations are checked from below. The Bureau had made her prove it on a model with lead weights. That was the flaw in the Fold, and the flaw was not hers to have found. The flaw had been found a year ago, column by column, by a woman with one glove, who read foundations the way she read ledgers — for what they would bear — and who had been paying a steward's dead brother's bond, season by season, for the reading.

The Communion kept one book, and the book was a lie, and the lie had been going north at grain prices for a year.

Mira locked the tithe-house, checked the lock, and checked it again, which was her one superstition, and walked to the niche through the length of the town, which belonged to none of the town's three roads. The stars were out, hard and far apart, like light the sky had spent already.

The Threshold stood open a hand's width.

He did that when he was waiting for someone and did not want to say so. In four years she had never once seen him wait.

She put her thumb to the cold iron at her throat and did not take it off, and went in to say the cost out loud.
