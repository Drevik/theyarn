The silver needle did not lie, but Wren had.

The thread was the same—dyed with the crushed indigo of the Bazaar, a shade that should have matched the ledgered count—but the seam was wrong. It was a shallow stitch, a mend meant to hide a gap rather than close it. Wren sat in the light of the late afternoon, the sun trapped in the amber of the Fold’s garden, and looked at the discrepancy.

The inventory of the Weaver’s Cache was short three spools of void-silk.

She did not breathe. She did not move. She only watched the way the light caught the fraying edge of the scrap in her hand. A mistake in the counting was a burden; a mistake in the hiding was a rot. It was a slow, creeping decay, like dampness in the floorboards of an old manor. Every time she closed her eyes, she saw the ledger: the neat, ink-black columns, the precise tally of precious materials, and the ghost of the three spools that simply ceased to exist the moment she stopped looking at them.

The silence of the workshop felt heavy, thick with the scent of cedar and old dust. She felt the weight of the theft—not even a theft, she told herself, just a displacement. But displacement required an explanation, and she had none that did not sound like a lie.

The door to the workshop creaked. It was a heavy sound, the sound of wood meeting wood, a sound that belonged to the house. Drevik entered. He did not announce himself; he simply occupied the space that had been empty. He wore the black and deep-purple of his vestments, the high collar stiff and unforgiving, and the gold celestial embroidery caught the dying light like fallen stars. His presence did not fill the room; it changed the room's gravity.

He moved toward the table where she worked. His steps were silent, yet the floor seemed to achnowledge him, a subtle vibration under her feet. He did not look at the silk. He looked at her.

From beneath the white-ceramic kitsune mask, his gaze was a weight. She could not see his eyes, only the dark, star-flecked void where the mask ended, but she felt the pull of them. It was as if he were reading the tension in her shoulders, the way her fingers trembled ever so slightly against the fabric. He reached out and picked up a small, discarded bobbin. He turned it between his gloved fingers with the slow, methodical care of a man weighing a soul.

"The indigo is thinning, Wren," he said.

His voice was plain. It lacked the flourish of the preachers in the Veiled Communion. It was the voice of a man who spoke only when the truth required a vessel.

Wren kept her eyes fixed on the needle. "The dye is old," she said, her voice steadier than her heart. "The batches are not as consistent as they were in the first season. The heat in the drying room has been higher lately."

"The dye is fine," he said. He set the bobbin down. The click of the wood on the table was the only punctuation. "The count is wrong."

Wren felt the coldness of the Threshold pressing against the walls of the Fold, though the room was warm. She felt the mismatch—the gap between the thread she held and the truth she carried. It was a seam she had tried to pull tight, to smooth over with cleverness and silence, but the fabric of the even the strongest weave eventually reveals its flaws under pressure.

"I thought I could rebalance it," she said. The confession slipped out, a small, brittle thing, unbidden and terrifying. "I thought if I worked the surplus into the repair work, no one would notice the deficit. I thought I could find the error in the next shipment."

Drevik did not move. He stood like a monument of dark silk and gold. "To hide a deficit is to invite a fracture. You are mending a garment, Wren. You are not mending a foundation."

"I was protecting the tally," she said, and the lie tasted like ash. She finally looked up, meeting the blank, porcelain face of his mask. "I did not want the others to think the stores were failing. I did not want Vesper to think the security of the Fold was compromised by my incompetence. If the inventory is seen as unstable, the trust in the Weaver’s Cache dissolves. And if the cache dissolves, what are we left with? Just walls and hungry mouths."

"We are left with the truth of what we possess," Drevik countered. He stepped closer, invading the small circle of light around her workspace. The scent of him arrived—incumbent and overwhelming: incense, cold stone, and the sharp, metallic tang of the void. It was the scent of the man who held the door shut against the end of all things. "A house built on a false tally is a house built on sand. Do you think the void cares for your intentions, Wren? It only sees the gaps."

"It wasn't an intention to steal," she whispered, her eyes stinging. "It was an intention to maintain. There is a difference."

"The difference is academic," he said. His voice dropped an octave, becoming a low vibration that seemed to rattle her very ribs. "The thread is gone. The silk is missing. The space where it should be is an invitation to those who would strip this place bare."

He reached out, his hand hovering near hers. He did not touch her, but the heat from his palm radiated through the air, a low, steady hum. For a fleeting second, she wanted to lean into that warmth, to let him carry the weight of her shame. She saw the gold accents of his mask catch the light, and for a moment, she thought she saw the shadow of a smirk—not a cruel thing, but the sly, knowing expression of a man who had already seen the loose thread.

"The three spools," he said, the softness gone, replaced by a chilling finality. "Where are they?"

The silence stretched. It was a heavy, tactile thing, like wet wool. Wren thought of the small, dark corner behind the loom, the place where the silk sat, uncounted, waiting for a use she had not yet earned. She thought of the way the void-silk felt—cold, unnerving, as if it were trying to slip through her fingers even when she held it tight.

"They are in the cellar," she said, the words stripped of the need to weave. "Behind the grain sacks. I... I couldn't let them be recorded as lost. Not yet."

She said it as a confession, a surrender.

Drevik pulled his hand back. He did not congratulate her. He did not offer a comfort. He simply stood in the aftermath of her honesty, his silhouette cutting a jagged shape against the evening light.

"A debt is a debt, even if it is paid in silk," he said. "But a silence is a debt that grows interest. You have let the rot settle into the grain, Wren."

He turned to leave, his heavy vestments sweeping the floor with a rhythmic, low sound that echoed like a funeral march.

"Drevik."

He paused at the threshold of the workshop. He did not turn his head, but she knew he was listening. In the Fold, he heard everything.

"I will mend it," she said, her voice gaining a sudden, desperate strength. "The count. The mistake. I will make it right. I will find a way to account for every thread, I swear it."

"The thread is already pulled," he said, his voice trailing off into the shadows of the hallway.

He stepped out, and the door closed with a finality that left the room feeling much larger and much emptier.

Wren sat in the fading light, her hands shaking so violently that the needle became a blur of silver. She looked at the indigo thread, the way it lay across the table, a single, straight line that divided the light from the dark. She felt the crushing weight of her own smallness. She had thought she was being clever, an architect of stability, protecting the edges of her world from the chaos of scarcity. But she realized now that her silence had been its own kind of destruction.

The house was growing, the rooms were expanding, and the walls were thickening with every new soul that joined the Hearth. But as she stared at the empty space where the three spools should have been, Wren knew that the foundation was only as strong as the smallest stitch.

She stood, her legs heavy, and picked up her basket. She began to walk toward the cellar, the movement mechanical, driven by a sudden, sharp dread.

The hallway was a tunnel of deepening shadows. Outside, the bells of the Communion began to toll, marking the hour of the evening vigil. The sound was rhythmic, steady, a pulse of iron and prayer that vibrated through the very stones of the Fold. It was the sound of a world being held together, one bell-strike at a time, a desperate attempt to impose order on the encroaching dark.

Wren reached the cellar door and pressed her hand against the cold wood. The grain was rough beneath her palm. She thought of the silk, hidden and waiting like a secret sin. She thought of Drevik, walking the halls of a house that was becoming a fortress, a man who could smell a lie before it was even spoken.

She opened the door. The dark inside was not empty; it was full of the things she had tried to bury—the half-finished repairs, the forgotten scraps, the mounting discrepancies. As she stepped down into the gloom, the air turned damp and smelled of earth and old harvest.

But as her foot met the first stone step, a new sound drifted down from the upper floors.

It was faint, distant, cutting through the tolling of the bells. It was a sharp, metallic rasp—the unmistakable sound of a blade being drawn across a whetstone in the training hall. It was a rhythmic, aggressive sound, a sound of preparation.

Wren froze, her heart hammering against her ribs. The sound was not one of peace or maintenance. It was the sound of a defense being assembled. Someone was preparing for a struggle, sharpening their steel for a conflict that had not yet arrived, but was undoubtedly coming.

The enemy was no longer just at the gates; the enemy was already inside the seam, and the cloth was beginning to tear.
