Mira had been thirty days in the Fold, and twenty-nine of them she had spent failing at the front door.

It had no keyway. No hinges. No seam a lamp could find, no give a blade could test, no tolerances worth the word. It opened to Drevik's palm and to nothing else on any road. So she had cut it a key anyway, out of spite, because a door without a lock was a wall that lied about itself. She was nearly through the bitting when the bell rang.

The bell over the Threshold. Thirty days, and it had never rung once.

She rolled her tools, the two tension wrenches she trusted and the one she was still auditing, and went down to the hall with the roll under her arm. A bell was an incident. Incidents got forms.

Drevik was already on the stair, taking the steps two at a time, mitre riding crooked, which she had never once seen. The void crystals inside it had gone quick. Sera stood at the rail with ash still on her cuffs from the morning rite. Wren came out of the sewing room with pins in her mouth and did not take them out. Vesper arrived barefoot from the training hall, practice blade in one hand, half an apple in the other. Nobody had called them. The bell had rung once, and the whole house had come to it.

"That will be Corin Vey," Drevik said. "I left him a door at Marrow Ford. He's four days early, the ass."

"You left a door standing on a public road," Mira said.

"For one man and forty minutes. He's the only one I've ever left a door for." He said it lightly, hand already flat against the wood, and the gold of the half-mask caught the lamp, and she filed the sentence whole. Doors at milestones. Standing access. She filed it and hated the file.

Gold ran the seam. The door opened on evening: real evening, wind with rain in it, a milestone, a road going two directions, and a man.

Road dust to the knee. A coat cut for hiding a blade and worn open anyway, which she noted. Boots resoled twice this year. Hands she read first, always. Quick, easy, and older than the face above them.

"Drevik," the man said. "I knew you when your eyes were brown."

"They were never brown. You've told that wrong on forty roads, and you owe me a copper each time." Drevik pulled him in by the shoulders. "Come in before the weather does."

They held each other a long moment and laughed in the middle of it, and neither the mask nor the mitre got in the way, which by now they wouldn't.

The greetings went around the hall like something rehearsed without anyone rehearsing it. Sera had his coat before he offered it. Wren was humming before he kissed her cheek; Mira had learned that the hum was how Wren kept easy things. Vesper gave the Blazon's greeting, wrist turned formal, and Corin returned it with the wrist wrong on purpose, and Vesper, who had not smiled at a guest in thirty days, smiled.

"There's a door standing out there like it grew overnight," Corin said. "You know how unsettling your real estate is, Drev?"

"I do. It's the effect I cultivate. Now sit down, you're letting the cold in."

The pack came off at the long table, and the gifts came out of it the way cards come off a deck, one at a time, each one aimed. For Sera, a paper cone of incense, the kind her town had burned at the evening rite; Sera held it under her nose a while, then turned away to set it on the shelf, and the turning away was its own review. For Wren, a spool of silk thread, grey-green, the color of water over the drowned market; Wren held it to the lamp and hummed lower. For Vesper, a whetstone out of the Bellwood quarries, dark and fine-grained; Vesper weighed it in her palm and gave it the nod she gave to weapons.

He found Mira last, by the tool roll.

"The lockswoman." He said it the way you say a thing you've been looking forward to. "Half the roads say the Ascendant married a Bureau woman. The other half say he married a door."

"Mira," Sera said, and said it with a hand on Mira's shoulder, the way you set something down where you mean it to stay.

"I didn't know your trade when I packed, or you'd have got a proper gift. So you get the honest one." From the coat pocket: a brass key blank. No bitting. Dead-soft stock, the kind that takes a clean cut.

"I carry one for every roof I sleep under," he said. "Never cut one yet. Cut it for whatever you keep."

A cut key means the man has studied your lock. A blank means he wants to watch your hands do the cutting. Both readings fit in the same palm. She took it and thanked him, and it came out flat, and Sera said, "That's her warm one," passing, and Corin laughed like he'd been handed something.

The pot over the hearth was warm because it was always warm; the kitchen kept its own fire, and nobody had ever explained that to her satisfaction either. The long table sat six without being asked. By evening the house had grown a room off the stair landing for the guest, done growing before the cards were dealt.

"It's never done that for a guest," Wren said, pleased.

"It likes him," Drevik said, and dealt.

Corin brought out a deck gone soft at the corners, and Drevik turned his chair around to the guest's side of the table. She had never seen him turn a chair.

"Tell them about the toll-house at Marrow Ford," Corin said.

"There's nothing to tell. I won a day's receipts at cards and handed them back."

"Minus my wage. I dealt."

"The toll-master couldn't count past his thumbs," Drevik said, "so I kept his tallies for him that season. Green ledger. You've been telling the roads it was red for two years, because being wrong at scale amuses you."

"He only corrects that story at this table," Wren said, around a pin.

"Consider yourself told the true ledger, lockswoman," Corin said to Mira. "That's membership."

Sera asked what the season had cost. She asked everything's cost; Mira had learned that was the liturgy of her, the way locks were the liturgy of Mira.

"Two days of a poor man's arithmetic and one enemy I've since outlived," Drevik said. "Cheap, at the price I was."

"What was he, before?" Sera said. It was the gentlest interrogation Mira had ever heard.

"Poorer," Corin said.

"Was he kind?" Wren said.

"He was this. In a smaller coat." Corin tipped his cup. "The sky hadn't got into him yet, but it was already asking."

He lost three exchanges to Corin before the soup and won only one all night, and the one he won he gave back. Mira kept a ledger for his laughs by now. The ones at that table were new to it: unpriced.

Midway through the meal, Corin looked up along the hall the way a man checks a ceiling for weather. "The hall's grown since spring. Wren's room's doubled. That stair's new. Where does it grow from, Drev? Who lays the courses?"

"The hearth does. The house hangs off them." He tipped his cup down the table, at the four of them. "Every vow sets another stone. I hold the door, and I pay the sky. That's the whole arithmetic."

"Then I'll mind where I put my boots." Corin raised the cup to the wives. "To the masonry."

The masonry drank.

"And which room's the young one?" he said after. "Houses tell you where they're young. They creak differently."

"North side," Vesper said. "Hers. Don't ask to see it."

"I never lean on young walls." He put both hands up, easy. "I only like to know where they are. Bad sleeper. Road habit."

He was quiet a moment then, and when he spoke again the cards were down.

"You like it plain, this table, so here's the roads plain. At Fenlow, the river runs uphill from midnight to one. Every night since the equinox. The millers have quit arguing about it." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the wood. "And at the Bellwood, they took the bell out of the tower in autumn, on account of the ringing. The tower went on ringing."

The crystals in the mitre stopped.

Drevik sat with his cup halfway raised and did not move for two breaths. Sera's hands went still over the bread. Then he set the cup down with care.

"A Choir sings," he said. "This doesn't. Something's taking the hinges off the world's doors and wants me to call it weather. I'll walk out and see which side of the seam the hand is on."

"Roads are mine," Corin said. "I'll take you out and bring you back."

"Two days. I'll leave a door at the Ninefold crossing."

The table loosened. Sera let her breath go. Wren's hum came back. It was a good night's work, an old friend with true news and a road offered, and the house was glad of the plan, and Mira believed every word of the news. She filed, on a separate line, what the news was for.

He laughed twice more before the table cleared, and both laughs were priced. The crystals drifted again after that, but they did not get all their quick back, and Sera watched them not getting it.

"They say on the roads," Corin said, shuffling, "that you married a lockswoman. You. The man who leaves no locks behind him."

"She kept me out three days," Drevik said.

"Eleven minutes," Mira said.

Vesper didn't look up from her cards. "Eleven minutes to open the workshop. Three days to be let in. He's precise about the difference."

In her first week, he had picked the lock on her workshop once. Left a round loaf and a new lamp-glass on the bench and locked it behind him. Every morning after, he had knocked, and on the third morning she had asked what he wanted in there, and he had answered with a reason. The Bureau had handed her writs for six years. That was the whole difference, and she had opened the door. She had cut him a key the next week. She had not told him so.

At the stair, Sera caught her sleeve.

"You'll like him better when you've known him longer. He's the best thing the roads ever kept."

"I liked him at once," Mira said.

Sera laughed and squeezed her sleeve and went up. Mira stood on the stair with the doubt held level, the way you carry a full cup. Liking was not the problem. The problem had warm hands, and the first wife had vouched for it, and there was no form for disagreeing with the gate.

Her nights were her own, and she spent the first hour of them on maintenance.

She came down with the lamp turned low and stopped in the kitchen doorway. Corin sat on the hearth step with his hand flat on the floor, thumb riding the seam of the fourth stone, back and forth, patient. The ember light kept itself. He had no lamp.

"He does keep it warm," Corin said, without lifting his head. "I've slept in kitchens on four roads. This is the first one that tends itself."

"It tends everything here," Mira said. "That's the arrangement."

"Sera says the stones are the wives. I couldn't sleep, so I came down to count them. Four now." His thumb crossed the seam and came back. "This is the young one. You can feel the join. Young stone runs cooler. It never pretends to be older than it is."

He said it warmly.

"It's curing," she said. "Don't lean on it."

"I never lean." He rose without putting weight on it, without using the stone at all. "Good night, lockswoman."

He went up light. She stood in the doorway until the landing took him.

Then she knelt and laid her hands on the stone. Span, span, two knuckles. Since last week it had grown the width of a fingernail. The house was still learning to hold her. She pressed her palm flat where his thumb had been, and the stone was warm all the way through, warm either way, whoever's hand was on it. She stayed long enough to be sure of the measurement, and a moment past sure, and called the moment accuracy.

In her room she opened the drawer that held the last Bureau form she had never filed, and wrote one line.

Subject: Corin Vey. Relation to household: oldest friend. Reason for entry:

She left the field blank. She had a thumb on a seam, a door at a milestone, a room the house had grown overnight, and no evidence worth the word. The Bureau had taught her to find faults. Everything has them. The trade was learning which faults bear load.

She set the latch, the first real lock inside the Fold, cut by her own hand in the first week for a door nobody had asked her to guard. She lay down behind it, and she was not afraid. A lock is not fear. It is a tolerance, cut for what you mean to keep.
