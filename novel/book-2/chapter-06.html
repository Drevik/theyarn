Chapter 6: Testing Limits

Chapter Six: Testing Limits

The first blow caught Drevik across the ribs before he even saw Warden Thane move.

Air exploded from his lungs as he crumpled to the training mat, the fluorescent lights above swimming in his vision. The underground training facility smelled of sweat and something metallic—maybe blood. Probably blood.

"Again." Thane's voice cut through the ringing in Drevik's ears. The senior Warden stood with his arms crossed, not even breathing hard. His shadow stretched across the mat like a living thing, tendrils of darkness still reaching for Drevik even after the strike had landed.

Drevik rolled to his feet, one hand pressed against his aching ribs. Three days of this. Three days of being beaten into the ground, of having his limits tested and exceeded, of pushing through pain that should have stopped him hours ago.

"Your shadow walking is sloppy," Thane continued, circling like a predator. "You think because you can slip between spaces, that makes you fast. But you leave traces. Residue. Any competent enemy can track your path."

"I'll work on it." Drevik's voice came out rougher than intended.

"You'll work on it now." Thane's shadow lunged.

This time, Drevik was ready. He dropped into his own darkness, feeling the familiar cold rush as the Shadowlands opened around him. For a heartbeat, he existed in both places—training mat and twilight realm—before reappearing behind Thane.

His strike was fast. Thane was faster.

The Warden caught Drevik's wrist and twisted, sending him sprawling again. But Thane's eyes held something new. Interest, maybe. Or calculation.

"Better," he said. "Your transition time is improving. But you're still thinking of the Shadowlands as separate. A door you open and close." He offered Drevik a hand up. "It's not separate. It's a part of you. A part of this world. The Veil isn't a barrier—it's a membrane."

Drevik took the hand, rising on unsteady legs. "That's not how it was taught in initiate training."

"Initiate training is designed to keep you alive long enough to learn the truth." Thane's expression hardened. "The Elders don't want acolytes thinking too deeply about what lives in the dark. It attracts attention."

The words sent a chill down Drevik's spine. He remembered the darkness he'd carried back from his last journey into the Shadowlands—the cold weight that still lingered in his bones, the whispers that came in his sleep.

"Speaking of attention," Thane continued, glancing toward the observation window above the training floor. "You have an audience today."

Drevik followed his gaze. The glass was tinted, but he could feel the weight of multiple stares. Watching. Waiting.

"Political vultures," Thane muttered. "Half of them want to see you succeed. The other half want documentation of your failure. The smart ones haven't decided which side to take yet."

"Wonderful." Drevik rolled his shoulders, trying to loosen the tension building there. "Should I be worried about any of them?"

"You should be worried about all of them." Thane's shadow contracted, pulling tight against his body. "But that's not your concern right now. Right now, you need to focus on staying conscious."

The training resumed, and with it came the relentless pressure that had defined Drevik's week. Thane didn't just test his combat abilities—he probed every weakness, every gap in Drevik's defenses. Shadow manipulation. Veil Sight under stress. Physical endurance when magical reserves ran dry.

By the second hour, Drevik's vision had started to blur at the edges.

By the third, he was moving on instinct alone.

By the fourth, he stopped feeling the hits.

"That's enough for now." Thane's voice seemed to come from far away. "Your control is slipping. I can see the darkness you're carrying trying to surface."

Drevik blinked, realizing he'd been standing in place for several seconds without remembering how he got there. His hands were trembling—not from exhaustion, but from something else. Something cold and hungry coiling in his chest.

"Come back tomorrow. Same time." Thane was already walking toward the exit. "And Markinsoul? Whatever you're holding onto from your last crossing... you need to find a way to release it. Or it will release you."

The words followed Drevik into the locker room, echoing in the tile chamber as he stripped off his sweat-soaked training gear. The showers hissed to life, scalding water washing away the surface grime but doing nothing for the deeper chill.

He pressed his forehead against the tile wall, letting the heat sink into his muscles. The darkness inside him shifted, restless. In the week since his last journey into the Shadowlands, he'd felt it growing—a weight behind his sternum that hadn't been there before. It felt like hunger. Like waiting.

"You look like someone tried to drown you and failed."

Drevik turned to find Lexi leaning against the locker room doorway, her arms crossed over her chest. She'd changed out of her own training gear into a simple black tank top and jeans, but the shadows under her eyes spoke to her own exhaustion.

"Thane doesn't believe in half measures." He grabbed his towel, wrapping it around his waist as he stepped out of the shower spray. "What are you doing here? I thought your training rotation was with the Seekers this week."

"It was." Her eyes tracked over his form—clinical, assessing, but with something warmer underneath. "I requested reassignment."

Drevik paused in the middle of reaching for his clothes. "You what?"

"I requested reassignment." Lexi pushed off from the doorframe, crossing to stand in front of him. The locker room was empty save for the two of them, the afternoon light filtering through the high windows casting her auburn hair in shades of copper. "Thane's training methods are brutal. I know what he put the last acolyte through, and she had twice your experience."

"I can handle it."

"That's not the point." Her voice sharpened. "You shouldn't have to handle it alone. The Order throws new recruits into the deep end and expects them to either swim or drown. It's stupid and wasteful and—"

"Lexi." Drevik caught her wrist, feeling the tension vibrating through her. "Why does this matter to you?"

The question hung between them. In the silence, Drevik became acutely aware of how close she was standing. Of the water still dripping from his shoulders. Of the way her breath had caught when he touched her.

"Because." Lexi's eyes met his, fierce and vulnerable at once. "You're mine to protect. Mine to train. Mine to—" She stopped, jaw tightening. "The Elder Council wants to see you fail. My mother wants to see you fail. I'm not going to give them the satisfaction."

"Your mother." Drevik released her wrist, but Lexi didn't step back. "I keep forgetting she's one of them."

"One of the most powerful, which means one of the most dangerous." Lexi's hand came up, fingers hovering near his bruised ribs without quite touching. "She's been asking questions about you. About your progress, your abilities, your... potential."

The cold in Drevik's chest spread. "What kind of questions?"

"The kind that end with people disappearing." Lexi's voice was flat, but her eyes betrayed fear. Not for herself—for him. "Drevik, I need you to be careful. The factions are circling. Some see you as an asset to be cultivated. Others see you as a threat to be eliminated. And a few—" She stopped, looking away.

"A few what?"

"Nothing. Just... be careful." Her hand finally touched his skin, feather-light over the bruise spreading across his ribs. "Does it hurt?"

"Yes." Drevik didn't lie. "But I've had worse."

"I know." Lexi's touch traced the edge of the bruise, clinical and intimate at once. "I read your mission report. The thing you faced in the Shadowlands—I've never seen anything like it. And the darkness you brought back..." Her eyes rose to meet his. "It's still there, isn't it? I can feel it from here."

Drevik nodded slowly. "Thane noticed it too. He said I need to release it before it releases me."

"Easier said than done." Lexi's hand flattened against his chest, directly over his heart. Her palm was warm against his damp skin, grounding him against the cold coiling inside. "The Shadowlands don't let go easily. Whatever you touched, whatever touched you—it wants to keep you."

"Then I'll have to convince it otherwise."

A smile ghosted across Lexi's lips. "That's either very brave or very stupid."

"Can it be both?"

"Usually, with you." Her hand pressed harder against his chest. "I want to help. Let me help. Whatever training Thane is putting you through, I can supplement. I know techniques he doesn't teach—shadow weaving, emotional anchoring, ways to channel excess darkness into something useful."

Drevik covered her hand with his own, feeling her heartbeat through her palm. Or maybe that was his own pulse, thundering against his ribs. "Why? You already have your own responsibilities, your own training. Why add mine to the pile?"

"Because—" Lexi started, then stopped. Her eyes searched his face, looking for something. "Because someone has to keep you alive long enough to become useful."

The words were light, but her tone wasn't. Drevik could read the truth underneath—the fear, the care, the stubborn refusal to let him face this alone.

"Okay." He nodded slowly. "Show me what you know."

The secondary training room was smaller than the main facility, tucked away in a corner of the underground complex that Drevik had never visited. The walls were lined with crystals that pulsed with soft light, and the air hummed with contained power.

"This is a meditation chamber," Lexi explained, moving to the center of the room. "The crystals help focus your energy. They're usually reserved for Seeker-level exercises, but my mother's influence has some benefits."

"Your mother arranged access for you?"

"My mother arranged the entire room." Lexi's smile was sharp. "She just doesn't know I'm using it for this."

Drevik followed her to the center, where two meditation cushions sat facing each other. The crystals' hum intensified as he approached, responding to the darkness still coiled in his chest.

"Sit." Lexi settled onto one cushion, crossing her legs beneath her. "Close your eyes. I want you to focus on the darkness inside you—not fighting it, just... acknowledging it."

Drevik obeyed, lowering himself onto the cushion. His muscles screamed from the day's training, but he forced himself to relax, to breathe through the pain.

"Good." Lexi's voice came closer. "Now I want you to imagine that darkness as a physical thing. A shape, a color, a texture. Give it form."

The exercise was harder than it sounded. The darkness inside him wasn't a single thing—it was layers upon layers, cold and hot at once, smooth and sharp. When he finally grasped its shape, the image that formed made his stomach clench.

"It's... a sphere," he said slowly. "Black glass. With something moving inside."

"Something?" Lexi's voice was careful. "Or someone?"

Drevik's breath caught. He hadn't told anyone about the whispers that followed him from the Shadowlands. The voices that called his name in the space between sleeping and waking. The sense of being watched by eyes he couldn't see.

"I don't know," he admitted. "It feels... aware. Like it's watching me."

"That's not uncommon after a deep crossing." Lexi's hand found his knee, warm and solid. "The Shadowlands leaves marks on everyone. Some become more sensitive to its presence. Others carry pieces back with them."

"And which am I?"

"Both, I think." Lexi's thumb traced circles on his knee, absently soothing. "Your abilities are unusual, Drevik. The Foresight alone would make you valuable. But your connection to the Shadowlands—it's deeper than it should be. Deeper than Thane wants to admit."

"Thane doesn't want to admit a lot of things."

"Thane is scared." The words were blunt. "He's seen what happens when someone's connection to the Shadowlands grows too strong. The hunger it creates. The things it makes them do."

Drevik's eyes opened. "What things?"

"Stories." Lexi's expression was unreadable. "From before my time. The kind the Order doesn't like to discuss."

The cold in Drevik's chest pulsed, and with it came a flash of image—the Shadowlands stretching before him, endless and dark. A figure standing at the edge of his vision, watching. Waiting.

"Your connection is growing." Lexi's hand tightened on his knee. "I can feel it from here. The darkness is responding to your attention, trying to pull you deeper."

"Is that bad?"

"Is that a serious question?" Lexi's laugh was short, humorless. "Yes, it's bad. The Shadowlands wants to consume. It doesn't negotiate, doesn't compromise. If you let it pull you too far—"

"I won't." Drevik covered her hand with his own. "I won't let it."

"You might not have a choice." Lexi's eyes met his, fierce and frightened. "That's what I'm trying to tell you. The darkness inside you isn't just residue from your last crossing. It's a seed. And it's growing."

The crystals around them pulsed faster, responding to Drevik's spiking heartbeat. The sphere in his chest throbbed with something that felt almost like anticipation.

"What do I do?"

"We anchor you." Lexi shifted closer, her knees touching his. "Heart bonds are rare, but emotional tethers are common. Most acolytes form them naturally with their mentors, their training partners. Someone who can pull them back when they start to drift."

"And you're offering?"

"I'm insisting." Lexi's free hand came up to cup his jaw. Her touch was warm, grounding, human against the cold spreading through his veins. "Close your eyes again. Focus on my voice. On my touch. Let me pull you back if the darkness tries to take you."

Drevik let his eyes fall closed. In the darkness behind his lids, the sphere pulsed and spun, its surface reflecting images he couldn't quite see. The whispers started almost immediately—soft, sibilant, speaking words just beyond hearing.

"I'm here." Lexi's voice cut through the sound. "Focus on me. On my hand. My breath."

She was close enough that he could feel it now—the warmth of her exhale against his cheek, the brush of her hair falling forward. The whispers grew louder, more insistent, trying to pull him toward the sphere and whatever waited inside.

"Drevik." Lexi's forehead pressed against his. "Come back to me."

The intimacy of the contact surprised him. His eyes opened to find her face inches from his, her gaze steady and unafraid. The cold in his chest retreated, just slightly, pushed back by the warmth of her presence.

"Good." Her lips curved. "That's what an anchor feels like. Remember it. When the darkness pulls, you reach for your anchor."

"How do you know so much about this?"

"Because I've needed anchors too." Lexi's thumb traced along his jaw. "My mother's bloodline carries a strong connection to the Shadowlands. It's why she rose so high in the Order—and why she's so afraid of losing control."

"And you? Are you afraid?"

"Every day." The admission was quiet. "But fear keeps us alive. It keeps us careful. The problems start when people stop being afraid."

Drevik's hand came up to cover hers against his cheek. In the dim light of the meditation chamber, with crystals humming around them and darkness coiling in his chest, Lexi looked both fierce and fragile.

"Thank you." The words felt inadequate for what she was offering. "For this. For everything."

"Thank me by staying alive." Lexi pulled back, but her hand lingered against his skin. "Now let's try again. And this time, I want you to try speaking to the darkness instead of just observing it."

"Speaking to it?"

"Ask it what it wants." Lexi's expression was serious. "Understanding your burden is the first step to controlling it."

Drevik closed his eyes once more. The sphere materialized in his mind's eye, spinning faster now, its surface cracked with lines of deep purple light. The whispers had become a chorus, voices overlapping in a language just beyond comprehension.

What do you want?

He pushed the question toward the sphere, unsure if he was doing it right. For a moment, nothing happened. Then the sphere stopped spinning, its surface going smooth and still.

A voice rose from inside—not the whispers, but something clearer. Something familiar.

You.

The word echoed through his mind, and Drevik's eyes snapped open.

"Your form is acceptable."

Mira's voice dripped with calculated boredom as she circled Drevik's prone body. He'd been on the training mat for the past hour, working through a series of shadow manipulation exercises while she critiqued every movement.

"Acceptable?" Drevik pushed himself upright, wiping sweat from his brow. "I hit every target."

"You hit every target with sloppy technique." Mira's smile was sharp, deliberately provocative. "Power without precision is wasted potential. Anyone can throw a punch. It takes skill to place it exactly where you want it."

"I'll keep that in mind."

"See that you do." Mira turned toward the observation window, where Lexi had been watching for the past thirty minutes. Her posture was rigid, her arms crossed, her expression carefully neutral. "Your shadow is getting restless, Thornwood. Is watching your pet struggle making you nervous?"

"He's not my pet." Lexi's voice could have frozen fire. "And I'm not nervous."

"Could have fooled me." Mira's smile widened. "You've been glaring at me like you want to tear my throat out. Possessive doesn't suit you, Lexi. It makes you look... desperate."

"Careful." The warning in Lexi's tone was unmistakable.

"Or what?" Mira took a step toward the window, toward Lexi. "You'll challenge me? In front of the observers?" Her gesture took in the tinted glass above them. "Think about what that would look like to the Council. An Elder's daughter fighting over an acolyte like a child with a toy."

Drevik rose to his feet, positioning himself between them. "That's enough. Both of you."

"Defending her honor?" Mira's eyes glittered. "How charming. Though I wonder if she'd do the same for you."

"Mira." Drevik kept his voice level. "What do you want?"

"Right now? To help you train." Mira circled him, predatory and graceful. "You have raw talent, Markinsoul. But raw talent isn't enough for what's coming. The Shadowlands mission will kill you if you're not prepared."

"That's what Thane is for."

"Thane is teaching you to survive." Mira stopped in front of him, close enough that he could smell her perfume—something dark and floral. "I can teach you to succeed. There's a difference."

"What kind of difference?"

"The kind that matters." Her hand came up, fingers trailing along his shoulder. The touch was deliberate, provocative, designed to provoke. "Thane wants to make you a good little soldier. I want to make you powerful. There's a reason your abilities are so strong, Drevik. You're not like other acolytes. You're not like anyone I've seen before."

"You say that like it's a compliment."

"It's an observation." Mira's fingers traced down his arm, feather-light. "Compliments are for people who need reassurance. You don't need reassurance—you need challenge. You need someone to push you past your limits."

"And that someone is you?"

"Unless you'd rather keep playing it safe." Her eyes flicked to Lexi, then back to him. "Some of us prefer safety. Others prefer power. The question is—which are you?"

Drevik caught her wrist before her touch could travel further. "I prefer results. Show me what you're offering, or step back."

Mira's smile turned genuine. "Good. That's the answer I was hoping for."

She pulled her wrist free, then moved to the center of the training mat. "Shadow manipulation isn't just about controlling darkness. It's about understanding the relationship between light and dark. Every shadow is defined by the light that creates it. To master one, you must understand both."

"Thane already covered the basics."

"Thane covered the mechanics." Mira raised her hand, and the shadows in the corner of the room began to stretch toward her. "I'm showing you the art. Watch."

The shadows pooled at her feet, then rose into the air. They twisted and reshaped, forming something that looked almost like a sculpture—a figure made of pure darkness, standing tall and reaching toward the ceiling.

"A shadow is alive," Mira said softly. "It responds to intent, to emotion, to desire. When you try to control it through force alone, you're fighting against its nature. But when you work with it..."

The shadow figure shifted, moving through a series of combat forms. It was beautiful—fluid and deadly and impossibly graceful.

"When you work with it," Mira continued, "it becomes an extension of yourself. Not a weapon. A partner."

The shadow figure dissolved, flowing back to its original position. Mira turned to face him, her expression serious for the first time.

"That's what I can teach you. Not just to use the Shadowlands, but to work with it. To understand it as a living thing rather than a resource to be exploited."

"And why would you teach me this? What do you get out of it?"

Mira's smile returned, but it was softer now. "I get to watch you become something extraordinary. For some of us, that's enough."

From the observation window, Lexi's voice cut through the moment. "Drevik, your training schedule is already full. You don't need her interference."

"Interference?" Mira raised an eyebrow. "Is that what we're calling expertise now?"

"We're calling it what it is—an attempt to insert yourself into his training for your own purposes."

"My purposes are transparent." Mira's voice hardened. "I want him to survive. You want him dependent."

"That's not—" Lexi started, then stopped herself. When she spoke again, her tone was controlled. "Fine. Show him what you know. But I'm staying to observe."

"Of course you are." Mira turned back to Drevik. "Shall we begin?"

The evening found Drevik in his dorm room, surrounded by training supplements and hand-written notes. Ivy's care package had arrived via courier—an unmarked envelope containing energy supplements, muscle relaxants, and a detailed breakdown of shadow manipulation theory that was clearer than anything he'd received in formal training.

For the long nights, her note had read. You're pushing too hard. Let yourself rest when you can.

The supplements were working. His muscles still ached from the day's training, but the sharp edge of exhaustion had dulled. He could think clearly for the first time in hours.

A knock at the door interrupted his study.

Lexi stood in the hallway, a container in her hands and an expression on her face that suggested she wasn't entirely happy about being there.

"I brought food." She held up the container. "Real food. Not the processed garbage they serve in the dining hall."

"You didn't have to do that."

"I know." She pushed past him into the room, setting the container on his desk. "But you've been training for twelve hours straight. Even your shadow needs fuel."

Drevik closed the door behind her, acutely aware of how small the room felt with her inside it. The bed was unmade from his early morning. Training clothes were scattered across the floor. His notes covered every available surface.

"You're living like a prisoner," Lexi observed, looking around. "When's the last time you saw sunlight?"

"I see plenty of sunlight."

"You see training rooms and meditation chambers." She pulled the container open, releasing the smell of home-cooked stew. "When's the last time you went above ground?"

Drevik hesitated. "Yesterday?"

"Yesterday was rain. You haven't been above ground in at least two days." Lexi's expression softened. "The darkness you're carrying—it feeds on isolation. The more you withdraw, the stronger it gets."

"I'm not withdrawing. I'm training."

"There's a difference between training and hiding." Lexi pulled out his desk chair, sinking into it. "I know because I've done both. After my first crossing into the Shadowlands, I spent three weeks barely leaving my room. I told myself I was recovering. Really, I was letting the darkness convince me that nowhere was safe."

Drevik sat on the edge of his bed, facing her. "How did you break out of it?"

"Someone knocked on my door and refused to leave until I ate real food and saw actual sunlight." Her smile was wry. "Consider this me knocking."

The stew was better than anything he'd had in weeks. Drevik ate in silence while Lexi thumbed through his notes, occasionally making corrections or additions in her neat handwriting.

"Your theory is sound," she said finally. "But your understanding of the Veil is incomplete. The barrier between worlds isn't static—it breathes, expands, contracts. Trying to force through during a contraction phase will shred your consciousness."

"That's not in any of the training materials."

"It's not in the acolyte materials." Lexi set down her pen. "The Order controls what information reaches each level. By the time you reach Seeker, you learn that half of what you were taught as an initiate was simplified to the point of being wrong."

"Seems counterproductive."

"Seems dangerous," she corrected. "Which is why they do it. Too much knowledge too fast can destroy an acolyte. Or drive them to try things they're not ready for."

Drevik set down his empty container. "Is that what happened to the last acolyte who trained under Thane? You mentioned her before. What was her name?"

Lexi's expression closed. "Elara. She was talented. Strong. Maybe stronger than me." Her voice dropped. "She pushed too hard, too fast. Tried to cross into the Shadowlands without proper preparation. The Veil didn't just reject her—it absorbed her."

"Absorbed?"

"She's not dead." Lexi's hand found his, her grip tight. "She's somewhere in between. The healers say her body is still alive, but her consciousness... it's scattered. Pieces of her, trapped in the barrier between worlds."

"Is there a way to bring her back?"

"No one knows." Lexi's eyes met his. "That's why I'm here. That's why I'm training you, watching you, pushing you. I'm not going to let that happen to you. I refuse."

The intensity in her voice made Drevik's chest ache. He understood, suddenly, why she'd been so fierce during his training. Why she'd insisted on being involved despite the political cost.

"Lexi." He squeezed her hand. "I'm not Elara."

"No. You're not." Her grip tightened. "You're more talented than she was. And that terrifies me. Because the more potential you show, the more the Order will push you. The more the Shadowlands will call to you."

"I have you to pull me back."

The words hung between them. Lexi's expression shifted—something vulnerable flickering across her features before she locked it down.

"Promise me." Her voice was rough. "Promise me that when it gets too much—when the darkness feels like it's winning—you'll tell me. You'll let me help."

"I promise."

Lexi released his hand, rising from the chair. But she didn't move toward the door. Instead, she stood in the middle of his cramped dorm room, surrounded by his notes and his exhaustion and his desperate attempt to become something more than he was.

"Good." Her voice was steadier now. "Because I'm not losing anyone else."

She left before he could respond. The door closed behind her with a soft click, and Drevik was alone with the darkness in his chest and the weight of promises made.

The meditation chamber was empty at midnight.

Drevik had returned to it after Lexi left, unable to sleep, unable to shake the feeling that something was waiting for him. The crystals hummed as he entered, their light pulsing in response to his presence.

He settled onto the cushion in the center of the room, closing his eyes. The darkness inside him rose immediately—hungry, eager, responding to his attention.

What are you?

He pushed the question toward the sphere, feeling it pulse and shift in response.

I am you. The voice was clearer this time, more defined. The part you hide from. The hunger you deny.

Why are you here?

Because you brought me. The sphere's surface cracked further, purple light bleeding through. I was sleeping. You woke me.

Drevik's consciousness pressed harder. What do you want?

The sphere shattered.

Darkness exploded outward, and Drevik felt himself falling—not physically, but mentally, his awareness tumbling through layers of shadow and light. The meditation chamber disappeared. The crystals vanished. Everything he knew was replaced by the endless twilight of the Shadowlands.

He stood on a plain of gray grass, beneath a sky that held no sun. The landscape stretched before him, familiar and wrong at the same time. He'd been here before, during his crossing. But this time, he hadn't chosen to come.

Drevik.

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere. He spun, trying to locate its source.

Drevik.

A figure emerged from the shadows ahead. Not one of the twisted creatures he'd faced before—something else. Something human-shaped, wrapped in darkness that moved like living cloth.

The figure stopped ten feet away. In the gray light, Drevik could make out features—female, pale, with dark hair that floated around her face as if suspended in water.

Her eyes opened. They were the same green-gold as his.

Drevik. Her voice was soft, achingly familiar. My son.

The world lurched. Drevik felt something grab him from behind—not physical, but magical, a force pulling him backward, away from her.

"Wait—" He reached for her, straining against the pull. "Who are you? How do you know—"

Find me. Her voice was fading as he was dragged away. Find the truth. It's the only way to—

The Shadowlands dissolved.

Drevik gasped awake in the meditation chamber, his body rigid, his heart pounding. The crystals had gone dark, their light extinguished. His hands were shaking so badly he could barely form a fist.

The woman's face lingered in his mind—her eyes, her voice, the way she'd said his name.

My son.

The door to the meditation chamber burst open. Thane stood in the doorway, his expression sharp.

"Your shadow signature just spiked across half the compound." The Warden's eyes swept the room, taking in the dark crystals and Drevik's trembling form. "What happened?"

Drevik opened his mouth to answer, but his voice wouldn't come. The darkness in his chest had settled into something new—not just hunger anymore, but purpose.

Find me.

"I saw someone," he managed finally. "In the Shadowlands. A woman."

Thane's expression hardened. "Who?"

"She said she was my mother."

The Warden went still. For a long moment, neither of them moved. Then Thane crossed to Drevik, crouching to meet his eyes.

"Describe her."

Drevik did—her face, her voice, the way the darkness had wrapped around her like clothing. As he spoke, Thane's expression grew grimmer.

"You crossed over without meaning to." It wasn't a question. "Your consciousness slipped through the Veil."

"I didn't try—"

"I know." Thane's hand closed around Drevik's wrist, grounding him. "Your abilities are manifesting faster than we anticipated. The darkness inside you is creating pathways, opening doors."

"Who was she?" Drevik gripped Thane's arm. "Who is my mother? The records say she's dead. The Order told me she died when I was an infant."

"The Order told you what they wanted you to believe." Thane's voice was rough. "Your mother was Veiled One. The highest rank. She disappeared into the Shadowlands twenty-one years ago, and no one has seen her since."

"Until now."

"Until now." Thane rose, pulling Drevik to his feet. "This changes things. The Council will need to know—you've made contact with something that shouldn't exist."

"A person. She's a person."

"Is she?" Thane's eyes met his. "The Shadowlands changes everything it touches. Whatever your mother was, whatever she is now—she's been in that realm for two decades. The thing wearing her face might not be her anymore."

Drevik's chest tightened. The darkness inside him pulsed with something that felt like recognition, like longing.

Find me.

"She knew me," he said. "She knew my name."

"Then she's been watching." Thane's voice was grim. "Which means the Order's walls aren't as secure as we thought." He pulled Drevik toward the door. "Come. We need to report this before the wrong people hear about it from someone else."

As they left the meditation chamber, Drevik felt the darkness settle deeper into his bones. It wasn't just hunger anymore. It was direction. Purpose.

Somewhere in the Shadowlands, his mother was waiting.

And the darkness inside him wanted to lead him straight to her.
